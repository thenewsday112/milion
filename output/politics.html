Perspective: How will Weiser govern?

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Past performance is a great predictor of future performance. Attorney General Phil Weiser — poised to become Colorado’s next governor — agrees. Weiser has stated his eight years in office give Coloradans “clear guidance and a clear vision” about how he would perform as governor.  

The attorney general has a significant podium from which to advocate for or against laws and policies that impact public safety, as well as to use the office’s massive resources to lead litigation at the state and federal level. Yet, Weiser had scant relevant legal and leadership experience when he was sworn in, resulting in numerous misjudgments and failures.  

Let’s take a look at his record as AG on some high-profile issues to see what it may tell us about how he will govern. 

Budget 

As Colorado’s attorney general, he has excelled at spending taxpayer money and growing government. Since 2018, the year he was elected, Colorado’s population has grown 5.5%. Inflation has increased by more than 28%. Our state budget has ballooned by more than 50%. Weiser put all those metrics to shame. In his time as AG, Weiser doubled his office budget to $165 million and grew his staff by 50% — most of whom still work from home. 

Prediction: Weiser will seek to increase the growth of our Colorado budget beyond its current gluttonous rate. Weiser publicly opposes TABOR and seeks the kind of progressive tax code favored by east coast states, where he grew up. Watch for his support of a Democratic supermajority in the legislature to refer TABOR-repeal — er, reform — measures to our ballot. 

Crime rate 

Colorado is undeniably less safe after eight years of Weiser’s tenure as AG. The year before Weiser took office, the FBI ranked Colorado the 20th-worst state for violent crime. Last year, the year Weiser announced his candidacy for governor, our state had fallen to ninth worst. For property crime victimization, Colorado is third worst. Overall, according to U.S. News & World Report, Colorado is now the second-most dangerous state in America. 

Prediction: Colorado will continue to rank among the worst states in America for public safety as Weiser finds it politically impossible to veto the truckloads of offender-friendly bills sent to him by our DSA/progressive-dominated legislature. 

Fentanyl 

This month, Weiser told the Gazette, “I’ve spent the last nine years making sure I have gotten to be present and engaged and working to address Colorado’s challenges across our whole state.” Not when it comes to the fentanyl crisis still plaguing Colorado.  

After a 2018 campaign pledge to sue anyone who ever marketed opioids of any kind, Weiser went into political witness protection during the 2019 legislative effort to de-felonize enough fentanyl to kill 2,000 Coloradans. Fearing backlash from the drug-legalization-loving left, Weiser said nothing and did nothing as his party passed a bill that would help lead to a nearly 600% increase in fentanyl overdose deaths in Denver, Weiser’s home county. It is no surprise he opposes Proposition 132, the citizen-initiated ballot measure to ensure fentanyl dealers are sent to prison. Instead, Weiser chooses the status quo he helped to create, the one that saw Denver’s overdoses surge 34% from 2024 to 2025 while the rest of America saw a 19% decrease. 

Prediction: Colorado will continue to be one of America’s leaders in overdose deaths, as Weiser gives in to the failed “harm reduction” movement. 

Auto theft 

When Weiser was actually present and engaged in criminal justice matters, the results were equally troubling. The year before Weiser became AG, Colorado ranked sixth in the nation in motor-vehicle theft. By the end of his first term, when Weiser revealed his support for allowing thieves to commit “a third or fourth car theft in, say, three months” before jailing them, Colorado had climbed to first-worst in the nation — for the third year in a row. 

Prediction: Despite Colorado’s rejection of Weiser’s advice, strengthening (a bit) its weak car theft laws and empowering local law enforcement to pursue car thieves, Colorado will fail to break out of the 10-worst states for car theft under Gov. Weiser. 

Guns 

Weiser has an extremely limited view of the Second Amendment — at least for law-abiding citizens. He has never opposed any legislation that would further restrict Coloradans’ right to bear arms. Weiser described the U.S. Supreme Court’s recent decision striking down a Hawaii law that created a default “no carry” restriction on private property as “disappointing.” On his campaign website, Weiser pledges that he will “sign responsible gun safety legislation that will save lives.” 

It is an odd statement given that Weiser has championed access to firearms by — wait for it — convicted drug dealers and car thieves, despite their high statistical correlation with violence and gun crimes.  

The 2025 Colorado Auto Theft Annual Report states there “were 398 weapons charges and 231 cases filed specifically related to weapons offenses associated with motor vehicle theft.” Nonetheless, Weiser championed a change in the law that no longer makes it illegal for them to possess firearms in Colorado. 

Prediction: Weiser will sign every “guns are bad” piece of legislation the Democrat supermajority in the legislature puts on his desk. 

Leadership 

Weiser is most comfortable as a follower of his peers. His lack of meaningful experience prior to assuming the role of AG has led to numerous high-profile misjudgments. 

In fulfillment of his 2018 campaign pledge to sue anyone who ever sold an opioid, Weiser quickly jumped onto a series of global settlements with opioid distributors and manufacturers, shielding them from additional future liability. His “group think” settlement of claims against Johnson & Johnson resulted in just under $73 million to Colorado.  

Not everyone gave up so quickly. Washington’s Democrat AG Bob Ferguson rejected the global offer and filed his own lawsuit, resulting in $149.5 million — nearly double — paid to Washington, a state only 25% more populous than Colorado. 

In 2021, perhaps relying on his scant prior courtroom experience, he signed Colorado up for yet another bandwagon lawsuit, this time against Meta for various juvenile data privacy violations and youth social media addiction. A neighboring state, New Mexico, took a different approach. Democrat Attorney General Raúl Torrez rejected Weiser’s “go along to get along” approach and filed a standalone lawsuit against Meta. The gutsy former district attorney and federal prosecutor’s leadership and pursuit of civil justice for New Mexico resulted in a remarkably disparate outcome from Colorado’s. 

This past August, upon settling Colorado’s claims for a mere $615 million and providing Meta with blanket protection from additional lawsuits, Weiser proudly announced, “The relief we are getting in this settlement is very meaningful and well beyond what any court … is likely to order.” That claim did not age well.  

Last week, after a New Mexico jury found Meta liable for Torrez’s claims, the social media giant was ordered to pay New Mexico — a state roughly one-third the size of Colorado — $25 billion, an amount 4,000% more than Weiser obtained for us by following the crowd. 

Prediction: Having successfully rushed the fraternity of Democratic former AGs-turned-governors, Weiser will bond with Govs. Shapiro of Pennsylvania, Beshear of Kentucky, Ferguson of Washington and Stein of North Carolina and follow their lead on national issues. With no Polis to contend with, Weiser will look for manufactured opportunities to appear as the leader of a party increasingly controlled by socialists. He will continue to find comfort in “listening and collaborating,” while being led by the political current wherever it takes him — and us. 

Partisanship 

Weiser has shown himself to be the most hyperpartisan AG in our state’s history. Despite being attorney general for the entire four-year period of President Joe Biden’s one term in office, Weiser failed to file even a single lawsuit against him. Conversely, in less than two years of President Trump’s second term, Weiser has trumpeted his involvement in 88 lawsuits against him — nearly one per week. 

Weiser’s preference for following his fellow Democratic AGs from across the country is also reflected in his approach to suing the president. Of those 88 lawsuits he has filed, Weiser has led or coled only three. The remainder have been bandwagon “coalition” lawsuits filed by other Democratic AGs. 

Prediction: Weiser will continue to make attacking President Trump a primary focus of the first two years of his administration, especially as a distraction from or blame for, Colorado’s failure to address affordability, opportunity and public safety. Afterward, as he heads into reelection, he will blame Trump for the problems he has failed to fix. 

Civil rights 

When Weiser has chosen to exercise our state’s massive legal authority without the safety net of a crowd to follow, the results were equally troubling. At the Supreme Court of the United States, Colorado has become a punchline for the number of times it has been overturned for violating Coloradans’ constitutional rights.  

Weiser has unsuccessfully defended Colorado’s attacks on individual liberties (especially religious faith) in successive cases. In the cases 303 Creative, Counterman, Trump v. Anderson (a 9-0 drubbing) and Chiles (8-1, ouch), Weiser took positions that individual rights should yield to government. Even the high court’s liberal justices refused to buy his arguments. Heads up: On Nov. 3, the day Weiser is predicted to win the governor’s race, his office will again be in front of the court in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, seeking to force Catholic preschools to bend to the will of the almighty state on matters of faith. No other state has suffered as many Supreme Court smackdowns on First Amendment grounds since Weiser was elected AG. 

Prediction: Weiser will lose the St. Mary Catholic Parish case — but as governor, he will support Colorado government’s ongoing assaults on religious freedom and the First Amendment. If the newly elected AG who replaces Weiser will not do as Weiser did, expect Gov. Weiser to spend taxpayer dollars hiring high-priced, silk-stocking law firms to file suits on his behalf. 

Judiciary 

Weiser will also get to appoint Supreme Court justices and a generation of trial judges who will serve an average of 20 years, as well as the commissions that nominate justices and judges. 

Polis made the process to become a justice or judge deliberately and lopsidedly partisan. He reduced Republican representation on the Supreme Court Nominating Commission to three out of 17 members. For trial judges, the numbers are worse. Of the 161 possible judicial nominating commission members across Colorado, only 19 are Republican. Of the 35 commission members in the entire Denver metro area, only four are Republican. 

Prediction: Weiser will appoint more Republicans than Polis to the nominating commissions and point to it as a “Look, I’m moderate and balanced” moment. But he will not appoint enough to make meaningful balance in the selection of judges. Weiser will use identity politics as his touchstone for judicial appointments. Look for more judges to be appointed based more on their demographics than their potential judicial excellence, past performance or values consistent with the community in which they will serve. 

Clemency 

Weiser would also have unfettered clemency powers to reduce well-earned sentences being served by convicted felons and even to grant pardons and restore gun rights to convicted felons. 

Prediction: Weiser will not grant clemency based on a phone call to Kim Kardashian — like Jared Polis — which is now passé. But he will listen to the extreme-left flank of his party. Look for clemency that can be spun for political points with passing reference to “justice.” Weiser can and should be more transparent with his hand-picked clemency review board appointments and meetings. 

It appears inevitable we will have a Gov. Weiser and, likely, a Democrat-supermajority legislature that is out of control. There will be a lot of “listening and being present and collaborating,” but little leadership and tough decision-making. I fear Colorado’s toughest days are still ahead. 

George Brauchler is the 23rd Judicial District attorney and former district attorney for the 18th Judicial District.
