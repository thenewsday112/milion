The customer who wasn’t there

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The short version

  • In one set of books I worked on, a placeholder customer carried between 92% and 96% of revenue every month.
  • Deleting the bad entries breaks bank reconciliations and the audit trail. Reverse and rebuild instead, one month at a time.
  • Five balanced entries per month did the job, and one of them has no effect on profit, so it is the easiest to lose.
  • Measure the leftover residual, report it separately, and fix the bank-feed habit that created it.

In the books of one American technology company, the largest customer in a recent year was not a customer at all. It was a placeholder, created to receive electronic payments that the accounting system could not match to anybody in particular. Month after month, between 92% and 96% of the company’s revenue was booked against it.

The company was not hiding anything. It bills a large number of small business customers every week, and its payment processor deposits the proceeds in batches without a per-customer breakdown that the bank feed can read. Faced with hundreds of unidentifiable receipts each week, someone did the practical thing: one bucket, one customer, revenue recognized, bank reconciled. The profit and loss account was roughly right. Almost everything underneath it was useless.

What does a placeholder customer cost?

It destroys the information that receivables exist to provide. The aging report can no longer say who owes what. Customer-level profitability is impossible. Bad debts cannot be identified, because uncollected invoices sit undisturbed on real customers while the cash that should have settled them sits on the placeholder.

It also creates a tax problem that is easy to miss. In the United States, sales tax is administered state by state, and how much revenue arose in one state as opposed to another depends on knowing which customer paid. A ledger in which nine-tenths of revenue belongs to nobody cannot answer that. When a second method was used for the sales-tax workings, a comparison across a few hundred customers turned up a material difference. Neither number could be proved, because the books could not support either. (Sales-tax rules vary by state, and this is not tax advice.)

How do you rewrite revenue without erasing it?

The obvious repair is to delete the placeholder entries and post them again correctly. Don’t. Deleting invoices and payments breaks every bank reconciliation that included them, erases the audit trail and leaves the next reviewer unable to tell what was there before. An accountant who rebuilds history by erasing it has replaced one unreliable record with another.

The approach I used is to rebuild by reversal, one month at a time. Every original transaction stays where it is and the correction sits beside it. In this company’s rebuild, each month took five journal entries, imported in a fixed order and each balancing to zero:

  1. A reversal of the placeholder revenue for the month.
  2. Revenue for customers in one state, named individually.
  3. Revenue for customers in all other states, named individually.
  4. Write-offs of amounts billed but never collected.
  5. A transfer of the month’s collections from the placeholder to the customers who actually paid.

Each step had a checkpoint. After the reversal, the revenue account for the month should read zero. After the two revenue entries it should equal the total billed according to the company’s own billing file. After the write-offs, the total collected. The fifth entry moves receivables between customers and has no effect on profit at all, which makes it the easiest to lose. In one month six such entries went missing without changing a single figure on the income statement. They turned up only because the rule became to count journal entries from the transaction list instead of inferring completeness from the profit and loss account.

When an entry turned out to be wrong, it was not edited. The direction of the debit and credit in one deposit conversion had been specified backwards, and a second entry corrected it, so the error and its correction both stay visible. Months later, anyone reading the ledger can see what was done, what went wrong and how it was put right, without asking anyone.

What do you do with a residual that will not close?

This was the most instructive part. After every month was rebuilt to the billing file, a residual remained on the placeholder: the gap between what the accounting system had billed through it and what the billing file said had been collected. It did not shrink as months went by. It reopened every month, because it came from two systems recording the same activity differently. No single error was waiting to be found.

By year-end that residual stood at roughly $220,000. The temptation in any rebuild is to make such a number disappear with a final adjusting entry. I prefer to measure it consistently, report it separately from genuine receivables and say plainly that it comes from the old method and is not money anybody owes. Investors, lenders and tax authorities can live with a disclosed, explained residual. What they cannot live with is a clean balance sheet that turns out to have been made clean.

How do you stop it from happening again?

Rebuilding the past is pointless if the same mechanism keeps running. In the setup I saw, the bank feed suggested posting each unidentified receipt straight to revenue. That booked income for cash against invoices that had already booked the same income, and it is how the placeholder started.

The fix going forward is unglamorous. Unidentified receipts go to receivables with no customer attached. Once a month, a single entry moves that balance onto each customer according to what they were invoiced, and the credit is then applied against their invoices.

The lesson travels well beyond this one company. Placeholder customers, suspense accounts and “miscellaneous” vendors exist in almost every small business, usually created for a good reason in a busy week. They are a loan taken out against the future accuracy of the books, and like most loans they accrue interest. Paying it back is possible, as long as nobody pretends the loan was never taken.

The predictable business with unpredictable books

📅 Oct 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The short version

  • Most MSPs run three businesses through one revenue line, and the blended gross margin describes none of them.
  • Agreements bill in advance and licenses bill in arrears, so a month’s margin is partly fiction until cost and revenue are matched by agreement.
  • A monthly match of cost to revenue by agreement, product and sales order catches more leakage than a variance report.
  • Sales tax on managed services depends on the state. Someone has to own that calendar.

The pitch of a managed service provider (MSP) is reassuringly dull. For a fixed monthly fee per user or per device, it will keep a firm’s laptops patched, its servers backed up and its email out of the hands of fraudsters. Clients like the predictability. Buyers like it too, and in the deals I have seen they pay for recurring revenue.

Open the books of a typical small or mid-sized provider, though, and predictability is in short supply. Revenue lands in the wrong month. Costs arrive a month late. Hardware resale swells the top line and flatters growth while doing little for profit. Ask the owner what the gross margin is on managed services, separate from everything else, and the honest answer is often a shrug.

The cause is the business model. It has three features that ordinary small-business accounting handles poorly.

Why does a blended gross margin mislead?

Most MSPs I have worked with are really three businesses sharing a bank account. There is the recurring service, which is high-margin and labor-intensive. There is resale of third-party licenses, such as Microsoft 365 or security software, which is low-margin and almost entirely cost of goods. And there is project and hardware work, which is lumpy and moves cash in large chunks. Lumped into a single revenue line, the three produce a gross margin that describes none of them.

An illustrative month for a provider with roughly $250,000 in monthly billings shows how much the blend can hide:

A blended 41% looks respectable. It also hides that a quarter of the revenue earns 15%, and that a month with a large hardware order will push the headline margin down even though the core business has not changed at all. Owners who manage to the blended figure end up chasing the wrong problems.

Why does the monthly close drift?

Timing. In the setups I have seen, agreements are billed in advance, on the first of the month, through a professional services automation (PSA) platform such as ConnectWise. Licensing suppliers, especially distributors, tend to bill in arrears for consumption already incurred. So January’s revenue sits beside December’s license costs, and any month in which seat counts change produces a gross-margin figure that is partly fiction.

The PSA adds to the difficulty. In the ConnectWise-to-QuickBooks setups I have worked with, a recurring agreement, its mid-month additions, a one-off sales order and the procurement of the hardware behind it are separate objects, each with its own dates and billing status. When they sync into QuickBooks, they arrive as invoices dated when they were generated, not when the service was delivered. An invoice for a prorated addition in late March can be dated April 2. A sales order invoiced in installments can carry a date that matches neither the order nor the shipment. Month-end close at an MSP is, to a surprising degree, the work of putting each invoice back in the period it belongs to and finding the cost that goes with it.

My fix is to match cost to revenue at the level where they are actually linked: the agreement, the product and the sales order, not the account. Where revenue for a product exists without its cost, or cost without revenue, there is either an unbilled service or an unrecorded bill. Run monthly, this one exercise has caught more leakage in my clients’ books than any number of variance analyses on the profit and loss account.

Which taxes catch MSPs out?

Managed IT services sit in a gray zone of American sales-tax law. In my experience some states tax software and digital services, some tax certain IT services, and many treat a bundle of taxable and non-taxable elements by rules that differ in detail from one statehouse to the next. Picture a provider in one state with clients in two others. It can face three different answers to the question of what is taxable in a monthly agreement that includes licenses, monitoring and help-desk time.

Getting this wrong is expensive in a particular way. Sales tax that was never collected from customers is still owed by the provider, and it is hard to recover from clients years later. Payroll taxes add their own traps. I have seen state unemployment registrations lapse, quarterly returns go unfiled during a change of payroll provider, and a small penalty notice turn into a lien. None of this is exotic. It is small administrative facts that nobody owns.

Sales-tax and payroll rules vary by state and change over time, and this article is not tax advice. Check your own situation with a tax professional who knows your states.

Where does the money hide?

Some sector-specific wrinkles. The account-manager commissions I have seen are often calculated on revenue rather than margin, which rewards selling low-margin licenses. Some providers serve schools and libraries funded through the federal E-rate program, where payment depends on paperwork filed with a third party. Receivables there can sit open for months for reasons that have nothing to do with the client’s willingness to pay.

The common thread is that the information needed to run an MSP well already exists, scattered across the PSA, the distributor portals, the payroll system and the accounting software. What is usually missing is the connective tissue: a chart of accounts that separates the three businesses, a close process that dates revenue by service period, a monthly match of cost to revenue by agreement, and someone responsible for the tax calendar.

Nobody has to buy new software for this. The provider has to keep its own books with the discipline it promises for its clients’ servers. Most MSPs would never tolerate a backup that had not been tested. A margin that has not been tested is the same sort of risk.

Nothing failed. Everything was wrong

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The short version

  • A green tick or a “sync completed” line says the connection is alive. It says nothing about whether the data that travelled was complete or right.
  • Silent faults come in five families: records that never arrive, arrive twice, arrive altered, land in the wrong account, or lose their state, such as a reconciled flag.
  • Bank rules fail quietly too: a rule that was edited, or wrong from the start, can misfile the same payee for months.
  • A reconciliation shows that the book agrees with the bank at one moment. It does not show that the book agrees with the source system.
  • Count and total on both sides of every integration, by day and by account, and treat edits by “System” as a finding.

Every finance team has a dashboard light that reassures it. In the accounting software it is usually a small green tick beside the bank feed, or a line in the integration log that says “sync completed”. The tick means the connection is alive. It says nothing about whether what travelled down the connection was complete, correct or even the right thing.

That distinction sounds pedantic until it costs money. The cases in this piece come from my own work and from other controllers I have worked with. Take one from earlier this year: a mid-sized American services company found that its bank feed had stopped delivering one category of deposit, the daily settlements from its card processor, from one Tuesday in May. The feed did not disconnect. It went on delivering payroll debits, card payments and transfers on schedule, and the green tick stayed green. Only the settlements vanished. By the time anyone looked, a dozen deposits worth well over $100,000 were missing from the books, and a second settlement channel turned out to have failed in the same way. When the provider later repaired a genuine disconnection, the error code it fixed had nothing to do with the gap.

Nobody had done anything wrong. That is the problem.

A taxonomy of quiet

Engineers have a name for this class of fault: silent data corruption. Data is altered, dropped or duplicated somewhere between source and destination without any component raising an alarm. In storage systems it is treated as a serious hazard and hunted with checksums. In accounting, where the stakes are arguably higher, it is mostly hunted by luck and by the month-end close.

The faults fall into a handful of recognizable families:

  • A record exists in the source system and never arrives. The missing settlements above are the textbook case.
  • A record arrives twice, often because a retry succeeded after a timeout that was logged as a failure.
  • A record arrives with a field changed: a date shifted to the posting day rather than the service day, a customer reassigned to a default, an amount rounded.
  • The record is right but lands in the wrong account, usually because an automated rule has been edited, or was wrong from the start.
  • State loss. Something that was true about a record stops being true. A reconciled transaction quietly loses its reconciled status.

The last family is the strangest, and the least discussed. In the same company, the audit trail on one ordinary deposit showed thirteen edits over two days, each attributed not to a person but to “System”, each toggling the transaction between cleared and not cleared. Every bank transaction after a certain date had lost its reconciliation flag. The software’s own reports described the affected entries as “deleted” with a current amount of zero, although they were plainly intact. Anyone reading that report cold would conclude that money had been removed from the books.

The rule that changed

Misclassification deserves particular attention, because modern bookkeeping runs on rules. A mid-market company may have several hundred bank rules, each telling the software that a payee or a description belongs to a particular account. Rules are efficient and invisible, which is a dangerous combination.

In the case above, one rule had posted a payroll provider’s monthly fee correctly to payroll expense for four months, then began posting it somewhere else. Nobody remembered changing it. A second rule pointed at the same wrong account. A third had been sending the employer’s pension match into a liability account, where it accumulated for three months to just over $12,000 before anyone noticed that a balance which should be zero was not. Individually each amount was small. Together they distorted payroll cost, inflated a liability and nudged three months of management accounts in the wrong direction.

I have seen the same pattern wherever an operational platform feeds the ledger. A professional-services automation (PSA) tool such as ConnectWise has synced invoices into QuickBooks perfectly well while carrying the invoice date rather than the service period, so that a month’s recurring revenue straddled two periods in the books. A dealership management system has pushed a day’s sales as a summary, and when one day failed to post, the summary for the next day looked entirely normal. The integration is not broken. It is simply doing something slightly different from what the accountant assumes.

Why the close does not catch it

The conventional defense is the bank reconciliation, and it is a good one. But it has blind spots. A reconciliation proves that the book agrees with the bank at a moment in time. It does not prove that the book agrees with the source system that generated the revenue, nor that the transactions are in the right accounts, nor that last month’s reconciliation is still intact this month. A dropped deposit shows up as a reconciliation difference only if someone reconciles; on accounts nobody has reconciled for a year, the error simply compounds.

There is also a human pattern. When a reconciliation is out by a few hundred dollars, the path of least resistance is an adjusting entry. Software makes this easy: several of the products I have worked with will offer to post the difference automatically. Each such entry converts a symptom of corruption into a permanent, unexplained line in the ledger. Do that for a year and the books balance perfectly, and explain nothing.

Checksums for the ledger

The remedy borrows from the engineers. Rather than trusting that a sync succeeded, one counts and totals independently on both sides of the integration and compares the results, by day, by account and by customer. If the processor says 474 payments settled on a given day and the ledger holds 474 lines summing to the same figure, the batch is probably intact. If it holds 473, or the right count with the wrong total, something has drifted, and the comparison says where to look.

This is the idea behind LedgerDrift, an open-source toolkit I have been developing for some two years and am now piloting with several organizations, from a vehicle dealership to a legal services firm. It does not replace the close. It runs alongside the integration, fingerprints what leaves the source system and what lands in the ledger, and reports the differences in terms an accountant can act on: missing, duplicated, altered, misfiled, or no longer reconciled.

The principle matters more than the tool. Any finance team can apply it with a spreadsheet and some discipline: never accept a sync log as evidence; reconcile counts as well as amounts; check that the rules engine still says what it said last quarter; and treat an audit trail full of edits by “System” as a finding rather than a curiosity.

The green tick will go on glowing. It was only ever telling you that the pipe was connected, never what came out of it.

Security Keys vs Passkeys: What I Use and Where

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The short version

  • I carry two YubiKey security keys, a main one and a spare, registered on the same accounts.
  • I use them on accounts that touch money, payments or other people’s work. Passkeys saved in Bitwarden cover the less important ones.
  • Some sites accept a key as the only login. If someone knows where you are registered and holds your key, that is a risk, so add a second step where you can.
  • For a team of five, I would start with one key per person.

Passwords came first in my setup. Keys came later, for the accounts where a break-in would hurt the most.

Which keys do I use, and how do they work?

I use YubiKeys. Someone who knows a lot about security recommended them more than any other brand. I looked at the options myself afterwards, and these were the ones that convinced me. Mine connect by USB-A and also have NFC. I have two, identical.

What I do at login depends on the platform. On one I use a lot, the page just says to sign in with a passkey. I click it, insert the key, and touch the metal sensor on it with my finger. That is the whole login. Mine are touch keys, not fingerprint ones. Some YubiKey models do read fingerprints, but mine don’t. Both keys have a PIN set, which adds a barrier if someone gets hold of one. Other platforms ask for a username and a password first, and then use the key as a second step. It works the same way.

On a phone, I hold the key against it and NFC does the rest. On a computer with no NFC, I plug the key into the USB-A port and touch the sensor.

Where do I use them?

On accounts that touch money. The ones where I get paid, where I keep savings, and where I look after other people’s work, like the platforms that distribute music. Nothing has given me trouble so far. Some platforms don’t support passkeys yet. More do every time I check.

What is the difference between a key and a passkey in Bitwarden?

I also keep passkeys in Bitwarden, and the difference for me is access. The physical key is there the second I need it, by NFC or USB. With a passkey in Bitwarden I need my device, my master password and my face. It is a little more trouble.

So I use the Bitwarden ones for accounts that are not so important. They are also what I always have with me when I don’t have my keys.

Is a passkey-only login risky?

Yes. Some sites let you sign in with the key alone. If someone knows which platforms you are registered on, holds your key, and the site accepts a passkey as the only step, that is a risk. A PIN on the key helps, so set one.

Where the site offers it, add two-step verification on top of the key.

What would a team of five do?

One key for each of the five, at the minimum. A spare for each person is better. The other option is a single spare kept where everyone knows to look for it, easy to reach and quick to get. Anyone who can reach it can use it, so choose the place with care.

I would start with the accounts that hold money or other people’s work. Those are the ones that cost the most if they fall.

Music Copyright Registration: Do It Before You Need It

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The short version

  • I registered an artist’s recordings with Colombia’s copyright office before any dispute. It was free, online, and took 10 to 15 business days.
  • The papers got two claimed songs back from the distributor. On the EP they changed nothing, because the problem came from inside it.
  • Next I would register the musical work too, and consider the US office.
  • If the problem comes from a distributor, go to distribution before lawyers.

When a distributor took down an EP I manage, the first thing I did was send them the copyright registrations for every recording. The registrations were already done. I had filed them the week the final masters came in, long before anything went wrong. That decision cost me nothing, and it is the one I would tell every small artist to copy.

What did I register, and when?

I registered the sound recordings, the finished tracks, with Colombia’s national copyright office. I filed as soon as we had the final masters. That was before any problem existed, and that timing matters. A registration made after a dispute starts looks like a reaction. One made before is a dated record that the work was yours.

I did it myself, in the artist’s name.

How long does it take, and what does it cost?

In Colombia it cost nothing. The whole process was online. I entered the information, and between 10 and 15 business days later I got the notice that it was ready. I downloaded the registrations and filed them where I keep the project papers. It was easier than I expected.

Registering the musical work, the composition behind a recording, is also free there. For that one you need the sheet music organized and ready. I did not register the musical work. I only registered the recordings. More on that below.

The US Copyright Office also takes registrations, and it charges for them. As of October 2026, its fee page lists $45 for one work by one author and $65 for the standard application. A fee increase has been reported for November, so check the page the day you file. I have not registered in the US.

What did the registrations do for us?

The first time was before the EP. Two songs were hit by copyright claims through the distributor. We sent the registrations, and the distributor returned the music.

The second time was the EP, and the same papers changed nothing with the distributor. That was a different kind of case. The EP had been pulled and a copy had resurfaced from inside the distributor’s own platform, so there was no outside claimant to answer to. The paperwork did its job later, in the claim I filed with Spotify. The whole story is in When Your Music Distributor Pulls Your Catalog.

What would I do differently?

Two things.

First, I would register the musical work too, not only the recordings. It is free, it takes some organizing, and it gives you one more document that says the music is yours.

Second, I would skip the lawyers at the start. We spent close to a month on them. They almost never answered, and when they did, the news was the same: nobody to sue, nothing to do. The fix was in distribution. If your problem comes from a distributor, go to distribution first.

Does a Colombian registration protect you abroad?

A Colombian registration has legal effect inside Colombia. It does not register the work anywhere else. Colombia belongs to the Berne Convention, so other member countries protect a Colombian author’s work the way they protect their own. You don’t have to register country by country for that protection to exist, because the right is born when the work is created. If someone infringes it abroad, though, you enforce it under that country’s laws. For music sold in the US, a US registration may matter if you ever need to enforce there, and it carries a fee. I’m not a lawyer, so ask one before you rely on any of this.

What do I tell someone who hasn’t registered?

Most people skip it because they are not famous and think nobody would bother with them. We were small too, and it happened to us.

Being small is not a shield.

This is my experience, not legal advice. Registration rules and fees vary by country and change over time.

Small Business Security Setup: What I Use Every Day

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The short version

  • I run most of my day on Proton’s tools and a few privacy-first apps, all on personal plans.
  • For a small team I would start with two things: a shared password vault with generated passwords, and aliases on a company-owned address.
  • Physical security keys come second, only for the accounts that matter most.
  • I have not tested these tools inside a company, so what I say about teams is a recommendation.

This is the security setup I run every day, and the starting point I would give a small business.

Most of my working day runs on a handful of tools, and I picked them for one reason: I want to know who can see my data. Some come from Proton. Some are small apps I found while looking for a way out of Google. I pay for Proton’s Duo plan, billed yearly, and for most of the other tools on personal plans. I have never tested any of them inside a company. So this article has two parts. First, what I use and why. Then the order I would give a business of five people, which is a recommendation and not a report.

How long did it take to leave Gmail and Hotmail?

About a month and a half, maybe two. I had been using the internet hard for years, so I had accounts everywhere. My rule was simple. Every time an email landed in my Gmail or Hotmail, I went to the site that sent it and changed my registered address to an alias. All the aliases forward to my Proton mailbox.

A couple of years later I still do it, but now about once every six months, because almost nothing arrives at the old addresses. Everything comes through the aliases.

The part that dragged was the sites that would not let me change my email. For those I had to write to support and ask them to change it by hand, or open a new account. That was the most tiresome step of the whole move. The rest has worked very well. The alias setup has its own article: Email Aliases: Why I Use a Different Address for Every Site.

What runs on my computer all day?

The VPN is on from the moment I turn the computer on until I shut it down. It starts by itself. I stopped noticing the change in speed a while ago. Now and then it hangs, which happens with any VPN, and I disconnect and reconnect. It lands on a faster server and that is the end of it.

Proton Mail is where everything arrives. Proton Calendar holds my whole day, and I like not having to wonder whether my schedule is feeding somebody’s AI model.

Proton Drive is the one I lean on most. It holds a copy of my entire computer, so I can reach my files from anywhere, even when I am away from home and need one specific file. I also send heavy files through it. It works well, and it is good to look at.

How I set it up is the useful part. I keep everything in one single folder. Inside it there are thousands of subfolders, but from the outside it is one folder, and that is the only thing I upload. If you want an easy backup, do the same: pick the one folder that holds everything vital, copy it to an external drive, and put it in your cloud. You only have to look after that one.

Proton Pass is my backup password manager. Bitwarden is the main one. Why I keep two is in Why I Keep My Passwords in Two Password Managers.

How do I use AI with sensitive files?

Proton has its own assistant, Lumo. Proton says it does not use your conversations to train the model, and it protects your data. That is the reason I use it for sensitive material, such as analyzing PDFs that carry private information. It keeps getting better, with something new added every few months.

Which Proton tools do I skip?

Proton Docs and Proton Sheets. They still feel like they need more development, so I don’t use them.

Proton Wallet is a Bitcoin wallet. I know how it works and I have looked at it, but I have never used it.

I have never used Proton Meet. As of October 2026, my Duo plan gives me the same limits as the free tier: up to 5 new meeting links a day, 50 people per meeting, calls of 60 minutes and one active meeting at a time. It looks good on paper. I have only read about it, so I won’t pretend to review it.

Why did I leave Google Photos?

I already had Google Photos. Then I started thinking about how much of my life was sitting there, and about a company that trains AI models having access to all of it. That bothered me. I downloaded Ente Photos and paid for the yearly plan.

It does what Google Photos did for me. Ente says its face recognition runs on the device. I can’t check that myself, so I’m repeating what its policies say. It is fast, and it syncs across all my devices. The AI extras are gone, and I don’t miss them.

For notes I use Notesnook. My drafts, my notes and my plans all live there, and I like how it works. The free plan is good enough that I would tell anyone to start with it. I tried Notion and never really understood it.

Where does a small business security setup start?

I have no company-plan numbers for any of these tools. I have not priced them, so I won’t give a monthly figure. I also know that sharing a vault on a personal plan has limits, and I haven’t checked what they are. What I can give you is the order I would follow.

First, the passwords. Put every password the team shares into a shared Bitwarden vault, open only to the people who need it. Nobody makes up a password by hand. The manager generates it, at least 20 characters if you can, and never fewer than 15. For myself I go to 24 or more.

Then add the secret ending I described in the password article, the short piece that never goes into the vault. With so few people you can agree on it together, and if one person forgets it, another can help. If someone leaves the team, change the ending. It is a lot of work. It is worth it.

When you need to pass a text or a file, use Bitwarden Send. It is an option inside the app, I use it often, and it beats WhatsApp.

Second, aliases. Sign up for services with an address that belongs to the company, not necessarily the official one. That keeps the company’s main address from being attached to every site and tool somebody tries out. If one starts sending spam or turns out to have leaked data, you switch that alias off. The how-to is in the alias article.

Those two, in that order. Everything else comes after.

Is any of this too much?

No. I don’t think any of it is excessive, for a company or for a person. It is the minimum anyone should have today.

Some people will call it too much work. A few might even call it paranoia. I enjoy it. It has been a fun process, and it is useful. People who could have laughed at it have been hacked since, and they ended up agreeing with me.

Keys are for accounts that need far more protection, like the ones that hold money or the company’s main systems. A key works together with the password. I would not put one on everything, only where the extra trouble pays off.

Why I Keep My Passwords in Two Password Managers

📅 Oct 12, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The short version

  • Every password is generated by a manager and is at least 24 characters, and I add a short ending that I never store in any app.
  • Bitwarden is my main manager and Proton Pass is my backup. I move the data across by hand every few days.
  • My two-step codes live in a separate app, not in the managers.
  • I once used one easy password everywhere. A leak at another company took my accounts down one by one.

For years I used the same email address and the same password everywhere. Short, easy to type, easy to remember. I figured nobody would bother with me.

What mistake started all this?

A big platform got hacked. It made the news around the world, and the stolen data included usernames and passwords. I never read past the headline. You think it won’t happen to you.

Then the services I had signed up for with that email and that password started falling, one by one. My guess is that someone took the stolen pairs and tried them on other sites. I never confirmed it. I can’t even tell you which platform it was, because it was years ago.

That is when I decided to use passwords so strong that I couldn’t know them myself.

Why do I use two password managers?

Bitwarden is my main one. I have used it for many years, and it was the first manager I ever tried. It works well in the apps and in the browser extensions. A few times I have even been a beta tester.

Proton Pass is my backup.

Why two? Things go wrong. I trust Bitwarden a lot, and I still keep a copy. A saying I like: don’t trust, verify.

Every few days I export my vault from Bitwarden and import it into Proton Pass. The transfer is simple. I don’t know whether the two can sync on their own, so I do it by hand. One warning: a standard export is a readable file. I delete it as soon as the import finishes.

Don’t trust, verify.

What rules do I follow?

Every password is at least 24 characters, symbols included. The manager generates it. I never make one up.

Then I do one more thing. The generated password is not the one I log in with. At the end of every password I add five or six characters that are not stored in any app or note. If someone got into Bitwarden or Proton Pass, the passwords they found would still be missing that ending. Some people call it a pepper. I also have a way back if I ever forget it, with people I trust. Without that, forgetting it would lock me out of everything.

It sounds like a chore. It is a few extra keystrokes. I add it at the end because after autofill the cursor is already there. At the start I would have to move it every time.

My two-step verification codes are in 2FAS, an open source app, and not in the password managers. I also use physical security keys, which deserve their own article.

What happens if I lose my phone?

I have this ready. I sign in to Bitwarden from another device, then close every session I can: Bitwarden, Proton, everything else. Even if someone got to the vaults, the passwords would not be enough to log in, because the ending is not in them.

How do I share a password with someone?

With Proton Pass, mostly because I like how it looks. It takes a couple of clicks. You send a link, choose when it expires and limit how many times it can be opened. Set a low number of views and it stops working once they are used.

I have never used a team plan, only personal ones. They have worked very well, but I have not tested a team plan, so I won’t recommend one. For a team, what I would try first is simple: stop sending passwords over WhatsApp or in a spreadsheet, and send a one-use link.

Email Aliases: Why I Use a Different Address for Every Site

The short version

  • I give every website its own email address, and all of them forward to one private inbox.
  • When one starts getting spam, I know who leaked or sold it, and I switch it off.
  • When one alias starts getting spam, I can tell which site exposed it, and I switch it off.
  • An alias hides your address. It does nothing for a weak password.

Nothing bad had happened to me when I started. I just signed up for a lot of websites. Work required it: new tools to test, platforms to try, downloads that wanted an email first. My inbox filled with dozens of messages a day that I never asked for.

How did I end up with 501 aliases?

I made a private mailbox with ProtonMail and decided nobody would ever get that address. Each website gets an alias instead, which forwards to the mailbox. I use addy.io for that.

As of October 2026 I have created 501 aliases: 378 active, 112 inactive, 11 deleted. They have forwarded 8,551 emails and blocked 9,478. I have replied from an alias 49 times and sent 41 emails.

I have more blocked emails than forwarded ones.

What happens to someone who doesn’t do this?

After my setup was running, two friends of mine had their email hacked. The first got his account back after a couple of hours. The attackers had not yet changed one of the recovery methods, so he got in through it.

The second lost the account for good. It was the address he had registered with banks, government services and other platforms. He could not even change the address on those accounts, and for some of them he had to go to the offices in person. As far as he could tell, the break-in came from a data leak.

Those two cases told me my choice had been the right one.

How does it work day to day?

For each service I use the service’s name at my own domain. I don’t create the alias beforehand. I type any name at my domain and it exists. The password for that site comes from Bitwarden, a different one every time. That is a topic for another article.

When spam starts, I switch the alias off. If I still need the account, I change the address on the site. I also keep a personal blacklist of platforms I know have leaked or sold data.

One case. I signed up on a site once. About two months later, spam started arriving at that alias. I looked up which site I had used it on and found that the site’s data had leaked. I switched the alias off and nothing else came in.

Shared domain or your own?

addy.io gives you a shared domain. I have three usernames on it, and I use them for things I need once and don’t want tied to me. It is the most anonymous option: no name, no domain of mine. The catch is that those aliases live with that provider. If I leave, I lose them.

That was my early mistake. I created many aliases on the shared domain. When I saw that other alias services exist, I saw the problem. I have not needed to switch in three or four years. With my own domain, moving is easy and every address stays mine. Some of mine carry my name, on sites where I would use my name anyway.

What does it cost, and how long does it take?

The account takes about two minutes. Connecting your own domain takes two more if you know DNS records, and longer if you don’t. I pay yearly for the Pro plan: $36 a year as of October 2026, and I have done it for three or four years now.

What can a business copy?

Most small companies register for everything with the same address. The password is usually weak too. People at companies I work with have created accounts for me on platforms I need, always with the same address and an easy password. That is a serious exposure. And if I leave and nobody changes the password, I can still get in.

What I would do is create one alias per tool and per person, named after the service and the person. When someone leaves, you search your alias list, find every account they touched and change them. I have not run this inside a company. It is what I would do.

The alias covers the address. The password is the other half, and I’ll cover it in the next article.

‘The role of a broker will be different in the next decade than what it is today.’ Do you agree/disagree with this statement?

Brokers who can bridge the gap between risk management and insurance advice will play an increasingly important role in helping businesses remain strong, adaptable and prepared.
