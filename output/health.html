Colorado has become a hotbed of democracy innovation | Vince Bzdek

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We’re just two days from the midterms and a very consequential vote for both Colorado and the country.

But don’t expect the midterms to fix everything that is roiling Colorado politics and democracy in general.

No matter how the vote turns out Tuesday, there are deep structural issues that still need to be addressed, said Landon Mascarenaz, founding CEO of Courageous Colorado, a cross-partisan democracy reform organization.

“The midterms are important. People have to vote. They gotta use their voice,” he said. “But we have to have a real conversation about the fact that, in too many places, the primary election result is the general election result. Ninety percent of races in Colorado are over in the primary.”

Unaffiliated voters, who make up a majority of early voters so far in the midterms, think the current system isn’t giving them what they want, so they are exiting the two-party system, Mascarenaz added.

“Eighty percent of Coloradans want more choices in politics,” he said. Ninety percent of them believe our democracy is in crisis nationally and think that politics are a major part of the problem. “Coloradans are crying out for change.”

A civic engagement fair on the Lory Student Center Plaza at Colorado State last week. (Courtesy of Colorado State University)

Mascarenaz was one of the presenters last week at Colorado State University’s fifth annual Democracy Summit, which brings together students, faculty, community members and civic leaders to explore democracy’s challenges and its future, including the latest reforms and innovations aimed at strengthening civic life.

“Democracy is something we build together, and that takes conversation, curiosity and a willingness to engage with people who may see things differently,” said Kjerstin Thorson, dean of the College of Liberal Arts.

Turns out, CSU, Fort Collins and Colorado in general have become hotbeds for democracy innovation and reform.

“We are truly leading the nation in this work in lots of different ways,” said Martin Carcasson, founder and director of the Center for Public Deliberation in Fort Collins, which is celebrating its 20th year.

Take a look at all that is happening in Colorado surrounding democracy improvement:

The Democracy Summit is part of an ongoing Strengthening Democracy Initiative at CSU, which President Amy Parsons has tied to CSU’s original land-grant mission of democratizing higher education. The University of Colorado Boulder has a similarly named but separate Higher Education and Democracy Initiative, established in 2022.

CSU also hosts The Straayer Center for Public Service Leadership, which helps build community networks to influence policy and impact governance and the Center for Democracy, Art and Popular Culture.

Katie Knobloch, associate director of the CSU deliberation center, also helped create the Rural Action Project, which partnered with 15 rural communities recently to take action on needed public projects. She also helped form the Youth Civic Action Program, which pairs CSU students and high school students to address public problems in their communities.

The Office of Engagement and Extension at CSU also works closely on democracy projects with Colorado communities to address social, health, economic and safety challenges. And the Family Leadership Training Institute of Colorado at the CSU Extension helps train regular citizens to be leaders in community decision-making.

CSU also recently launched the Colorado Democracy Prize, which awards $5,000 annually to student groups that bring community members together across lines of difference.

Fort Collins is also the first big city in the state to agree to ranked choice voting and was the first city to host a citizens assembly that passed a ballot initiative. Such assemblies are a democratic innovation that encourages better citizen engagement in politics. They are fairly new in the States but have become all the rage in Europe.

The University of Denver also has held a democracy summit for the last five years, focused more on international challenges.

And DU hosts The Colorado Project as well, which serves as a state platform for deliberation focused on challenging issues affecting the state. DU just announced a national academy for free expression and pluralism as well, dedicated to renewing the idea that “democracy depends on the free exchange of ideas and the capacity to work across differences.” The academy builds on something DU established called the 4D experience, which “helps students develop the habits of mind and character that civic life requires.” They’ve also launched the Debate Across Curriculum and Conflict Engagement & Resolution Initiative.

In addition to Courageous Colorado, the CSU summit also featured another Denver-based nonprofit, The Civic Canopy, focused on strengthening democracy. The Civic Canopy facilitates community-driven processes across the state to coordinate action needed to address challenges like housing, homelessness, health and community resilience.

One of the most interesting projects highlighted at the CSU summit was something called Civic Dialogues, a partnership between Courageous Colorado and change.org.

Civic Dialogues is a nonpartisan platform that has gathered 11,000 responses from Coloradans in an effort to identify issues important to residents that cross party lines. The motivation: most voters say there is no easy way for representatives to really know what citizens care about.

The platform facilitates live, small-group conversations online that help participants find common ground on the state’s most complex policy challenges.

The group put together a list of the top 10 ideas and the top 10 goals that the 11,000 participants agreed on most strongly.

One hundred percent of participants agreed that term limits and enforcing anti-corruption laws were a priority. One hundred percent also agreed that town hall meetings to inform residents were an absolute necessity. Ninety-seven percent want corruption investigated regardless of party affiliation, and 95 percent want to ban out-of-state money in elections.

On top goals, 95 percent agreed that government spending should be audited much better, 95 percent want the influence of special interests reduced and 95 percent want increased legislative accessibility. This one caught my eye: 92 percent agree that civics education should be expanded.

That’s a lot of agreement from two sides we’re told are hopelessly polarized.

Attendees at the summit even got a small taste of participatory democracy themselves. A number of possible democratic reforms were displayed on posters at the conference, and we all walked around discussing pros and cons with student facilitators, then gave our thumbs-up or thumbs-down on each one. I tell you, it was the most “small d” democratic fun I’ve had in years.

Some of the reforms are things I’ve never really considered before, like allowing online signatures for candidates petitioning to be on the ballot or making Election Day a paid holiday.

The reforms we voted on included a top-two all-candidate open primary, in which all voters — Democratic, Republican and Independent — get to vote. Or ranked choice voting in presidential primaries, in which voters don’t just vote for their favorite candidate but rank their second, third and fourth choices as well. Or how about an age limit of 80 for statewide office? Or term limits of eight years for statewide office and local office?

Here’s something that would really get state legislators’ knickers in a twist: An annual statewide assembly of randomly selected citizens who study a major issue they pick each year, then recommend a course of action and the legislature has to vote on it. Another one that would curdle their coffee over at the Capitol: let state and local governments set aside part of their budgets each year for residents to decide how to spend directly. Or how about requiring a citizens panel for each ballot initiative, with the citizen summary is included in the Blue Book, the official state-issued voter guide?    

Students present democracy innovations for attendees at the CSU Democracy Summit to vote on. (Vince Bzdek, The Gazette)

Publicly financed campaigns were also floated, as was a stipulation that the top three donors behind super PACs giving to campaigns must be named in all political ads they pay for.

An idea that will get a test drive in Montana on Tuesday: stripping corporations formed under state law of their ability to spend money on campaigns. States apparently have the power to decide this, essentially nullifying the infamous Citizens United ruling — who knew!

And finally, a proposal dear to a journalist’s heart: require the state legislature’s website to allow anyone to look up every recorded committee and floor vote by each legislator.

All the proposals are aimed at getting citizens involved in civic life more deeply, beyond and outside elections alone. These crazy folk seem to think our government should be of, by and for the people. What a concept!

Patti Schmitt, community development director of the CSU extension office, said it this way: “Democracy begins with the experience of being heard.”

Many of the democracy reform and civic engagement groups in the state are working together on such projects right now.

Bill Fulton, founder of The Civic Canopy, said: “I feel like the appetite and atmosphere of collaboration has never been more dynamic.”

Courageous Colorado is trying to coordinate many of those partnerships, “working on ensuring that Colorado leads the nation to open up and build a stronger democracy. We have a massive opportunity in our society right now to confront the closed structures in our democracy because people are asking for it. They want it.”

In sum, Knobloch sees a lot of reasons for hope for a better politics in the state right now. “When you hand power back to citizens, they can do truly incredible things,” she said. “And when you are on the ground with folks trying to make a community a better place, there is no greater feeling.”

Vince Bzdek, executive editor of The Gazette, Denver Gazette and Colorado Politics, writes a weekly news column that appears on Sunday.

INTRODUCTION


Zanzibar (island), island in the United Republic of Tanzania, located in the Indian Ocean 30 km (20 mi) off the East African coast. Zanzibar’s sister island of Pemba lies an equal distance to the northeast. Zanzibar extends 90 km (56 mi) north to south and 43 km (27 mi) east to west and has an area of 1,660 sq km (640 sq mi). Covered with luxuriant tropical plant life, Zanzibar is a low-lying coral island with elevations no higher than 120 m (400 ft). Temperatures average between 24º and 27ºC (75º and 81ºF) throughout the year and are tempered by ocean breezes. Zanzibar's average annual rainfall, influenced by Indian Ocean monsoon wind patterns, is more than 1,520 mm (more than 60 in). The island’s once prevalent forests have largely been replaced by cultivated crops, including cloves, bananas, and coconuts. Animal life is limited to small mammals and abundant birds.

POPULATION AND ECONOMY

The population of Zanzibar in 1995 was an estimated 456,934. The port city of Zanzibar is the island’s principal urban center and is located on Zanzibar’s sheltered western shore. Smaller Zanzibari towns include Chwaka on the eastern shore, Kizimkazi in the south, and Mkokotoni in the northwest.
Zanzibar’s principal inhabitants are black Africans, descendants of the indigenous Hadimu, Tumbatu, and Pemba peoples. These were Bantu-speaking peoples who immigrated to Zanzibar from the nearby mainland, drawn by the island’s fertile soils and abundant water sources. The indigenous peoples later became known collectively as Shirazi to distinguish them from more recent black African arrivals. By the 10th century Arabs from Oman and other parts of southwest Asia had settled on the island. The Arabs formed Zanzibar's political, social, and economic elite until the island’s 1964 revolution forced out most of the Arab population. Sunni Islam is the religion of most of Zanzibar’s inhabitants. A small Indian community that includes believers in Shia Islam as well as Hindus and other religious groups is also present. Swahili is the primary language of island residents, with Arabic and English holding lesser influence.
Agriculture is the dominant economic activity of Zanzibar. Major products include food crops and cloves. Zanzibar once led in clove production, supplying more than 90 percent of the world’s supply. Tourism is also important to Zanzibar’s economy. The Stone Town section of Zanzibar city reflects the port’s past importance and is a prime tourist attraction. In 2000 it was named a World Heritage Site. The city of Zanzibar is linked to the Tanzanian capital of Dar es Salaam by regular ferry connections, and the city also has an international airport.

HISTORY

By the 15th century Zanzibar was a minor member of a series of Arab and African commercial centers that stretched along the East African coast. In the late 15th century Portuguese explorers and colonists arrived on Zanzibar, which soon came under Portuguese influence. The Portuguese established small settlements on the island and maintained a major base at Mombasa on the coast of Kenya from the late 1500s to the late 1600s. However, by the mid-17th century, influence in the region began shifting to other European powers and to the Arabs of Oman. The Omanis ousted the Portuguese from Zanzibar in 1729.
Zanzibar's rise to importance within the sphere of East Africa and the Indian Ocean began during the reign of Sayyid Sa‘īd ibn Sultan, sultan of Oman and Zanzibar from 1806 to 1856. After winning firm control of his Arabian domains, Said turned his attention to East Africa and began a series of innovative agricultural reforms—including the introduction of clove plantations—that established Zanzibar's new role in the region. Sa‘īd instituted a loose form of control over much of the nearby mainland through commercial domination and a strong military presence on Zanzibar. By the time of Said’s death in 1856, Zanzibar was a leading producer of cloves; a major commercial hub for the exchange of African, Indian, European, and American merchandise; and a primary disseminating point for new influences—including the religion of Islam—into east central Africa.
Zanzibar’s increasing importance drew the attention of the United Kingdom, the dominant colonial power around the Indian Ocean in the 19th century. The British had worked closely with Said’s administration in Oman and continued this role in Zanzibar through a series of consuls, present on the island beginning in 1841. The British administrators helped stabilize the authority of Zanzibar’s Arab rulers; however, they opposed the Arabs on the important issue of slavery. Under Said’s leadership, Zanzibar had developed a vigorous slave trade. African slaves, who composed between 60 and 90 percent of the island’s population, worked on Zanzibar’s clove plantations and performed many other functions in the local economy. The harsh working conditions on the plantations had serious health consequences that made necessary the continuous import of new slaves from East and Central Africa. Slaves were also brought to Zanzibar to be exported, mostly to southwest Asia. The British government opposed slavery, and after the death of Said, his successors faced increasing British pressure to end the slave trade.
The unity of the Omani state ended in 1860 when the British oversaw the division of Zanzibar and Oman into separate sultanates. Said’s successors, Majid ibn Sa‘īd and Barghash ibn Sa‘īd, largely maintained control over Zanzibar for three decades, although the British forced Barghash to sign treaties abolishing the slave trade in the 1870s. In the mid-1880s Britain and Germany extended their imperial control over East Africa, and the sultanate of Zanzibar lost control of its mainland territories. In 1890 Zanzibar became a British protectorate.
Although the British administration encouraged trade in Zanzibar, the island gradually lost its commercial importance as the European colonies in mainland East Africa became more economically developed. Zanzibar evolved as a minor territory of the British Empire, a state with an economy that was overly dependent on the export of cloves. The British reorganized the governmental system established by Zanzibar’s Arab rulers, eventually creating a body called the Legislative Council with members appointed by the colonial authorities. The policies of the colonial administration strongly favored Zanzibar’s dominant Arab population over the black African population. The latter two groups held mainly subordinate positions within Zanzibari society; were largely without representation in the government; and were disadvantaged in areas such as economic assistance, labor, and education.
Once African colonies starting moving towards independence after World War II (1939-1945), the British began changing Zanzibar’s political system. A new constitution paved the way for the first free elections to the Legislative Council in 1961. However, the process of moving toward independence revealed the latent hostility present between Zanzibar’s Arab elites and the African population. Political parties became divided along ethnic lines, and Africans, suspecting electoral fraud, rioted following the elections. In 1963 elections the Arab-dominated Zanzibar National Party and its ally, the Zanzibar and Pemba Peoples Party, defeated the African-supported Afro-Shirazi Party; the two parties led the country to independence in December of that year. In January 1964, however, a revolutionary group, supported by most of the black population, seized power in a bloody rebellion. Zanzibar's last Arab sultan fled the country along with most of the Arab population. Under land-nationalization policies and other reforms, Zanzibar’s large clove and coconut plantations were broken up and distributed to smaller landholders, and rural patterns of life were greatly altered. In April 1964 Zanzibar joined with Tanganyika to form the new state of Tanzania. Within the union, Zanzibar retains its own administration for island affairs.
