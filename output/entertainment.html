FEATURED ENTERTAINER OF THE WEEK

Inde Navarrette

Actress

November 01, 2026

Inde Navarrette’s Quietly Compelling Rise, From Liberty High to Smallville

Inde Navarrette has built her career in emotionally unsettled places: a high school reckoning with violence, a family fractured by secrets and a romance shaped by grief and memory. She does not arrive on screen with the force of a celebrity demanding attention. Her more effective tactic is to make the person she is playing feel as though she had a life before the camera found her.

That quality made Navarrette a valuable late addition to 13 Reasons Why and, soon afterward, a central member of the younger ensemble on Superman & Lois. As Sarah Cushing, later Sarah Cortez, she had to exist beside capes, alternate worlds and city-threatening disasters without becoming incidental to them. Sarah’s depression, ambitions, mistakes and changing relationship with Jordan Kent supplied the series with consequences that could not be solved by superpowers.

Navarrette’s name was presented for this week’s feature alongside claims that she made a Halloween hosting debut on Saturday Night Live, starred in a project called Obsession and is preparing to play Rogue in the Marvel Cinematic Universe. Those claims should not be repeated as fact. They are not supported by established NBC, Saturday Night Live or Marvel casting records, and no reliable official announcement connects Navarrette to Rogue. Her documented career is more compact but also more revealing: a transition from short-form work to a provocative Netflix drama, a Sundance film opposite Sienna Miller and Diego Luna, and four seasons on an unusually family-centered superhero series.

The result is a performer worth watching for what she has actually accomplished. Navarrette entered two heavily scrutinized television worlds before she was old enough to have accumulated a long résumé, then found ways to make supporting characters register as people rather than plot devices. That is the real reason her career merits a closer look.

Early Life

Navarrette was born in Tucson to a family with Mexican and Australian roots: her father is Mexican, while her mother is Australian. That mixed heritage later gave particular resonance to her work on Superman & Lois, where the Cushing family’s Mexican American background and original Cortez surname became part of the story rather than an incidental character detail.

She attended Mar Vista High School in Imperial Beach, a coastal community in San Diego County. Unlike performers whose childhoods are documented through years of studio productions, Navarrette reached wider audiences near the end of her teens. Her early screen credits were modest, allowing her to learn the practical rhythms of production before joining two series with large, vocal audiences.

That timing matters. She was not introduced as the center of a franchise or promoted through a highly managed child-star narrative. Instead, she entered established ensembles and had to define characters through limited screen time. The skill became evident in 13 Reasons Why, where she arrived during the show’s final season carrying the history of a character who was no longer alive. It became even more important on Superman & Lois, where intimate family scenes regularly had to hold their own against the machinery of superhero television.

Career

Navarrette’s first widely seen turning point came in 2020, a year in which she appeared in both a globally discussed Netflix series and a Sundance-selected feature. The two projects asked for different kinds of restraint.

In the fourth and final season of 13 Reasons Why, she played Estela de la Cruz, the younger sister of Montgomery de la Cruz. Monty had committed brutal acts during the series and was subsequently killed off screen; he was also blamed for Bryce Walker’s murder as part of a cover-up devised by the principal characters. Estela therefore entered Liberty High carrying a name that meant something to nearly everyone around her.

The role could easily have functioned as a narrative instrument designed to expose the cover-up. Navarrette instead played Estela as someone trying to understand a brother whose cruelty she could neither erase nor fully reconcile with the person she had known. Estela’s connection with Tyler Down, one of Monty’s victims, made the material especially delicate. Their scenes placed grief, accountability and the limits of inherited guilt in the same room. Navarrette joined a series already surrounded by debate about its handling of trauma, but she brought a comparatively quiet perspective to its final reckoning.

The same year, she appeared as Ellie in Wander Darkly, writer-director Tara Miele’s nonlinear romantic drama starring Sienna Miller and Diego Luna. The film premiered at the Sundance Film Festival before receiving a wider release through Lionsgate. Its structure moves through memory, perception and uncertainty after a traumatic event, requiring the supporting cast to fit into a story that deliberately withholds a stable sense of time. For a young actor, it was valuable company: Miller and Luna are performers known for conveying complicated emotional histories without overexplaining them.

Navarrette’s defining television opportunity arrived with Superman & Lois. Her casting as Sarah Cushing was announced in 2020, before the series premiered on The CW in February 2021. The show began with a familiar comic-book proposition—Superman and Lois Lane returning to Smallville with their teenage sons—but distinguished itself by paying unusual attention to mortgages, marriages, mental health and the accumulated disappointments of adulthood.

Sarah was the daughter of Lana Lang and Kyle Cushing and the older sister of Sophie. She became a friend and romantic interest for Jordan Kent, the anxious son of Clark Kent and Lois Lane who was beginning to develop Kryptonian abilities. Yet Sarah was never simply the non-powered girlfriend waiting beside the superhero story. The pilot established that she had survived a suicide attempt and was receiving treatment for depression. Her frankness with Jordan created an immediate connection between two teenagers accustomed to feeling that something inside them was difficult to explain.

Navarrette’s performance helped keep that relationship from becoming an idealized high-school romance. Sarah could be compassionate and impatient, perceptive about Jordan’s pain and careless with his trust. She pursued music, questioned the future available to her in Smallville and reacted to the collapse of her parents’ marriage. Some of her decisions divided viewers, particularly when the series complicated her relationship with Jordan. Navarrette’s task was not to make every decision agreeable. It was to preserve Sarah’s right to be as inconsistent as the show’s male teenagers.

The Cushing household also gave Superman & Lois a second family through which to examine Smallville. Lana’s history with Clark connected the series to Superman mythology, but Kyle and Sarah represented residents whose lives were shaped by the town rather than by Krypton. As the series explored the family’s Mexican heritage, the Cortez name became significant. That development allowed Navarrette to play a young woman considering not only where she wanted to go, but what parts of her family history she wanted to carry with her.

Superman & Lois ran for four seasons, concluding in 2024 with a shortened final season. Its production demands shifted over time, and its last chapter operated with a reduced regular cast, but Sarah remained part of the emotional geography the series had constructed around Smallville. Navarrette had spent the transition from her late teens into her early twenties playing a character making a similar passage.

The series also placed her within an unusual corner of superhero entertainment. Superman & Lois arrived through The CW, home of the interconnected Arrowverse, but eventually established that its characters inhabited a separate continuity. That separation gave the writers room to build their own Kent and Lang families without remaining tied to every event in the network’s earlier programs. For Navarrette, it meant working in a franchise with extensive mythology while portraying a character whose most important battles were recognizably human.

Her career so far is not a long list of interchangeable credits. It is a small group of projects with demanding subject matter: suicide and recovery, sexual violence and its aftermath, family rupture, memory and adolescent identity. The next meaningful step will be determined less by the size of a franchise than by whether a role gives her the same opportunity to locate a private life inside a public story.

Gracie Abrams turned her Halloween visit to Saturday Night Live into a two-part tribute to Baz Luhrmann’s 1996 film Romeo + Juliet.

The two-time Grammy nominee used her pair of musical performances to portray both sides of the movie’s doomed romance, drawing inspiration from the looks worn by Claire Danes and Leonardo DiCaprio in the stylized adaptation of William Shakespeare’s tragedy.

Two Songs, Two Costumes

Abrams began with “Hit the Wall,” a track from her third studio album, Daughter from Hell. For that performance, she appeared in a version of the angel costume worn by Danes in the film.

The staging extended the cinematic reference beyond Abrams’ wardrobe. She started the song from behind an aquarium, adding another visual connection to the movie while performing for the live audience at Studio 8H.

When Abrams returned later in the episode, she switched characters as well as songs. She performed “Here You Come, There You Go” from the deluxe album while dressed in a tropical button-down shirt modeled on DiCaprio’s look, complete with the metal sleeves associated with his suit-of-armor costume.

Together, the appearances gave viewers a pair of coordinated Halloween transformations rather than a single costume carried across both musical numbers. The first performance centered on Danes’ Juliet, while the second moved to DiCaprio’s Romeo.

A Return to Studio 8H

The episode marked Abrams’ second stint as an SNL musical guest. She previously made her debut on the program in 2024.

The night also brought a separate milestone for host Inde Navarrette, who made her hosting debut on the show.

ShowBiz Take

Abrams’ appearance worked as more than a Halloween wardrobe showcase because each costume was assigned its own performance. By dividing the Romeo + Juliet homage between “Hit the Wall” and “Here You Come, There You Go,” she created a visual through line across her two musical slots while still giving each song a distinct presentation.

The film references were also specific and immediately tied to its central characters: Danes’ angel costume for the opening number, followed by DiCaprio’s tropical shirt and armor-inspired sleeves later in the episode. For Abrams’ second visit as musical guest, the coordinated concept made both performances part of the same Halloween-themed statement.

Inde Navarrette used her Saturday Night Live hosting debut to skewer Shane Gillis’ controversial gym comments, tease her upcoming Marvel role and turn her breakout performance in Obsession into a darkly comic opening monologue.

The Halloween episode also delivered a surprise return for Tom Hanks’ David S. Pumpkins during the cold open, while Gracie Abrams joined Navarrette as the night’s musical guest.

Navarrette Turns Her Breakout Role Into an ‘SNL’ Joke

Navarrette opened by addressing the role that brought her acclaim this summer. In Curry Barker’s horror hit Obsession, she plays Nikki Freeman, a woman who is cursed after Bear, played by Michael Johnston, wishes that she would love him more than anyone else in the world.

“You probably know me from the movie Obsession,” Navarrette told the audience. “People ask me if I’m anything like my character Nikki, and I’m like, No. No, no, don’t do that.”

She then insisted she was nothing like Nikki, pointing to her boyfriend, Nick Aiello, in the audience. The camera revealed him duct-taped to his seat as Navarrette declared that they had “such a chill relationship.”

The monologue soon shifted to Navarrette’s next major role: Rogue in Marvel Studios’ forthcoming X-Men reboot. She said she had been working out because of the physical expectations attached to appearing in a Marvel movie.

That setup led directly to the night’s pointed Shane Gillis joke.

“So every morning, I put on my little whore outfit, and I hit the gym,” Navarrette said.

The line referenced Gillis’ Oct. 10 SNL monologue, in which he said women now “dress like whores” at the gym before describing brightly colored shorts and complaining that men were not supposed to look. His remarks generated controversy following the episode.

Halloween Costumes Bring an ‘X-Men’ Audition

Navarrette invited cast members onstage to display their Halloween looks, joking that they could not go out that night. Marcello Hernandez arrived as “a sexy TSA agent,” while Kam Patterson portrayed Elizabeth Holmes.

Jeremy Culhane used the costume segment to pitch himself for Jake Schreier’s upcoming X-Men movie. Dressed as Wolverine, he told Navarrette that he had heard the character had not yet been cast.

“Oh, I’m so sorry. I can’t comment on that,” she replied.

Culhane answered that he could comment — and that he would love to appear in the movie with her.

Grace Reiter then appeared as the bird involved in the viral Kelly Clarkson and Art of Play controversy. Veronika Slowikowska completed the group costume dressed as the American Idol winner, demanding, “Sell me the damn bird!” Reiter’s bird shot back, “I am not for sale!”

Tom Hanks Brings Back David S. Pumpkins

The episode’s cold open began with Reiter and Culhane taking a spooky ride before Hanks made an unexpected appearance as David S. Pumpkins.

James Austin Johnson’s Trump interrupted the Halloween sketch to address the upcoming midterms and compare himself with Pumpkins. Johnson’s character said both had appeared in 2016 and described them as “very loud, very orange” and “very frankly annoying.”

Saidah Belo-Osagie joined him as Nicki Minaj, introduced as Trump’s “friend and future press secretary.” Showing support for the president, she joked, “Trump’s out here killing it — and by it, I mean my career.”

Slowikowska also appeared as Katie Zacharia, Trump’s brief, former White House press secretary. Her character failed to complete the show’s familiar opening declaration, instead beginning, “With confidence. Live from New York. And from so many cities,” before being cut off.

From ‘Obsession’ to Marvel

Navarrette’s hosting debut follows the extraordinary performance of Obsession. The low-budget movie has grossed more than $522 million worldwide and became Focus Features’ biggest film of all time.

The TIFF title also became the top-grossing festival acquisition and recorded the biggest second-weekend box-office increase in modern times outside the Christmas holiday.

Beyond playing Rogue in the Marvel Studios reboot, Navarrette is set to star in Apple Original Films’ science-fiction thriller The Waffle House Index. She will also executive produce that project.

Her episode arrived during SNL Season 52, which has also featured hosts Jalen Brunson, Dakota Johnson and Gillis.

ShowBiz Take

Navarrette’s debut smartly connected every part of her current moment: the unsettling premise of Obsession, the physical preparation for X-Men and the controversy left behind by the show’s previous host. Rather than merely acknowledging Gillis’ remarks, she repurposed them into a compact punchline on the same stage where they were delivered.

The episode also balanced that sharp callback with Halloween nostalgia through Hanks’ surprise appearance, while the Wolverine bit allowed SNL to play directly with the attention surrounding Navarrette’s confirmed role as Rogue.

What Happens Next?

Druski will host the Nov. 7 episode with The Strokes as musical guest. He will be the first social media influencer to host SNL.

John Mulaney will then return to host on Nov. 14, joined by musical guest Phoebe Bridgers.

Before Iain Armitage became the young Sheldon Cooper, his first television acting role placed him at the center of a very different kind of story. The actor made his debut as a kidnapping victim in a 2017 episode of NBC’s “Law & Order: SVU.”

Armitage was 8 when the Season 18 episode, titled “Chasing Theo,” premiered on January 11, 2017. He played Theo, a 6-year-old boy whose disappearance prompted an investigation by Lieutenant Olivia Benson, played by Mariska Hargitay, and the rest of the SVU squad.

The case begins after Theo’s mother, played by Rachelle Lefevre, leaves him alone in their apartment for the night while she attends a party. Investigators initially consider whether the child’s nanny, played by Adriana DeGirolami, may have taken him. The inquiry also raises a more complicated question: whether the nanny might have believed she was acting in Theo’s best interests because of his home life.

Because Theo is missing for much of the episode, Armitage does not appear throughout the entire story. His performance is instead concentrated in scenes near the beginning and conclusion of “Chasing Theo,” giving the future sitcom lead a dramatic introduction to television.

A Major Year Followed His “SVU” Debut

The procedural appearance opened an especially active 2017 for Armitage. Later that year, he joined HBO’s “Big Little Lies” as the son of Shailene Woodley’s character.

He also began playing young Sheldon Cooper in “Young Sheldon,” the “Big Bang Theory” spin-off that would make him widely associated with the precocious central character. The contrast between that role and his “SVU” debut is striking: before leading a prequel as its young brainiac protagonist, Armitage entered television through a dark missing-child storyline.

His work during 2017 extended beyond those three television shows. Armitage also appeared in the movies “The Glass Castle,” “Our Souls at Night” and “I’m Not Here.” Taken together, the projects made “Chasing Theo” the starting point of a year that quickly expanded his list of screen roles.

Armitage Had Already Visited the “SVU” Set

Although “Chasing Theo” marked Armitage’s acting debut and his first “Law & Order” role, it was not his first experience on the “SVU” set.

An undated throwback shared by Armitage’s official Facebook page showed him visiting the production before his guest appearance. During that earlier visit, he pretended to be a witness on the stand and spent time with Hargitay and Raúl Esparza, who played Rafael Barba.

Armitage’s connection to Esparza extends beyond the procedural. Armitage’s father, Scottish stage actor Euan Morton, appeared with Esparza in the 2003 Broadway production of “Taboo.” Esparza is also one of Armitage’s godfathers, and Armitage called him “Pumpkin” when he was a child.

“Chasing Theo” remains the only time Armitage and Esparza have acted together on screen. Both later participated in the 2020 virtual concert “Take Me to the World: A Sondheim 90th Birthday Celebration,” which Esparza hosted.

ShowBiz Take

Armitage’s “SVU” appearance matters as more than an early guest credit because it offers a sharp counterpoint to the role that followed. In the space of one year, he went from playing a child at the heart of a grim disappearance case to portraying Shailene Woodley’s on-screen son in “Big Little Lies” and taking the lead in “Young Sheldon.”

The episode also brought his professional debut into a setting he had already visited through his personal connection to Esparza. That combination gives “Chasing Theo” a distinctive place in Armitage’s early work: it was his first acting role, his only on-screen performance alongside one of his godfathers and the opening chapter of a packed 2017 that included television and film appearances.

Taika Waititi is pushing back against the harsh critical reception for “Klara and the Sun,” pointing instead to the film’s significantly stronger audience response on Rotten Tomatoes.

The director shared the movie’s poster on Instagram alongside its 78% audience score. That figure stands in sharp contrast to the film’s 28% critics score, the lowest yet for the “Jojo Rabbit” director.

Waititi said he has spent nearly 15 years avoiding reviews of work involving himself and his peers, but argued that he still pays attention to moviegoers.

“I haven’t read a review of anything aimed at me or my peers in almost 15 years. But I do listen to the people,” he wrote.

His caption then turned directly toward critics. “So this Halloween, let’s spare a thought for those sad lost souls who couldn’t achieve their dreams of becoming artists, and thus became critics,” Waititi continued. “The same people who also hated Jojo Rabbit. Irrelevant. Out of touch. Obsolete. In the bin.”

A difficult theatrical debut

Waititi’s defense arrives as “Klara and the Sun” faces trouble beyond its reviews. The movie opened at No. 15 in theaters, earning $720,000 from 880 locations during its debut weekend.

Sony subsequently reduced the film’s theatrical footprint after reviews panned the release. Among the descriptions leveled at the movie were “calamitous” and a “strained, whimsical comedy that’s ruinous.”

The movie is based on Kazuo Ishiguro’s 2021 novel and unfolds in a dystopian future. Jenna Ortega stars as Klara, an obsolete companion android attempting to form a bond with her new owner, a sick young girl named Josie.

Ortega has also spoken recently about distancing herself from both reviews and box office reporting. In a Vogue interview, she said reading reviews of her films “would send me into a psychosis of some sort.”

The actor similarly avoids checking box office updates when her team sends them, explaining: “I don’t want it to become a numbers thing.”

ShowBiz Take

The most striking part of the “Klara and the Sun” response is the 50-point gap between its critics and audience scores. Waititi’s Instagram post makes clear which measure he considers more meaningful, even as the theatrical numbers present a separate challenge for the film.

His comments are not a measured disagreement with individual reviews; they are a broad rejection of professional criticism and its relevance. By invoking “Jojo Rabbit,” Waititi also framed the current backlash as part of a pattern in which critics and the audiences he values do not necessarily arrive at the same verdict.

Still, the 78% audience score and the movie’s opening-weekend performance represent two different forms of public response. Waititi has chosen to spotlight the favorable one, while Sony’s decision to scale back the theatrical footprint reflects the difficult commercial start.

For “Klara and the Sun,” that divide now sits at the center of the story: a director openly dismissing a 28% critical score, a star determined not to be consumed by reviews or revenue, and an audience rating offering the film’s most encouraging counterpoint.

Singaporean filmmaker Ang Geck Geck Priscilla has taken the top film prize at the Singapore International Film Festival’s Silver Screen Awards, with her debut feature “Ah Girl” named best film in the Asian Feature Film Competition on Saturday.

Set in 1990s Singapore, “Ah Girl” centers on a seven-year-old girl whose parents have separated. The win marks another milestone in the project’s long connection with the festival: Ang workshopped the film at SGIFF’s Southeast Asian Film Lab in 2015. It later received the Youth Jury Award at the International Film Festival Rotterdam.

Ang is an alumna of the Busan Asian Film Academy. Her earlier honors include the best short film prize at the Singapore Short Film Awards for her 2012 short, “Broken Crayon.”

Asian feature prizes

Afghan filmmaker Shahrbanoo Sadat earned best director for “No Good Men,” which opened the Berlinale. Set in Kabul shortly before the Taliban’s return to power, the drama follows a camerawoman facing sudden uncertainty over her work, family and freedom.

Sadat has lived in Germany since 2021. Her previous films include “Wolf and Sheep,” which premiered at Cannes in 2016, and 2019’s “The Orphanage.”

The jury divided the best performance award between two actors. Pushpa Thing Lama was recognized for “Elephants in the Fog,” the story of a member of Nepal’s third-gender Kinnar community searching for her missing daughter. Putri Marino shared the honor for “Four Seasons in Java,” a magical realist drama about a woman returning to her hometown after spending two decades in prison.

Japanese director Nagahisa Makoto received the Special Jury Prize for “Burn,” his second feature, which premiered at Sundance. Nagahisa previously won awards at the same festival for the 2017 short “And So We Put Goldfish in the Pool” and the 2019 feature “We Are Little Zombies.”

Short films share the spotlight

In the Southeast Asian Short Film Competition, “Golden Island” was named best Southeast Asian short. Directed by Yogyakarta-based artist-filmmaker Arief Budiman, the Rotterdam competitor follows one videographer from Java and another from Papua as they collaborate on a film about Papua.

Ratchapoom Boonbunchachoke won best director for “The Bovine Comedy.” The Thai filmmaker’s Locarno premiere takes place in 2036, when cows have nearly become extinct. His 2025 feature debut, “A Useful Ghost,” won the Critics’ Week Grand Prize at Cannes and received a special mention at SGIFF.

German-Indonesian filmmaker Berthold Wahjudi took the Special Jury Prize for “‘Vaterland’ or A Bule Named Yanto,” which premiered in Cannes’ Critics’ Week. Sarah Lingam won best performance for “Flame of the Forest,” about a young nurse who returns home and is blamed for her grandmother’s death.

Singaporean experimental animator Exyl’s “Singapore God” was selected as best Singapore short.

The Audience Choice Award went to Singapore Panorama Programme 2, “Second Life, Second Skin.” The five-film package comprises Shara Tay’s “Kiss Me Climax,” Ash Goh Hua’s “Subterranea,” Syahmi Iman’s “Reanimator,” Leon Cheo’s “Orang Minyak” and Ewan Leong’s “Encik.”

Pitching and honorary awards

SGIFF also presented prizes from its Pro Pitching Forum, held Oct. 30. “Agape,” from Yamaguchi Shin and Miyahara Takuya, received the SGIFF Pro Pitching Forum Award, while Lệ Diễm Hà’s “Dear Son An” won the SGIFF Pro Pitching Forum Award for the Asian Producers Network.

Tong Khoon Mun and Zhou Ke Xin’s “Beg, Borrow, Steal” collected the SGIFF Abroad Award supported by Sony. Sam Chua Weishi and Rein Maychaelson’s “Primal” received the SGIFF Abroad Award.

Earlier in the festival, Aamir Khan accepted the SGIFF Screen Icon Award from Peter Chan during an In Conversation With session on Oct. 22. Kore-eda Hirokazu received the SGIFF Cinema Honorary Award from Boo Junfeng on Oct. 24. Kore-eda released two features this year, “Sheep in the Box” and “Look Back.”

ShowBiz Take

“Ah Girl” brings the festival’s development and awards programs into the same success story. A project workshopped at the Southeast Asian Film Lab in 2015 has returned as a completed debut feature and captured the Silver Screen Awards’ leading film honor. The result also places a Singaporean story at the center of an awards slate spanning filmmakers and performances from across Asia.

Kerry James Marshall’s “The Histories” Finds a Fitting Home in Paris

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There has been a visible uptick in Black American artists being shown in Paris. Mickalene Thomas’s “All About Love” was at the Grand Palais (December 2025-April 2026), and Henry Taylor’s “Where Thoughts Provoke” was at Musée Picasso Paris (April-September 2026), plus a Faith Ringgold show in 2023. Palais de Tokyo presented Melvin Edwards’ last living show (October 2025-February 2026). In continuing with this pattern, which is hopefully not a mere trend, the Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris has the first retrospective in France of maestro Kerry James Marshall (b. 1955, Birmingham). His monumental figurative works centering Black life punch through the longstanding racist exclusion of Black communities within the art historical canon. “You’re never gonna see the range of treatment of Black subjects anywhere else, by anybody,” the artist said in a video interview near the end of the exhibition. He is correct.

Kerry subtly references art historical archetypes from which Black lives were excised, inserting their place after the fact within nuanced scenarios, where racist indignation is sublimated. He also creates counterfactual portraits for figures for whom there is no visual record, like the abolitionist David Walker, or a wedding portrait of Harriet Tubman, as a way to depict them “fully fleshed out” with a “full humanity” rather than heroic if flattened figures, as he explained in the interview.

The show, titled “The Histories,” comes to Paris from the Royal Academy of Arts in London after Kunsthaus Zürich. The exhibition showcases more than four decades of work, pulling from public and private collections (notably, the Pinault collection and Fondation Louis Vuitton in France). The 70 paintings in the exhibition include eight newly made works, though only one new relative to London and Zurich. The colonial pasts of the U.S., U.K. and France have their own respective realities, and these different histories are certainly interesting in terms of what visitors bring to the show.

“The Histories“
Artist: Kerry James Marshall
Venue: Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris
Address: 11 Av. du Président Wilson, 75116 Paris
Through: January 24, 2027

The exhibition is divided into 12 sections, spanning 1980 to the present. One of the differences relative to the Royal Academy show is the order in which those cycles appear. The changes are in relation to the architecture, reconfiguring the flow of the show: in Paris, everything is displayed against white walls and there’s one way through the space, while the 18th-century Royal Academy venue offered an enfilade of rooms, giving long sight lines. “The first priority was to make things look great,” curator Mark Godfrey told Observer. Because Kerry’s work has not been seen as much in Paris as in London, Godfrey felt it imperative to organize these series more or less chronologically, barring the last third of the show.

Marshall draws on 19th-century French painting, and it is powerful to see his take on contemporary culture and Afrofuturism in conversation with a historical patrimony. “In the wall text in London, we made reference to Manet and to Caillebotte and to Géricault and to David, and we’re doing that here as well. The nice thing here is that those masterpieces are in museums that are a 20-minute walk away,” Godfrey pointed out.

The show starts with a work that didn’t make it to Paris, A Portrait of the Artist as a Shadow of His Former Self (1980), although it is depicted within the frame of Portrait of the Artist & a Vacuum (1981), hung on the wall. While Marshall was doing a residency at the Studio Museum in the mid-’80s, he made the Ralph Ellison-themed painting Invisible Man (1986). Setting a Black figure against a black background, it’s part of an ongoing theme of Kerry addressing the idea of invisibility, a way of confronting stereotypes. Kerry was also thinking about the legacies of 20th-century abstraction, notably Malevich’s Black Square—in this way, Kerry’s works “are as conceptual as they are visual,” Godfrey noted. These paintings are joined by a much later canvas from 2008, Untitled (Painter), that continues formal ideas about a Black figure—this time an artist, smeared painting palette in the foreground—against a dark background.

The next sequence of four works is a series made in the early 90s about the legacies of the Middle Passage when enslaved Africans were forcibly taken to Brazil, the Caribbean and the United States. Enslaved Africans on a ship in shackles is not Kerry’s approach, but instead a complex and allusive crossover between that history and everyday life in the ’90s. In Plunge (1992), a figure is about to jump into a swimming pool “that doubles as the Atlantic Ocean, or a diving back into a very traumatic history,” Godfrey said. African religions are also coded here: the red crosses “might strike European viewers as things connected to the symbol for help, but in Yoruban symbology, the red cross is associated with Eshu, who’s one of the pantheon of Yoruban gods,” Godfrey explained. There are also veves—pencil diagrams drawn to summon gods—in Voyager (1992), which come from Haitian voodoo.

The next sequence engages with everyday life, as painters in France did. Kerry spent a lot of time with Georges Seurat’s A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, which influenced Past Times (1997). It’s from a series of paintings called The Garden Project—three of which hang here—set in project housing built in the ’40s and ’50s for communities of Black Americans who were migrating from the south. They were built in a spirit of optimism and hope, but by the time Kerry painted them, they’d become run-down and under-resourced. Rather than painting suffering and crime, Kerry paints people gardening, children playing, teenagers dating. There are Chicago skyscrapers on the horizon, references to the music of the Temptations or Snoop Dogg, and the Black middle class enjoying pursuits like water skiing and golf.

Godfrey noted that they’re as interesting in terms of fabrication as for their spirit: they were made on large unstretched canvases, painted on sheets of paper glued onto the surface, sometimes with collage elements. “Why I enjoy looking at them again and again is as much because of how they’re made as what they might be pointing to or about,” Godfrey said. This hybrid of history and leisure as related to the Black experience feels emblematic of Kerry’s work, but Godfrey notes that Jacob Lawrence’s paintings—although on a smaller scale—depict moments of important history, like the Haitian revolution, but also people playing pool, or children playing games on the streets of Harlem. “That combination of commitment to history and everyday, I think Kerry’s not the first Black artist to do that.”

De Style, a 1993 work featuring five guys at a barber shop, is one of the first paintings Kerry sold to a museum (LACMA). Meaningfully, Kerry visited that very museum growing up; he was both impressed by the large paintings there but also hyperaware that none featured Black subjects. The title namechecks Mondrian’s established movement, De Stijl—moreover, there are visual references to Mondrian with the planes of blue, red and yellow. “It’s both what you might expect to find in a barber shop and also not,” Godfrey reflected. “He’s very attentive to the organization of a picture” and, moreover, motivated by “different strategies of pictorial craft.”

Then there are four souvenir paintings that were made for an exhibition in 1997 at the Renaissance Society in Chicago. At the time they were made, Kerry was thinking about what the legacies of the 1960s meant in the 1990s. “Other people were doing that as well—Spike Lee made the film Malcolm X in this decade,” Godfrey noted. Middle-aged, middle-class Black women in domestic interiors (with Ebony magazines, lace doilies, floral sofas) appear as angelic figures under commemorative presences: the Kennedys, Martin Luther King, Medgar Evers, the four teenage girls killed in the church bombing in Birmingham, Alabama, in 1963, freedom riders, Malcolm X, members of the Black Panther Party like Fred Hampton and Mark Clark, who were killed in Chicago in 1969.

A work first shown in the Venice Biennale in 2003 is the polished floor-based sculpture Wake—it summons the journey made by enslaved Africans, but it also accumulates medallions depicting African Americans every time it’s made, growing and growing. As such, it draws on the legacy of the sacred power Nkisi Nkondi and the idea that, in West African sculpture, the more elements added, the more powerful it accrues. “Instead of having a vision of ongoing trauma, Kerry replaces that with this sculpture which is about increased cultural achievements,” Godfrey explained.

Changing scale, an interlude in the museum showcases original sketches from his comic Rhythm Mastr, which was self-published but officially reissued in 2025 with Rizzoli. Begun in 1999 and ongoing, it features fighters pushing back against the problems that affect Black American neighborhoods in a substitute for the South Side of Chicago; Kerry did the visuals and the text. (In London, an independent show dedicated to Rhythm Mastr happened alongside the main painting show.)

Further in, the ongoing painting of modern life is beautifully evoked in School of Beauty, School of Culture (2012), made 20 years after Kerry painted the barbershop scene: it acts in dialogue with it, as a kind of sister work. People are seen getting ready for a night out, posing, taking photos of each other. Kerry sometimes makes his own clothes in order to see how they fall on a figure; some of the fabrics therein are based on silhouettes he will have made.

Kerry was looking at Fragonard, Watteau and the tradition of rococo Romantic painting for the early 2000s series Vignettes. Love scenes are taking place, yet Kerry makes it clear there is no romantic space outside of politics for Black couples. The glittered painting that is maybe the most spectacular of the series, Vignette #14 (La La La), includes a Pan-African flag, a fire in the distance, the toppled torch of the Statue of Liberty. “The tune is ‘Ma Cherie Amour’ from Stevie Wonder, but the look on the guy’s face is one of concern rather than great love,” according to Godfrey. That unease is just as much a part of these works as the dominant gauzy pink.

The elaborate, heterogeneous 2014 painting Untitled (Studio), from the Met’s collection, acknowledges the tradition of studio painting in Paris, as in The Painter’s Studio (1855) by Gustave Courbet. In a close study on the Met’s website, it is noted that the table is crowded with cans of paint, brushes and knickknacks, including a jokey-looking skull and a small bust of President Abraham Lincoln (“assassinated just months before the 13th Amendment enshrined freedom for enslaved persons across the country”). There are two nude men and two clothed women, and an easel on which the scene is not being replicated. It is elaborate and contains multitudes, oddities mixed with classicism.

The show ends with a sequence of eight works newly made for this exhibition, which premiered at the Royal Academy. Here, the history of slavery includes how one African tribe would enslave people from another. In Cove (2025), slaves are being walked onto a beach to be loaded onto canoes and then shipped out. Nearby, a painting shows the return journey to shore where the canoe is now laden with the booty from the slave sales. Some things Kerry put into this painting are historically accurate, like cowry shells, kegs of rum and guns—but some are absurdly fictional, like gift-wrapped presents. “He’s often blurring or confusing the idea of what a history painting is and what a fantasy painting is,” Godfrey said.

The last painting in the show, from 2025, also blurs history and fantasy: Léopold Senghor, one of the key figures within the Négritude movement, wrote about the beauty of Blackness shortly before becoming president of Senegal and married Frenchwoman Colette Hubert. The wedding happened in 1957; Senegal became independent in 1960. Kerry’s depiction is “as if Senegal and France were almost sewn together”—quite literally through a union of flags, as if proposing a speculative history, mixing accurate research and total fabrication.

At the time of the show’s opening, Godfrey accompanied Marshall to the Louvre and the Musée d’Orsay, where he was “deeply happy” and an eager observer of the “compression of space” among older paintings by, say, Ingres. Although reimagination is intrinsic to Marshall’s work, Godfrey noted that he looks to artists of the past to consider “pictorial compositional challenges… in such a way that it will be engaging five hundred years after you’ve made it.”

Your Phone Says “Storage Full.” Here Is What Is Actually Taking the Space.

📅 Aug 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Your granddaughter is leaning over the cake, the candles are lit, everybody is
singing, and you raise your phone. Instead of the shutter sound you get a gray box:
Storage Almost Full. You tap OK, you try again, you get the same
box. By the time you give up, the candles are out and you have nothing.

So that night you do what everybody does. You open Photos and start deleting —
the 2019 beach trip, the screenshots of your daughter’s flight number, the twelve near-identical
shots of the dog. You get a little room back. Thursday the box returns. Now you are down photos you
cannot get back, and the phone is still full.

Here is what almost nobody uses: your phone already counted everything and sorted it biggest
first, on one screen. Read it and you stop guessing — because your problem pile may not be your
neighbor’s.

Step zero: stop guessing and read the bar

On iPhone: open Settings → General →
iPhone Storage, and give it about a minute to finish counting.

The colored bar breaks your phone into Apps, Photos, Messages and System Data
(the category once called “Other”). Under it, Apple lists every app sorted by size,
largest first — the honest answer for your phone. Above it you may see
Recommendations with a Show All link.

On Android: open Settings → Storage, then tap a
category to see inside it. On a Samsung Galaxy it lives under Settings →
Battery and device care → Storage; on the newer Galaxy phones that
same menu reads only Device care, with Battery broken out as its
own line above it. Google itself says settings
vary by phone: if neither path matches, type Storage into the search box at the top of
Settings. That works on any brand.

Now start where your phone tells you to start, not where an article tells you to.

Your biggest slice What is really in there Go to WhatsApp high on the app list Videos other people sent you, saved automatically Hiding place 1 System Data / “Other” (iPhone) App leftovers with no button to clear them The catch Messages (iPhone) Attachments inside texts you never delete Hiding place 3 An app you do not recognize Something you stopped opening, still holding files Hiding place 5 Photos really is the biggest Then it is photos — but delete them last Last section

Hiding place 1: the media WhatsApp saved for you

Every video your cousin forwards to the family group lands on your phone as a file. Not a link — a
file, sitting there. Ten minutes here beats an hour in Photos.

  1. In WhatsApp, open Settings → Storage and data (on some
    phones it reads Storage and mobile data) → Manage storage.
  2. At the top you get two shortcuts: Larger than 5 MB and
    Forwarded many times. Tap Larger than 5 MB.
  3. That list already arrives with the heaviest file on top — you do not have to sort anything.
    Tap Select in the top corner (on iPhone the same button reads
    Manage), tap Select All, tap the trash can and confirm. This
    removes the files and keeps the written messages.

On Android there is a faster door. Open Files by Google →
Clean → the card that says Delete WhatsApp media →
Select files → tick what you want gone → at the bottom tap the blue bar that reads
Move X files to Trash, and confirm. Then finish the job in the same app: the menu
(three lines, top left) → Trash → delete what is sitting in there. Google built a
card for one app’s leftovers. That tells you how common this is.

Hiding place 2: app cache (Android only — and this matters)

Cache is temporary junk an app keeps to load faster. On Android you dump it in five seconds per
app: Settings → Apps → the app →
Storage & cache → Clear cache. On Samsung:
Settings → Apps → the app → Storage →
Clear cache. Nothing you care about is lost.

Right beside it sits Clear storage. Google’s own wording: Clear cache
deletes temporary data, Clear storage permanently deletes all app data. Tap
Clear storage on WhatsApp and your chat history is gone — not hidden, gone.
Read the button before your thumb lands.

iPhone owners: skip this step. iOS has no clear-cache button. Not hidden — it
does not exist. Any tutorial that sends you looking for it is describing a different phone.

Hiding place 3: the attachments buried in your texts (iPhone)

Back in iPhone Storage, look under Recommendations for
Review Large Attachments. It lists every photo, video and PDF ever sent to you in
Messages, biggest first. Delete from the top.

Then close the tap so it does not refill: Settings → Apps →
Messages → Keep Messages. Three choices —
30 Days, 1 Year, Forever — and your phone
shipped on Forever. On an older iPhone there is no Apps list in
Settings; tap Messages straight down the Settings list instead,
and Keep Messages is in the same place inside it.

Hiding place 4: downloads you forgot you downloaded

The insurance PDF, the boarding pass, the coffee maker manual. Two minutes:

iPhone: open Files → On My iPhone →
Downloads. Tap the three dots (…) in the top right corner: the
sort choices — Name, Kind, Date,
Size, Tags — sit right there on some iPhones and one level in,
under View Options, on others. Either way you want Size. The
heaviest file jumps to the top; delete from the top. If you download episodes
in the TV app, Recommendations may also offer Review Downloaded
Videos — one movie can outweigh a thousand photos.

Android: Settings → Storage →
Free up space — on most phones that button opens the cleanup screen of
Files by Google. Scroll to the card named Downloaded files, tap
Select files, then Move X files to Trash — and empty that Trash
afterwards from the menu, or the files are still on the phone. To keep it tidy on its own, reopen
that screen, tap the menu
→ Settings → turn on Smart Storage.

iPhone: in iPhone Storage, tap any app and you get
Last Used plus two buttons. Offload App removes the program and
keeps your documents and data — the icon stays, with a small cloud on it.
Delete App removes everything. After a big iOS update, people have reported
Last Used resetting to the update date, so if a date looks wrong, go by size
instead: the heavy app you do not recognize. Automate it at
Settings → Apps → App Store →
Offload Unused Apps.

Android: open the Play Store → your profile photo →
Manage apps & device → Manage → the
Unused apps filter, which shows how long each one has sat untouched. To automate:
profile photo → Settings → General →
Automatically archive apps. It ships turned off, it only acts once the phone is

The catch: deleting the conversation does not delete the files

This is where most people lose an evening. You delete the loud group chat, you check the bar, and
almost nothing has changed.

On Android, the media WhatsApp downloaded does not live inside the conversation.
It sits in a separate folder — Internal storage → Android → media → com.whatsapp → WhatsApp →
Media. Deleting the chat removes the message; the files stay there, still taking the space.
They only go with the chat if you check the box that reads Also delete media received in

the number barely move.

Deleted → unlock with Face ID → Select → Delete All. If
you do not see Utilities at all, scroll to the very bottom, tap
Customize & Reorder and tick it. Anything you
“deleted” sits there for 30 days, still using your storage, unless you empty it yourself. If you

Photos → Optimize iPhone Storage.

Free up space on this device → confirm on the button that reads Free up X
from device. That deletes only the local copy of what is already backed up. Then tap
Collections at the bottom (older versions of the app call that tab
Library) → Trash → Empty trash: the trash holds
backed-up items 60 days and never-backed-up items 30. What looks like a bug and is not — Google says
pictures under 30 days old may be left on the phone on purpose, so if most of yours are recent you
get little back.

moved down. Then open the camera and photograph the wall. If the shutter fires, you are done.

The Story Behind "Wake Up Call"

Some songs entertain us. Others tell a story. Wake Up Call does something far more profound—it gives voice to unimaginable loss while offering a message of hope.

Written by acclaimed singer, songwriter and producer Jamie Sparks, the song was inspired by the tragic loss of his son, Josiah Sparks. Rather than allowing grief to become the final chapter, Jamie transformed heartbreak into music that encourages compassion, healing and a renewed appreciation for life.

"I wrote this song for a few reasons. Wake Up Call was written in response to the violence that claimed the life of my son Josiah Sparks."

Jamie explains that the tragedy forced him to confront realities he had once known but no longer witnessed every day. Growing up surrounded by violence, poverty and hardship, he eventually moved away for work and built a life where those circumstances were no longer part of his everyday experience. After Josiah's death, however, those same social issues became impossible to ignore.

The second reason for releasing the song was deeply personal. Jamie wanted to honour his son's memory while reminding listeners that violence never affects just one victim. Every life taken leaves behind grieving parents, siblings, relatives, friends and entire communities forever changed.

Perhaps the most touching inspiration came from Josiah himself. Before his passing, he often encouraged his father to return to making music. Jamie had not released new material in several years, and through this project he fulfilled a calling his son had reminded him not to abandon.

Today, Wake Up Call serves not only as a memorial to Josiah but also as an invitation for all of us to love more deeply, forgive more readily, reconnect with those we've drifted away from, and remember that tomorrow is never guaranteed.

More Than a Song

"Wake Up Call" is more than a single. It is a reminder that behind every headline is a family whose lives are forever changed.

Jamie Sparks created this music in memory of his son, but its message reaches far beyond one family. It encourages us to cherish the people we love, extend kindness whenever possible, and never take another day for granted.

Perhaps that is the true wake-up call.
