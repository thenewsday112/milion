One of The Big Bang Theory’s most memorable Christmas gifts came with an unexpected connection to Leonard Nimoy himself.

In the Season 2 episode “The Bath Item Gift Hypothesis,” Penny, played by Kaley Cuoco, gives Sheldon, played by Jim Parsons, a napkin signed by Nimoy. Penny explains that the actor had visited her restaurant and used the napkin to wipe his mouth.

That detail sends Sheldon into delighted disbelief. Because the napkin may contain Nimoy’s DNA, Sheldon realizes that he has theoretically moved closer to being able to clone the actor. The exchange delivers both comedy and an unusually touching payoff to Sheldon’s admiration for Nimoy and his Star Trek character, Spock.

But the napkin was more than a standard prop created for the episode. Nimoy had personally supplied it after the show’s writers contacted him.

How Leonard Nimoy contributed to the episode

Discussing the story with Collider, Nimoy confirmed that the production had asked him for the unusual item.

“Some time ago, they asked me if I would provide a napkin that I had used, and I did,” he said. “It has become one of the most highly talked about and most highly enjoyed episodes of all time, I understand.”

The contribution gave the scene another layer: Sheldon’s fictional gift was built around an actual object provided by the person he revered. For viewers, it looked like one more pop-culture gag in a series filled with them. Behind the scenes, however, Nimoy had directly helped make the joke possible.

The moment arrived early in the comedy’s 12-season run, during which The Big Bang Theory repeatedly drew on geek culture. Its many references included Star Wars, Marvel, DC and Lord of the Rings, but Star Trek — and Spock in particular — occupied an especially prominent place in Sheldon’s world.

Nimoy later found another way onto the show

The napkin was not Nimoy’s final contribution to The Big Bang Theory. In Season 5, he voiced a Spock action figure that speaks to Sheldon during a dream sequence, an idea that delighted series creator Chuck Lorre.

Nimoy never appeared physically on the series, although he said an in-person role had been considered. The voice performance instead provided a workable way for him to participate.

“For various reasons, a physical appearance didn’t work out, but when they came up with this idea of voicing the Spock character with Sheldon being given a ‘Star Trek’ transporter, I thought the whole idea was wonderful,” Nimoy told Collider. “It was a way for me to deliver an appearance on the show and to work with that very, very talented bunch of people.”

Other Star Trek cast members eventually appeared on the comedy in person. George Takei, who played Sulu, portrayed himself in Season 4. William Shatner, identified with Captain James T. Kirk, later appeared in Season 12 after the writers agreed to a condition he had set.

ShowBiz Take

The Nimoy napkin stands out because it was not merely another name-check or replica tucked into the background. The writers turned Nimoy’s real contribution into the centerpiece of a scene built around Sheldon’s intense affection for Spock and the actor who played him.

It also shows how The Big Bang Theory could make its pop-culture references part of the storytelling rather than simply use them as decoration. Penny’s gift drives the episode’s emotional and comic payoff, while the prop’s real origin gives the scene an extra off-screen story.

Nimoy’s later voice role completed that connection in a different form. Although a physical appearance never came together, the used napkin and the talking Spock figure allowed him to become part of the series twice — first through an unexpectedly authentic prop, and later through his own voice.

Taika Waititi is answering the critical response to Klara and the Sun by pointing to a sharp split between reviewers and moviegoers.

The director posted the film’s poster on Instagram on Saturday, drawing attention to its Rotten Tomatoes scores. The image showed a 78 percent audience score alongside a 28 percent critics score.

Waititi said he has avoided reading reviews directed at him or his peers for nearly 15 years, but indicated that he still pays attention to audiences.

“I haven’t read a review of anything aimed at me or my peers in almost 15 years. But I do listen to the people,” he wrote. “So this Halloween, let’s spare a thought for those sad lost souls who couldn’t achieve their dreams of becoming artists, and thus became critics. The same people who also hated Jojo Rabbit. Irrelevant. Out of touch. Obsolete. In the bin.”

The blunt post arrives as Klara and the Sun faces a challenging theatrical start. The film, which opened in theaters the previous week, earned an estimated $725,000 during its opening weekend and debuted outside the top 10.

It launched in 880 theaters across North America. At the time of publication, its worldwide gross stood at approximately $1.7 million.

Inside Klara and the Sun

Waititi’s film is adapted from Kazuo Ishiguro’s 2021 novel of the same name. Set in a dystopian future, the story centers on an obsolete companion android attempting to form a connection with Josie, a young owner who is dealing with an illness.

Jenna Ortega and Amy Adams star in the film. Artificial intelligence is central to its premise through the story’s use of AI companions, a subject Waititi also addressed at the movie’s New York premiere earlier this month.

Speaking with The Hollywood Reporter, the filmmaker acknowledged the continuing discussion over how artificial intelligence could be used within the entertainment industry. Although he described some level of adoption as probably unavoidable, he drew a distinction between AI functioning as a tool and serving as a substitute for people.

“There’s a big discussion about AI coming in: Will people use it? How much will they use it? I know it’s probably inevitable that we end up using it in our industry, but as long as it’s used by humans as a tool, as opposed to a replacement — I don’t want to replace humans. I want to work with them,” Waititi said.

ShowBiz Take

Waititi’s Instagram response turns the film’s divided Rotten Tomatoes reception into the center of the conversation. Rather than addressing individual reviews, he is using the audience score to challenge the relevance of the broader critical verdict.

The 50-point difference between the two scores gives that argument a clear numerical hook: audiences represented on the platform were substantially more positive than critics at the time captured in the poster. Waititi’s reference to Jojo Rabbit also makes clear that he sees the latest reviews as part of a wider disconnect between critics and reactions he considers more meaningful.

At the same time, the film’s early box-office figures provide another measure of its reception. With an estimated $725,000 opening weekend from 880 North American theaters and a debut outside the top 10, Klara and the Sun began its theatrical run with modest commercial results.

For now, the film is generating two distinct discussions: one about the divide between critics and audiences, and another about AI’s place in stories and in the industry itself. Waititi has made his position on both subjects unusually direct—he is dismissing the negative critical consensus while arguing that artificial intelligence should support human work rather than replace it.

For “Klara and the Sun,” that divide now sits at the center of the story: a director openly dismissing a 28% critical score, a star determined not to be consumed by reviews or revenue, and an audience rating offering the film’s most encouraging counterpoint.

Maya Jama may not have won “The Celebrity Traitors,” but she came within touching distance of the endgame after spending five weeks hiding in plain sight.

The “Love Island” host was the only original Traitor to reach the final episode. Along the way, she recruited comedian James Acaster, professor Hannah Fry and YouTuber King Kenny, while avoiding suspicion from the Faithfuls until the final roundtable.

That changed when “Good Omens” star Michael Sheen focused on Jama, leading to her banishment shortly before the endgame. Looking back on her remarkably long escape from scrutiny, Jama admitted she was surprised that nobody had identified her sooner.

“It was actually quite shocking for me too, I’m not gonna lie,” she told Variety the day after the finale. “After every day I was like, ‘How the fuck did I get away with that one? And why are you not looking at me?’”

Inside the heated final roundtable

Jama watched the finale with a group of guests months after filming took place in May. Although she remembered the broad events, she did not know which moments would appear in the finished episode and was particularly anxious to see how she handled the pressure of the last roundtable.

“I remember feeling my heart jumping out of my chest at every moment,” she said, adding that the confrontation felt even more heated during filming than it appeared on television. After watching it back, however, Jama said she was proud that she had maintained her composure.

Her parting message after being banished — “Good luck, you’re going to need it” — later contributed to James Blunt being removed from the endgame. Jama said that, with her game over, she felt the remaining players would have to fend for themselves.

Kenny’s vote against Jama also helped seal her exit, but she holds no grudge against her recruited ally. She said he appeared to have little choice once the room turned against her, because defending her would also have exposed him. Kenny was subsequently voted out.

The remaining Faithfuls, comedian Romesh Ranganathan and actor-singer Sebastian Croft, emerged victorious. Each donated £41,500 ($55,600) to a charity of their choice.

Jama has also decided to donate the amount she would have won to Action for Children, the charity she was supporting. The organization provides foster care and residential support for children and young people seeking asylum in the U.K.

“Obviously, I would have loved to get to the [endgame], but the whole point is money going to charity at the end of the day,” she said.

How Jama stayed undetected

Jama entered the game split over whether she wanted to become a Faithful or a Traitor, though she leaned toward the latter because it promised the fuller experience and a different personal challenge.

Her strategy was to behave naturally and build relationships. Before filming, she knew Kenny, Ranganathan, Rob Beckett and Leigh-Anne Pinnock, but said none knew her well enough to recognize when she was lying. As the cast grew closer, Jama believed that genuine bonding made it harder for the others to imagine she was deceiving them.

Her run also outlasted fellow original Traitor Richard E. Grant, who became the first person banished after mistakenly saying his role aloud. Jama said she would have enjoyed having Grant beside her for more mischief, but believed excitement and panic may have contributed to his mistake.

Acaster found the role much more difficult after being recruited and ultimately exposed himself at the penultimate roundtable. Jama said she had not known beforehand that he did not want to become a Traitor and acknowledged that balancing a charity game with the players’ real emotions was harder than it looked.

After Acaster’s departure, Jama felt her position had become desperate. She and Kenny chose to target Sheen because he was the player most suspicious of her and she did not believe she could change his mind.

Jama said the production became deeply immersive during three and a half weeks in a Scottish castle. Players received a five-minute daily phone call with producers present, but she said she skipped the calls to get more sleep after returning late from the turret each night. Even then, she estimated that she slept only three or four hours a night.

The roundtables were also longer and more intense than viewers saw. Jama said every contestant contributed, although only selected exchanges appeared onscreen. Sessions were intended to last an hour, but some ran for 90 minutes and one may have lasted two hours.

ShowBiz Take

Jama’s achievement was not simply reaching the finale; it was doing so as the sole surviving original Traitor without attracting meaningful suspicion until the final roundtable. Her account shows that the performance depended on more than quick thinking. Exhaustion, long confrontations and the emotional weight of close friendships all shaped a game in which deception became increasingly difficult to separate from real relationships.

She also left with those relationships intact. Kenny, Ranganathan, Croft and Fry were among the guests expected at her annual Halloween party, where Jama planned to dress as Olympic champion Mo Farah in a bald cap and an Olympic outfit he sent her. She said a cast group chat was created immediately after her departure, giving the contestants a private place to discuss the experience while bound by nondisclosure agreements.

For Jama, the lasting lesson was resilience. Despite minimal sleep and mounting stress, she said she was proud of how long she held her position — even if her five-week masterclass in staying undetected ended one roundtable too soon.

Singaporean filmmaker Ang Geck Geck Priscilla has taken the top film prize at the Singapore International Film Festival’s Silver Screen Awards, with her debut feature “Ah Girl” named best film in the Asian Feature Film Competition on Saturday.

Set in 1990s Singapore, “Ah Girl” centers on a seven-year-old girl whose parents have separated. The win marks another milestone in the project’s long connection with the festival: Ang workshopped the film at SGIFF’s Southeast Asian Film Lab in 2015. It later received the Youth Jury Award at the International Film Festival Rotterdam.

Ang is an alumna of the Busan Asian Film Academy. Her earlier honors include the best short film prize at the Singapore Short Film Awards for her 2012 short, “Broken Crayon.”

Asian feature prizes

Afghan filmmaker Shahrbanoo Sadat earned best director for “No Good Men,” which opened the Berlinale. Set in Kabul shortly before the Taliban’s return to power, the drama follows a camerawoman facing sudden uncertainty over her work, family and freedom.

Sadat has lived in Germany since 2021. Her previous films include “Wolf and Sheep,” which premiered at Cannes in 2016, and 2019’s “The Orphanage.”

The jury divided the best performance award between two actors. Pushpa Thing Lama was recognized for “Elephants in the Fog,” the story of a member of Nepal’s third-gender Kinnar community searching for her missing daughter. Putri Marino shared the honor for “Four Seasons in Java,” a magical realist drama about a woman returning to her hometown after spending two decades in prison.

Japanese director Nagahisa Makoto received the Special Jury Prize for “Burn,” his second feature, which premiered at Sundance. Nagahisa previously won awards at the same festival for the 2017 short “And So We Put Goldfish in the Pool” and the 2019 feature “We Are Little Zombies.”

Short films share the spotlight

In the Southeast Asian Short Film Competition, “Golden Island” was named best Southeast Asian short. Directed by Yogyakarta-based artist-filmmaker Arief Budiman, the Rotterdam competitor follows one videographer from Java and another from Papua as they collaborate on a film about Papua.

Ratchapoom Boonbunchachoke won best director for “The Bovine Comedy.” The Thai filmmaker’s Locarno premiere takes place in 2036, when cows have nearly become extinct. His 2025 feature debut, “A Useful Ghost,” won the Critics’ Week Grand Prize at Cannes and received a special mention at SGIFF.

German-Indonesian filmmaker Berthold Wahjudi took the Special Jury Prize for “‘Vaterland’ or A Bule Named Yanto,” which premiered in Cannes’ Critics’ Week. Sarah Lingam won best performance for “Flame of the Forest,” about a young nurse who returns home and is blamed for her grandmother’s death.

Singaporean experimental animator Exyl’s “Singapore God” was selected as best Singapore short.

The Audience Choice Award went to Singapore Panorama Programme 2, “Second Life, Second Skin.” The five-film package comprises Shara Tay’s “Kiss Me Climax,” Ash Goh Hua’s “Subterranea,” Syahmi Iman’s “Reanimator,” Leon Cheo’s “Orang Minyak” and Ewan Leong’s “Encik.”

Pitching and honorary awards

SGIFF also presented prizes from its Pro Pitching Forum, held Oct. 30. “Agape,” from Yamaguchi Shin and Miyahara Takuya, received the SGIFF Pro Pitching Forum Award, while Lệ Diễm Hà’s “Dear Son An” won the SGIFF Pro Pitching Forum Award for the Asian Producers Network.

Tong Khoon Mun and Zhou Ke Xin’s “Beg, Borrow, Steal” collected the SGIFF Abroad Award supported by Sony. Sam Chua Weishi and Rein Maychaelson’s “Primal” received the SGIFF Abroad Award.

Earlier in the festival, Aamir Khan accepted the SGIFF Screen Icon Award from Peter Chan during an In Conversation With session on Oct. 22. Kore-eda Hirokazu received the SGIFF Cinema Honorary Award from Boo Junfeng on Oct. 24. Kore-eda released two features this year, “Sheep in the Box” and “Look Back.”

ShowBiz Take

“Ah Girl” brings the festival’s development and awards programs into the same success story. A project workshopped at the Southeast Asian Film Lab in 2015 has returned as a completed debut feature and captured the Silver Screen Awards’ leading film honor. The result also places a Singaporean story at the center of an awards slate spanning filmmakers and performances from across Asia.

The ceremony was held at the Singtel Waterfront Theatre on the penultimate day of the 37th SGIFF. The festival runs through Nov. 1. Launched in 1991, the Silver Screen Awards was the first international film competition with a dedicated section for Asian cinema.

North Korea sanctions drew defiance from Pyongyang on October 31, 2026, when a foreign ministry spokesperson said sanctions and pressure “may work on the weak but never on us.” The statement landed two days after attackers drained about $6.3 billion from Binance, the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchange, in what analysts call the biggest crypto hack on record. Blockchain intelligence firms TRM Labs and Chainalysis blame North Korea’s TraderTraitor hacking group for the theft, though the Pyongyang statement never mentioned the Binance breach.

Pyongyang Rejects Sanctions After the Binance Hack

The Korean Central News Agency (KCNA) carried the statement, which answered a joint declaration from 11 countries and the European Union. That declaration reaffirmed monitoring of North Korea’s activities under United Nations Security Council resolutions and welcomed fresh US, Australian and EU sanctions on cyber operations and overseas workers. The spokesperson called the push a “strategic failure” and insisted that North Korea sanctions will not change the country’s status as a nuclear weapons state.

How the $6.3 Billion Binance Crypto Heist Unfolded

TRM Labs reports that the attacker moved about $6.36 billion out of Binance wallets across ten blockchains, most of it within roughly 20 minutes after 20:05 UTC on October 29. Binance puts the affected total near $6.3 billion, has set aside more than $6.34 billion to cover losses and says user funds remain safe. The FBI and US Secret Service now work with the exchange, and President Donald Trump confirmed a US inquiry into a possible North Korean link. The heist dwarfs the $1.5 billion Bybit hack in February 2025, which the FBI also pinned on North Korea.

What North Korea Sanctions Mean for Crypto Security

Experts say Pyongyang turned to crypto theft to raise foreign currency for its economy and nuclear program under tough UN sanctions. Chainalysis now links North Korea linked hackers to nearly 89% of reported crypto hack losses in 2026, which raises a hard question: do North Korea sanctions slow the regime, or does stolen crypto help it dodge them? Exchanges should respond by hardening wallet infrastructure, monitoring cross-chain withdrawals in real time and vetting remote IT staff, since investigators cite IT worker infiltration and executive impersonation as common tactics. Attribution still rests on blockchain analysis, so watch for official confirmation from US authorities.

Kerry James Marshall’s “The Histories” Finds a Fitting Home in Paris

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There has been a visible uptick in Black American artists being shown in Paris. Mickalene Thomas’s “All About Love” was at the Grand Palais (December 2025-April 2026), and Henry Taylor’s “Where Thoughts Provoke” was at Musée Picasso Paris (April-September 2026), plus a Faith Ringgold show in 2023. Palais de Tokyo presented Melvin Edwards’ last living show (October 2025-February 2026). In continuing with this pattern, which is hopefully not a mere trend, the Musée d’Art Moderne de Paris has the first retrospective in France of maestro Kerry James Marshall (b. 1955, Birmingham). His monumental figurative works centering Black life punch through the longstanding racist exclusion of Black communities within the art historical canon. “You’re never gonna see the range of treatment of Black subjects anywhere else, by anybody,” the artist said in a video interview near the end of the exhibition. He is correct.

Kerry subtly references art historical archetypes from which Black lives were excised, inserting their place after the fact within nuanced scenarios, where racist indignation is sublimated. He also creates counterfactual portraits for figures for whom there is no visual record, like the abolitionist David Walker, or a wedding portrait of Harriet Tubman, as a way to depict them “fully fleshed out” with a “full humanity” rather than heroic if flattened figures, as he explained in the interview.

The show, titled “The Histories,” comes to Paris from the Royal Academy of Arts in London after Kunsthaus Zürich. The exhibition showcases more than four decades of work, pulling from public and private collections (notably, the Pinault collection and Fondation Louis Vuitton in France). The 70 paintings in the exhibition include eight newly made works, though only one new relative to London and Zurich. The colonial pasts of the U.S., U.K. and France have their own respective realities, and these different histories are certainly interesting in terms of what visitors bring to the show.

The exhibition is divided into 12 sections, spanning 1980 to the present. One of the differences relative to the Royal Academy show is the order in which those cycles appear. The changes are in relation to the architecture, reconfiguring the flow of the show: in Paris, everything is displayed against white walls and there’s one way through the space, while the 18th-century Royal Academy venue offered an enfilade of rooms, giving long sight lines. “The first priority was to make things look great,” curator Mark Godfrey told Observer. Because Kerry’s work has not been seen as much in Paris as in London, Godfrey felt it imperative to organize these series more or less chronologically, barring the last third of the show.

Marshall draws on 19th-century French painting, and it is powerful to see his take on contemporary culture and Afrofuturism in conversation with a historical patrimony. “In the wall text in London, we made reference to Manet and to Caillebotte and to Géricault and to David, and we’re doing that here as well. The nice thing here is that those masterpieces are in museums that are a 20-minute walk away,” Godfrey pointed out.

The show starts with a work that didn’t make it to Paris, A Portrait of the Artist as a Shadow of His Former Self (1980), although it is depicted within the frame of Portrait of the Artist & a Vacuum (1981), hung on the wall. While Marshall was doing a residency at the Studio Museum in the mid-’80s, he made the Ralph Ellison-themed painting Invisible Man (1986). Setting a Black figure against a black background, it’s part of an ongoing theme of Kerry addressing the idea of invisibility, a way of confronting stereotypes. Kerry was also thinking about the legacies of 20th-century abstraction, notably Malevich’s Black Square—in this way, Kerry’s works “are as conceptual as they are visual,” Godfrey noted. These paintings are joined by a much later canvas from 2008, Untitled (Painter), that continues formal ideas about a Black figure—this time an artist, smeared painting palette in the foreground—against a dark background.

The next sequence of four works is a series made in the early 90s about the legacies of the Middle Passage when enslaved Africans were forcibly taken to Brazil, the Caribbean and the United States. Enslaved Africans on a ship in shackles is not Kerry’s approach, but instead a complex and allusive crossover between that history and everyday life in the ’90s. In Plunge (1992), a figure is about to jump into a swimming pool “that doubles as the Atlantic Ocean, or a diving back into a very traumatic history,” Godfrey said. African religions are also coded here: the red crosses “might strike European viewers as things connected to the symbol for help, but in Yoruban symbology, the red cross is associated with Eshu, who’s one of the pantheon of Yoruban gods,” Godfrey explained. There are also veves—pencil diagrams drawn to summon gods—in Voyager (1992), which come from Haitian voodoo.

The next sequence engages with everyday life, as painters in France did. Kerry spent a lot of time with Georges Seurat’s A Sunday on La Grande Jatte, which influenced Past Times (1997). It’s from a series of paintings called The Garden Project—three of which hang here—set in project housing built in the ’40s and ’50s for communities of Black Americans who were migrating from the south. They were built in a spirit of optimism and hope, but by the time Kerry painted them, they’d become run-down and under-resourced. Rather than painting suffering and crime, Kerry paints people gardening, children playing, teenagers dating. There are Chicago skyscrapers on the horizon, references to the music of the Temptations or Snoop Dogg, and the Black middle class enjoying pursuits like water skiing and golf.

Godfrey noted that they’re as interesting in terms of fabrication as for their spirit: they were made on large unstretched canvases, painted on sheets of paper glued onto the surface, sometimes with collage elements. “Why I enjoy looking at them again and again is as much because of how they’re made as what they might be pointing to or about,” Godfrey said. This hybrid of history and leisure as related to the Black experience feels emblematic of Kerry’s work, but Godfrey notes that Jacob Lawrence’s paintings—although on a smaller scale—depict moments of important history, like the Haitian revolution, but also people playing pool, or children playing games on the streets of Harlem. “That combination of commitment to history and everyday, I think Kerry’s not the first Black artist to do that.”

De Style, a 1993 work featuring five guys at a barber shop, is one of the first paintings Kerry sold to a museum (LACMA). Meaningfully, Kerry visited that very museum growing up; he was both impressed by the large paintings there but also hyperaware that none featured Black subjects. The title namechecks Mondrian’s established movement, De Stijl—moreover, there are visual references to Mondrian with the planes of blue, red and yellow. “It’s both what you might expect to find in a barber shop and also not,” Godfrey reflected. “He’s very attentive to the organization of a picture” and, moreover, motivated by “different strategies of pictorial craft.”

Then there are four souvenir paintings that were made for an exhibition in 1997 at the Renaissance Society in Chicago. At the time they were made, Kerry was thinking about what the legacies of the 1960s meant in the 1990s. “Other people were doing that as well—Spike Lee made the film Malcolm X in this decade,” Godfrey noted. Middle-aged, middle-class Black women in domestic interiors (with Ebony magazines, lace doilies, floral sofas) appear as angelic figures under commemorative presences: the Kennedys, Martin Luther King, Medgar Evers, the four teenage girls killed in the church bombing in Birmingham, Alabama, in 1963, freedom riders, Malcolm X, members of the Black Panther Party like Fred Hampton and Mark Clark, who were killed in Chicago in 1969.

A work first shown in the Venice Biennale in 2003 is the polished floor-based sculpture Wake—it summons the journey made by enslaved Africans, but it also accumulates medallions depicting African Americans every time it’s made, growing and growing. As such, it draws on the legacy of the sacred power Nkisi Nkondi and the idea that, in West African sculpture, the more elements added, the more powerful it accrues. “Instead of having a vision of ongoing trauma, Kerry replaces that with this sculpture which is about increased cultural achievements,” Godfrey explained.

Changing scale, an interlude in the museum showcases original sketches from his comic Rhythm Mastr, which was self-published but officially reissued in 2025 with Rizzoli. Begun in 1999 and ongoing, it features fighters pushing back against the problems that affect Black American neighborhoods in a substitute for the South Side of Chicago; Kerry did the visuals and the text. (In London, an independent show dedicated to Rhythm Mastr happened alongside the main painting show.)

Further in, the ongoing painting of modern life is beautifully evoked in School of Beauty, School of Culture (2012), made 20 years after Kerry painted the barbershop scene: it acts in dialogue with it, as a kind of sister work. People are seen getting ready for a night out, posing, taking photos of each other. Kerry sometimes makes his own clothes in order to see how they fall on a figure; some of the fabrics therein are based on silhouettes he will have made.

Kerry was looking at Fragonard, Watteau and the tradition of rococo Romantic painting for the early 2000s series Vignettes. Love scenes are taking place, yet Kerry makes it clear there is no romantic space outside of politics for Black couples. The glittered painting that is maybe the most spectacular of the series, Vignette #14 (La La La), includes a Pan-African flag, a fire in the distance, the toppled torch of the Statue of Liberty. “The tune is ‘Ma Cherie Amour’ from Stevie Wonder, but the look on the guy’s face is one of concern rather than great love,” according to Godfrey. That unease is just as much a part of these works as the dominant gauzy pink.

The elaborate, heterogeneous 2014 painting Untitled (Studio), from the Met’s collection, acknowledges the tradition of studio painting in Paris, as in The Painter’s Studio (1855) by Gustave Courbet. In a close study on the Met’s website, it is noted that the table is crowded with cans of paint, brushes and knickknacks, including a jokey-looking skull and a small bust of President Abraham Lincoln (“assassinated just months before the 13th Amendment enshrined freedom for enslaved persons across the country”). There are two nude men and two clothed women, and an easel on which the scene is not being replicated. It is elaborate and contains multitudes, oddities mixed with classicism.

The show ends with a sequence of eight works newly made for this exhibition, which premiered at the Royal Academy. Here, the history of slavery includes how one African tribe would enslave people from another. In Cove (2025), slaves are being walked onto a beach to be loaded onto canoes and then shipped out. Nearby, a painting shows the return journey to shore where the canoe is now laden with the booty from the slave sales. Some things Kerry put into this painting are historically accurate, like cowry shells, kegs of rum and guns—but some are absurdly fictional, like gift-wrapped presents. “He’s often blurring or confusing the idea of what a history painting is and what a fantasy painting is,” Godfrey said.

The last painting in the show, from 2025, also blurs history and fantasy: Léopold Senghor, one of the key figures within the Négritude movement, wrote about the beauty of Blackness shortly before becoming president of Senegal and married Frenchwoman Colette Hubert. The wedding happened in 1957; Senegal became independent in 1960. Kerry’s depiction is “as if Senegal and France were almost sewn together”—quite literally through a union of flags, as if proposing a speculative history, mixing accurate research and total fabrication.

At the time of the show’s opening, Godfrey accompanied Marshall to the Louvre and the Musée d’Orsay, where he was “deeply happy” and an eager observer of the “compression of space” among older paintings by, say, Ingres. Although reimagination is intrinsic to Marshall’s work, Godfrey noted that he looks to artists of the past to consider “pictorial compositional challenges… in such a way that it will be engaging five hundred years after you’ve made it.”

One platform where AI agents work as a team

Most AI tools, however, operate in isolation, leaving teams to pay for several products and stitch the results together by hand. Mockingjay Network brings all of that into a single environment. No code, your models, your machines

Mockingjay is delivered as a desktop application that businesses install and run locally.

Public-area lots include sculptural lounge chairs and bespoke bistro and large-format dining tables with polished marble, terrazzo or dark-stained timber surfaces on cast-brass and cast-iron bases. The collection also includes restaurant furnishings, sculptures, fine art, decorative objects and architectural lighting. The hotel specification includes Crib 5 fire-rated items and BREEAM Excellent environmental compliance.

The sale offers hotel operators, developers, interior designers, restaurateurs, build-to-rent operators and private buyers the opportunity to acquire coordinated furnishings. The Other House South Kensington opened in 2022 as a Residents’ Club for members and overnight guests. Aendre Group, founded by Eric Jafari and Chris Strong, acquired The Other House group in July 2026.

Simon Rose of Pro Auction said: “Usually, an auction of this scale follows the conclusion of an established operational lifecycle. To step into an iconic Covent Garden island site that was on the threshold of its July 2026 debut and find thousands of pieces from Europe’s top-tier luxury manufacturers—spanning both private guestrooms and magnificent public lounges—that have never been used, never been scratched, and remain in immaculate condition is unheard of. It is an extraordinary opportunity for developers, operators, and design professionals alike.”

The auction will be held as a live and online webcast, with global bidding through major auction platforms. Viewing and digital walkthroughs will be available by appointment.
