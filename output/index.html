Photographic poetry: Melani Tutt elevates mystique of Cheyenne Cañon

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Melani Tutt’s eyes view the world in intricacy, seeking out the play of light like the lens of a camera, as she captures the gradations of a fern’s wispiness or the variable depth of a fog bank rolling out.

“I can be driving home and stuck under the Tejon Street underpass in late afternoon and because of the way the light hits, see the texture and symmetry in a cracked wall with rusty pipes,” she said.

Her visual superpower has become her life’s pursuit as a professional photographer and pictorial author.

Tutt’s new book, “Sanctuary: North Cheyenne Cañon,” strives to show all who enjoy Colorado Springs landscapes an unseen vantage point of one of the most familiar. 

“Intimate portraits of the simple beauty that surrounds us daily,” is how Tutt describes her elegant, high-quality hardback. 

From wide vistas to intricate details, Melani Tutt seeks to preserve the stunning sights of North Cheyenne Cañon in southeast Colorado Springs. (Courtesy of Melani Tutt)

The purpose of the limited-edition book is twofold, Tutt said. 

She hopes her photographic art unearths vivid memories for all who have walked, run, biked, driven, picnicked and soaked in the peace of the specific section of nature.

Perhaps by spending time lost in the book’s depictions, people can relive their past enjoyment of the canyon from afar, maybe sitting on a couch.

For 30 years, Tutt has been a frequent canyon hiker and bicyclist, finding refuge in the solitude and splendor of the park in southwest Colorado Springs.

Before the unsettling days of the COVID-19 pandemic, Tutt began photographing the four seasons across the popular area, which has attracted locals and visitors since Colorado Springs citizens purchased the core 640 acres in 1885.

A few decades later, in 1907, Colorado Springs founder William Jackson Palmer donated nearly 500 acres, and over the years the park grew from other smaller donations. 

A graduate of the Brooks Institute of Photography in California, Tutt felt called to immortalize the canyon from a 21st-century perspective, in all its vast glory and hidden delights.

“I think our parks are struggling with overuse, there’s graffiti, the threat of wildfire,” Tutt said.

Her process for this most recent era of her career comes directly from an interplay with the canyon — finding a comfortable sitting spot to meditate, then intuitively lifting the camera to snap a specific moment of lucidity.

Tutt does what’s needed to catch the best shot, scrambling up and down hillsides to locate the distant melody of trickling water, or leaning against cold granite on a winter day to inhale the crisp air and be present when unexpected signs of life appear. 

“I’ll go out in the worst of the weather, when it’s snowy, rainy, foggy and frozen. Most people don’t see the canyon in bad weather; I think it’s beautiful. I like to find something people might miss normally.”

Melani Tutt goes out in all kinds of weather to capture familiar and hidden beauty in North Cheyenne Cañon, as portrayed in her new book, “Sanctuary: North Cheyenne Cañon.” (Courtesy of Melani Tutt)

“Sanctuary: North Cheyenne Cañon” is the first pictorial documentation of the site and follows Tutt’s exploration of another local landmark in her first book, “The Sacredness of Everything.”

That book, published in 2019, began as a quest to provide photos for rooms at the Garden of the Gods Resort and Wellness Center.

The images evolved into the fine art book that’s also presented in a coffee table format, as is her newest book.

Tutt has done commercial photography for more than 35 years and more recently has focused on nonprofits in the community, from Early Connections Learning Centers and Habitat for Humanity to the Tactile Gallery at the Colorado Springs Fine Arts Center at Colorado College, among others.

Like early Colorado Springs resident and author Helen Hunt Jackson — for whom the canyon’s Helen Hunt Falls is named — Tutt finds that North Cheyenne Cañon’s diverse flora and fauna, breathtaking views and cascading waterfalls speak to the heart and soul.

Both women discovered a “reverent connection with nature,” as Hunt wrote, and Tutt safeguards through her work.

Which highlights the second goal of “Sanctuary: North Cheyenne Cañon” — supported by the book’s historical introduction from the curator of history for the Colorado Springs Pioneers Museum, Leah Davis Witherow: to showcase what the canyon looks like in its current state.

“So that 100 years from now, it’s a record of what the canyon is today, as a visual historical document,” Tutt said.

To reveal new views of a beloved place, to give a physical way to recollect and enjoy again and again, and to provide a concrete record at this turning point in preservation. 

Photographic artist Melani Tutt shoots in black-and-white and color. (Courtesy of Melani Tutt)

One of Tutt’s photos from North Cheyenne Cañon, titled, “First Light on Daniel’s Pass Trail,” is part of an exhibit currently on display at the Colorado Springs Pioneers Museum. The collection of work by various artists is called “50% of the Story: Women Expressing Creativity.”

A book signing will be held 1-4 p.m. on Nov. 14 at Broadmoor Galleries, 1 Lake Ave., which represents Tutt as a featured gallery artist.

For more information, go to https://broadmoorgalleries.com/.

A Los Angeles Itinerary for Art Lovers

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Los Angeles is arguably one of the world’s premier art capitals, even if it doesn’t quite have that reputation. Perhaps that’s because L.A. sprawls, and its distributed ecosystem of world-class institutions, mega-galleries, independent artist spaces and art schools doesn’t feel cohesive the way some other art scenes do. Still, it’s not for nothing that many of the 20th and 21st Centuries’ most influential conceptual and studio artists—John Baldessari, Mike Kelley, Paul McCarthy, Catherine Opie, Mark Bradford, Charles Gaines—made teaching in L.A. an integral part of their practices.

My last visit to Los Angeles was so long ago that I honestly wasn’t sure what to expect, but the scene turned out to be as sprawling as the city itself. The museum landscape ranges from encyclopedic institutions like LACMA and the Getty to contemporary-focused ones like LA MOCA, the Hammer and the Broad. Gallery-wise, there are plenty of blue-chip flagships, as well as innovative midsize and boutique galleries that champion more idiosyncratic talent.

You’ll probably see less art in four days here than you might elsewhere, and even eight days wouldn’t be too many; you could still visit a different art museum every day. Tempering your expectations is important. There’s less traffic than you’d expect, but everything is farther apart, so I recommend planning each day around a single neighborhood. Here’s how I structured my time in the City of Angels, which happened to coincide with Los Angeles Design Week/end.

Day 1

Check into The LINE LA

  • The LINE LA in Koreatown has built a loyal following thanks to its design, music and club credentials. Originally built in 1964 as the Wilshire Plaza, the mid-century modern hotel got a brutalist interior makeover when the LINE took over in 2013. On the outside, it’s classic L.A. Inside, it has a very millennial identity, though not in a bad way, with lobby decor that includes velvet benches and textured wall coverings cut from indigo denim and T-shirt fabric. The hallway to my room felt like a liminal space, all echoey bare concrete, but the room more than made up for it with a massive bed facing floor-to-ceiling windows and a brilliant view of the Hollywood sign. All in all, it’s a wonderful home base for exploring Koreatown and sites along the Metro line.
The Line Hotel In Koreatown. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Explore Koreatown

Koreatown is one of the most walkable neighborhoods in Los Angeles, covering roughly three square miles along Wilshire Boulevard, Olympic Boulevard and 6th Street. Wander without a destination and you’ll run into dessert cafés, soju bars, pocha, Art Deco apartment buildings that look frozen in time and plenty of street art. The neighborhood also has the city’s highest concentration of nightclubs and 24-hour businesses, so if all-night private karaoke (noraebang) is your thing, you’re in luck. Keep in mind that much of what Koreatown has to offer hides in plain sight: behind parking lots, up unmarked staircases, on second-floor balconies and inside shopping plazas that give no hint from the sidewalk of what’s within. Finding the best of it takes a willingness to go looking. If you’re pressed for time, head to Chapman Market, a Spanish Colonial Revival plaza turned food hub, for Korean pub food at Toebang Cafe.

L.A.’s Koreatown is loaded with great street art. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Have dinner at Openaire

If you’re staying at the LINE LA, plan to eat at least one meal at Openaire, the hotel’s in-house restaurant. Set on the pool deck inside a glass conservatory framed by exposed ironwork and filled with lush greenery, it makes for a very cool indoor-outdoor experience. Chef Josiah Citrin’s menus focus on seasonal ingredients and California cuisine, and dinner options tend toward simple ingredients, creatively prepared. We sampled the milk bread with porcini butter (which was to die for) and the roasted Japanese sweet potato with miso cream, and from there we worked our way down the entree list, from branzino to brick-pressed jidori chicken. The cocktail program is built around botanical infusions and fresh citrus, with nods to Koreatown’s East Asian flavors. I recommend the Forbidden Fruit, a light, effervescent mix of vodka, jasmine green tea, lychee, lemon juice and sparkling white wine.

Openaire, on the pool deck of the LINE LA, is in an actual greenhouse. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Grab drinks at Kiss Kiss Bang Bang

The LINE LA has two speakeasy-style clubs dreamed up by nightlife veterans Mark and Jonnie Houston: Break Room 86 and Kiss Kiss Bang Bang. Break Room 86 is a love letter to 1980s pop culture, with cassette tapes, TV sets playing period broadcasts, vintage lockers and classic Atari arcade games. Kiss Kiss Bang Bang, meanwhile, leans into 1970s Studio 54-style disco decadence; you enter through a concealed facade built to look like a working bank of ATMs. Inside, there’s a multicolored light-up dance floor, mirror balls, plush velvet and brass. DJs spin disco edits, funk, house and classic dance cuts for roller-skating performers and go-go dancers, and the drinks are throwbacks made modern: elevated Harvey Wallbangers, tropical sours and champagne cocktails. Both clubs draw lines down the block by 10:30 p.m. on Friday and Saturday nights, but you can book table service or a karaoke room ahead of time if you want to feel like a VIP.

You reach Kiss Kiss Bang Bang, which has a decadent 1970s glam feel, by walking through an ATM. Photo: Dylan James Ho and Jeni Afuso, courtesy of the LINE LA

Day 2

Unwind at Wi Spa

Wi Spa is Los Angeles’s most famous and expansive traditional Korean bathhouse, with four floors open 24 hours a day, seven days a week. The gender-segregated floors have hot, warm and cold-plunge soaking tubs, dry saunas, steam rooms and shower stations, both seated and standard U.S.-style. (Note: clothing and swimsuits are strictly prohibited in the tubs, so be ready to chill out in the buff.) The big draw is the signature scrub, known as seshin or simply the “Korean Buff,” Wi Spa’s most renowned treatment, in which a practitioner exfoliates nearly every inch of you with textured viscose mitts. After the shampoo and oil massage that followed, I was probably the cleanest I’ve ever been. On the communal floor, some spa-goers nap on mats in a large open space while others pop in and out of several saunas with different health benefits, including salt, clay, jade and ice. The clay sauna, where you can burrow into a bed of warm beads, felt cozy. The bulgama, which heats up to about 230°F, was punishing.

The soaking tubs at Wi Spa. Courtesy Wi Spa

Have lunch at Hangari Kalguksu

Hangari Kalguksu is one of Koreatown’s most beloved comfort-food destinations, known for its huge portions and equally huge lines. Luckily, the queue moves fast, so you’ll be sampling traditional noodle soups, bossam, sujebi and dumplings relatively quickly. Start with the pork belly (or jowl) served with radish kimchi, leafy greens and condiments, then, if it’s a hot day, choose a cold noodle dish like the perilla oil makguksu. Just be aware that the enormous stoneware bowl will be filled to the brim, with more than enough for two, maybe three, lunches.

Pork belly and pork jowl served with crunchy cabbage leaves and sweet and spicy radish kimchi. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Tour the David Geffen Galleries at LACMA

LACMA as a whole holds some 120,000 objects and works, making it the largest museum collection west of the Mississippi and, by its own description, an encyclopedic one. The new David Geffen Galleries is an elevated, single-story structure that sprawls like an amoeba across Wilshire Boulevard, and it has been generally well received, considered a must-see for art lovers. The works inside aren’t sorted by era, movement or national boundaries, but loosely by ocean. “One of our junior curators asked, could we get away from the politics of land and think about how ideas and people and goods moved over waterways to create culture?” Rebecca Morse, LACMA’s curator of photography, told me. The eclectic mix of contemporary paintings alongside ancient artifacts and classical sculpture heads off museum fatigue, as does the deliberate lack of placards. “There are QR codes and some extended information, but there’s not a ton of didactic information, and that’s by design. We wanted people to have a really visual experience and not feel like they had to read. Of course, how people feel about that varies.” The works in the main loop and the walled-off galleries are more spaced out than you’d expect, so a visit can be quick without feeling thin.

Manjunath Kamath, Vikatonarva, 2024. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Shop at ROW DTLA

ROW DTLA is ostensibly a self-contained retail and dining district, but it feels like more than that when you’re strolling around after sundown. There’s Rappahannock Oyster Bar, design shops, third-wave coffee, the famous Pizzeria Bianco and two Michelin-starred restaurants, Kato and Hayato. There’s also high-end curated vintage fashion, Japanese design goods and artsy ceramics. If it’s art you’re after, Friday Gallery focuses on talent from the Middle East, South Asia, Latin America and elsewhere in the Global South. On Sundays, LA Smorgasburg transforms the central lot into a sprawling street-food festival, but my visit was built around Christina Chae’s silver clay workshop at Claedo Studio. She walks you through the ins and outs of working with silver clay as you create an original design (with or without gemstones), which is then fired and polished while you wait.

Sculpt a custom ring in Christina Chae’s silver clay workshop at Claedo Studio. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Have dinner at Manuela

Manuela, the restaurant inside the Hauser & Wirth complex in the Arts District, pairs easily with a visit to the galleries. Set in the restored Globe Mills complex, a collection of interconnected late 19th- and early 20th-century indoor-outdoor spaces, it has a luxuriant vibe, with loads of greenery. Across the courtyard, the restaurant keeps a potager where herbs, pomegranates, chiles and more are grown for the kitchen and bar, and resident chickens supply at least some of the eggs. Executive chef Kris Tominaga, whose background is in Southern cuisine, runs a seasonal menu with vegetables, grains, fish and game sourced from Southern California farms and producers. Our table shared the ceviche, a Persian cucumber salad, cream biscuits with honey butter and country ham, cornbread with a generous topping of butter and several other starters with and without down-home flair. The cocktail program uses house-made bitters and tonics. The parsley gimlet was particularly refreshing on a hot night, and the nonalcoholic Ta Ta Tatiana, with watermelon, ginger, honey, lemon, mint and bitters, made a great chaser. For my main, I ordered a simple steak so perfect I still think about it.

Just a few of the apps at Manuela in the Arts District. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Find a party, see some art

My trip overlapped with the kickoff of Los Angeles Design Week/end, a citywide grassroots festival spotlighting the region’s design community. It opened with a party at Small Green Door, a boutique creative production studio and community art space, with live DJs, art installations, design, drinks, food, dancing, flash tattoos, workshops and magic. Just inside was “After the Tide,” a materially oriented group exhibition curated by Nadia Khagani and Rose Zhang featuring ceramics, sonic works and sculpture. Rather than the warehouse I’d expected, the space beyond the show was a maze, with art and activities around every corner. The crowd was an eclectic mix of ages and dress codes, showcasing the diversity of L.A.’s creative community. (To find a party happening while you’re in town, try Resident Advisor.)

One of the works at Rhythm of the Night, a Los Angeles Design Weekend celebration presented with Dublab at Small Green Door. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Day 3

Check in at Silver Lake Pool & Inn

Swapping hotels is a little insane, I’ll admit, but when time is limited and you’re spending most of a day in one neighborhood, it’s not a bad idea given the city’s sprawl. We chose the boutique Silver Lake Pool & Inn (one of Observer’s best boutique hotels in L.A.) at the intersection of Santa Monica Boulevard and Griffith Park Boulevard, in the heart of Silver Lake. It has a relaxed mid-century California aesthetic with Mediterranean coastal influences. The centerpiece is the classic palm-fringed rooftop pool deck with its bar and striped lounges shaded by scalloped umbrellas. The rooms are styled with warm wood accents, terrazzo tiling and custom mid-century modern revival furniture. While I appreciated the LINE LA’s buzzy location and expansive view, my room at Silver Lake Pool & Inn felt homey and cozy—more like a studio apartment than a hotel.

The eponymous pool at Silver Lake Pool & Inn. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Walk around Echo Park

After getting settled in Silver Lake, we met up with Holland Denvir, founder of Los Angeles Design Week/end, to check out the Eastside design scene. This was the third year of LADW, and “the goal is really to share with residents and visitors what’s here locally—artist studios, galleries you might not know about, workshops, wood shops, welding shops. This is a really good picture of what’s happening,” Denvir said. We saw only a tiny slice of it, since this year’s edition spanned more than 15 neighborhoods, with 130 designers, studios, galleries and brands participating. It’s anything but fussy. “We thought, there’s not going to be panels, because panels kind of stop you from discovering and progressing—it’s expert versus novice energy, and we didn’t want that,” explained Meghan McNeer, co-founder and creative director of LADW. “We’re in the Wild West, we’re dreamers. So let’s get people on the street, let’s give them places they already know, but then next door there’s something activated that they can discover.” In Echo Park, we visited several spaces, including Dusk & Sun, a furniture showroom that hosts collaborative exhibitions with independent makers, and the studio of artist Peter Shire, known for boundary-defying work across fine art, ceramics and industrial design. Asked why he gravitates to bright colors and playful patterns, he said, “it’s a mystery. You’re in the middle of doing it, and you know, after a while you’re doing it some more, and then you get known for it and people want you to just do it. You’re constantly trying to figure out where it came from… But seriously, it makes me feel good. It’s really that simple.”

Artist Peter Shire was born in the Echo Park district of Los Angeles, where he still lives and works. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Visit the Paul McCobb Museum

Sticking with design, we headed back to Silver Lake for a tour of the Paul McCobb Museum, which is far from a typical museum experience. Founded by artist and conceptual collector Yogi Proctor, it occupies his mid-century home and is both a curatorial project and a scholarly archive devoted to preserving, documenting and studying the work of mid-century industrial designer Paul McCobb. Proctor led our tour himself, in the manner of classic house museums like the Sir John Soane’s Museum in London. Some pieces are arranged much as they were in Edgar Kaufmann Jr.’s “Good Design” exhibition series at MoMA in the 1950s. “This lamp, the armchairs, this table—those pieces were all included in that exhibition,” Proctor explained as he walked us through his home, which is beautifully styled with McCobb pieces in every room. Beyond furniture, the displays include McCobb-designed textiles and archival materials. (Note: tours are by appointment only, and the address is shared once you book at paulmccobbmuseum.org.)

The Paul McCobb Museum, housed in a private residence, is dedicated to the influential American designer’s work. Photo: Christa Terry for Observer

Have lunch at Bar Besito

Bar Besito is a tapas and wine bar in Silver Lake that pairs Spanish culinary traditions with seasonal California produce. The dining room is small but doesn’t feel cramped; sit at the tiled bar and you can watch the action in the kitchen. Chef Keith Phillips’s menu features shareable small plates and conservas alongside an all-Spanish beverage program of wines, vermouth, sherry and ciders. We ordered olives, croquetas, heirloom beans and tortilla for the table, then shared several bocadillos. The white sangria was the perfect touch, though if you’d rather have something nonalcoholic, there’s a Ghia lime and salt spritz. It was a relaxing pause before I set off on my own to go museum hopping.

Tour the Broad

Admission to the Broad is free and walk-ups are welcome, but without a timed ticket, you’ll have to wait in the line that snakes around the lobby. Founded by Eli and Edythe Broad, the museum holds a collection of postwar and contemporary hits. In other words, even if you can’t name the works on view, you can probably name their makers: Jean-Michel Basquiat, Keith Haring, Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein, Barbara Kruger, Kara Walker and so on. During my visit, one room compellingly paired works by John Baldessari and Cindy Sherman. The Koons display was popular, with a good selection of the classics. Pop over to LA MOCA

Hitting the Broad and LA MOCA back-to-back is easy, since they’re essentially across the street from each other on South Grand Avenue. Admission is also free, and without the congestion at the entrance and in the galleries, the experience is more relaxed. During my visit, the main exhibition was “In These Times: Recent Acquisitions,” a survey of works added to the collection over the past five years by artists including Christina Forrer, Charles Gaines, Sawako Goda, Jacqueline Humphries, Alex Israel, Tomashi Jackson, Julie Mehretu and Nairy Baghramian, Rebecca Morris, Diane Simpson, John Sparagana, Magdalena Suarez Frimkess, Henry Taylor and Ulla Wiggen. Have dinner at Sous Sous at Hotel Lucile

Hotel Lucile, in a former church, opened in September as the latest iteration of a space that has had many lives. Built in 1931, it hosted religious services into the 1980s, after which it became an antiques market. The recent renovation kept the existing bones, including the original stained glass and molding, so the property still has a churchy feel. Its art program is curated by dealers Rhett Baruch and Patty Sanchez, who have assembled a collection of antique and contemporary works that guests can purchase if they’re so inclined. The former chapel is now Sous Sous, a restaurant open to the public for dinner, with a menu inspired by the culinary traditions shared by the South of France and the American South. Think Marseille and Nice by way of Charleston and New Orleans: locally sourced seafood and produce, plenty of herbs, a raw bar and a cocktail program built around citrus and spice. We sat in the pulpit, sharing tomato salad, chicken liver mousse, crab salad and oysters. Colorado has become a hotbed of democracy innovation | Vince Bzdek

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We’re just two days from the midterms and a very consequential vote for both Colorado and the country.

But don’t expect the midterms to fix everything that is roiling Colorado politics and democracy in general.

No matter how the vote turns out Tuesday, there are deep structural issues that still need to be addressed, said Landon Mascarenaz, founding CEO of Courageous Colorado, a cross-partisan democracy reform organization.

“The midterms are important. People have to vote. They gotta use their voice,” he said. “But we have to have a real conversation about the fact that, in too many places, the primary election result is the general election result. Ninety percent of races in Colorado are over in the primary.”

Unaffiliated voters, who make up a majority of early voters so far in the midterms, think the current system isn’t giving them what they want, so they are exiting the two-party system, Mascarenaz added.

“Eighty percent of Coloradans want more choices in politics,” he said. Ninety percent of them believe our democracy is in crisis nationally and think that politics are a major part of the problem. “Coloradans are crying out for change.”

A civic engagement fair on the Lory Student Center Plaza at Colorado State last week. (Courtesy of Colorado State University)

Mascarenaz was one of the presenters last week at Colorado State University’s fifth annual Democracy Summit, which brings together students, faculty, community members and civic leaders to explore democracy’s challenges and its future, including the latest reforms and innovations aimed at strengthening civic life.

“Democracy is something we build together, and that takes conversation, curiosity and a willingness to engage with people who may see things differently,” said Kjerstin Thorson, dean of the College of Liberal Arts.

Turns out, CSU, Fort Collins and Colorado in general have become hotbeds for democracy innovation and reform.

“We are truly leading the nation in this work in lots of different ways,” said Martin Carcasson, founder and director of the Center for Public Deliberation in Fort Collins, which is celebrating its 20th year.

Take a look at all that is happening in Colorado surrounding democracy improvement:

The Democracy Summit is part of an ongoing Strengthening Democracy Initiative at CSU, which President Amy Parsons has tied to CSU’s original land-grant mission of democratizing higher education. The University of Colorado Boulder has a similarly named but separate Higher Education and Democracy Initiative, established in 2022.

CSU also hosts The Straayer Center for Public Service Leadership, which helps build community networks to influence policy and impact governance and the Center for Democracy, Art and Popular Culture.

Katie Knobloch, associate director of the CSU deliberation center, also helped create the Rural Action Project, which partnered with 15 rural communities recently to take action on needed public projects. She also helped form the Youth Civic Action Program, which pairs CSU students and high school students to address public problems in their communities.

The Office of Engagement and Extension at CSU also works closely on democracy projects with Colorado communities to address social, health, economic and safety challenges. And the Family Leadership Training Institute of Colorado at the CSU Extension helps train regular citizens to be leaders in community decision-making.

CSU also recently launched the Colorado Democracy Prize, which awards $5,000 annually to student groups that bring community members together across lines of difference.

Fort Collins is also the first big city in the state to agree to ranked choice voting and was the first city to host a citizens assembly that passed a ballot initiative. Such assemblies are a democratic innovation that encourages better citizen engagement in politics. They are fairly new in the States but have become all the rage in Europe.

The University of Denver also has held a democracy summit for the last five years, focused more on international challenges.

And DU hosts The Colorado Project as well, which serves as a state platform for deliberation focused on challenging issues affecting the state. DU just announced a national academy for free expression and pluralism as well, dedicated to renewing the idea that “democracy depends on the free exchange of ideas and the capacity to work across differences.” The academy builds on something DU established called the 4D experience, which “helps students develop the habits of mind and character that civic life requires.” They’ve also launched the Debate Across Curriculum and Conflict Engagement & Resolution Initiative.

In addition to Courageous Colorado, the CSU summit also featured another Denver-based nonprofit, The Civic Canopy, focused on strengthening democracy. The Civic Canopy facilitates community-driven processes across the state to coordinate action needed to address challenges like housing, homelessness, health and community resilience.

One of the most interesting projects highlighted at the CSU summit was something called Civic Dialogues, a partnership between Courageous Colorado and change.org.

Civic Dialogues is a nonpartisan platform that has gathered 11,000 responses from Coloradans in an effort to identify issues important to residents that cross party lines. The motivation: most voters say there is no easy way for representatives to really know what citizens care about.

The platform facilitates live, small-group conversations online that help participants find common ground on the state’s most complex policy challenges.

The group put together a list of the top 10 ideas and the top 10 goals that the 11,000 participants agreed on most strongly.

One hundred percent of participants agreed that term limits and enforcing anti-corruption laws were a priority. One hundred percent also agreed that town hall meetings to inform residents were an absolute necessity. Ninety-seven percent want corruption investigated regardless of party affiliation, and 95 percent want to ban out-of-state money in elections.

On top goals, 95 percent agreed that government spending should be audited much better, 95 percent want the influence of special interests reduced and 95 percent want increased legislative accessibility. This one caught my eye: 92 percent agree that civics education should be expanded.

That’s a lot of agreement from two sides we’re told are hopelessly polarized.

Attendees at the summit even got a small taste of participatory democracy themselves. A number of possible democratic reforms were displayed on posters at the conference, and we all walked around discussing pros and cons with student facilitators, then gave our thumbs-up or thumbs-down on each one. I tell you, it was the most “small d” democratic fun I’ve had in years.

Some of the reforms are things I’ve never really considered before, like allowing online signatures for candidates petitioning to be on the ballot or making Election Day a paid holiday.

The reforms we voted on included a top-two all-candidate open primary, in which all voters — Democratic, Republican and Independent — get to vote. Or ranked choice voting in presidential primaries, in which voters don’t just vote for their favorite candidate but rank their second, third and fourth choices as well. Or how about an age limit of 80 for statewide office? Or term limits of eight years for statewide office and local office?

Here’s something that would really get state legislators’ knickers in a twist: An annual statewide assembly of randomly selected citizens who study a major issue they pick each year, then recommend a course of action and the legislature has to vote on it. Another one that would curdle their coffee over at the Capitol: let state and local governments set aside part of their budgets each year for residents to decide how to spend directly. Or how about requiring a citizens panel for each ballot initiative, with the citizen summary is included in the Blue Book, the official state-issued voter guide?    

Students present democracy innovations for attendees at the CSU Democracy Summit to vote on. (Vince Bzdek, The Gazette)

Publicly financed campaigns were also floated, as was a stipulation that the top three donors behind super PACs giving to campaigns must be named in all political ads they pay for.

An idea that will get a test drive in Montana on Tuesday: stripping corporations formed under state law of their ability to spend money on campaigns. States apparently have the power to decide this, essentially nullifying the infamous Citizens United ruling — who knew!

And finally, a proposal dear to a journalist’s heart: require the state legislature’s website to allow anyone to look up every recorded committee and floor vote by each legislator.

All the proposals are aimed at getting citizens involved in civic life more deeply, beyond and outside elections alone. These crazy folk seem to think our government should be of, by and for the people. What a concept!

Patti Schmitt, community development director of the CSU extension office, said it this way: “Democracy begins with the experience of being heard.”

Many of the democracy reform and civic engagement groups in the state are working together on such projects right now.

Bill Fulton, founder of The Civic Canopy, said: “I feel like the appetite and atmosphere of collaboration has never been more dynamic.”

Courageous Colorado is trying to coordinate many of those partnerships, “working on ensuring that Colorado leads the nation to open up and build a stronger democracy. We have a massive opportunity in our society right now to confront the closed structures in our democracy because people are asking for it. They want it.”

In sum, Knobloch sees a lot of reasons for hope for a better politics in the state right now. “When you hand power back to citizens, they can do truly incredible things,” she said. “And when you are on the ground with folks trying to make a community a better place, there is no greater feeling.”

Forum: Springs’ space future is bright without Space Command

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

While Colorado Springs navigates a changing space environment, and one without a major combatant command that used to call the city home, it is in a good position to benefit from increasing federal investments in national security space.

During the 30th annual Economic Forum at the University of Colorado Colorado Springs, business leaders shared how the city and the region are squarely ready and perhaps even benefiting from Space Command’s ongoing departure.

Relocating the command will cost the region more than 1,000 jobs, but other initiatives are afoot that will likely offset those losses, said Seth Harvey and Dean Bucher, two Springs-based people in the space industry.

Space Command unifies the capabilities of all branches to fight battles in space. President Donald Trump ordered its departure from Colorado Springs to Huntsville, Ala., on Sept. 2, 2025. It is different from Space Force, which is a dedicated military branch like the Army or Navy, and still has a significant presence in Colorado Springs.

The Command’s move remains contentious. But in mid-October, city and regional business leaders and lawmakers traveled to Huntsville in a bid to bury the hatchet that had long divided the two cities. National security concerns and the drive to support warfighters in the U.S. military are too vital to squabble over the location, leaders said, according to a post from the Space Foundation.

While the loss may sting, Harvey and Bucher say the local future is bright, citing key developments:

The Space Force’s Space Systems Command, responsible for all logistics, acquisitions, launch and development within the young military branch, chose Colorado Springs to be the headquarters of the Infrastructure Portfolio Acquisition Executive. The office will oversee a staff of 300 people when it is fully moved to Colorado Springs, The Gazette previously reported. It controls a budget of $4 billion, according to published reports.

“I actually think that we are in a far better position today as an economy and a community with that decision being made by Space Force when we had Space Command,” said Harvey, the founder and CEO of Bluestaq, a Springs-based data analytics company. The epicenter of executing the nation’s space strategy and space plans will still be Colorado Springs, he said.

Seth Harvey, the founder and CEO of data company Bluestaq, speaks at a panel discussion during the 30th UCCS Economic Forum on Wednesday. (Alex Edwards, The Gazette)

With the acquisition executive’s move to town comes more investment on Schriever Space Force Base. In May, it was announced that a $250 million Space Force Operations Facility will be built on the secluded base, which will support 2,500 jobs.

Military spending on space is set to increase. The White House in April proposed increasing the Space Force’s budget for fiscal year 2027 to $71.1 billion, more than doubling the $32 billion it received in 2026. This bodes well for Colorado Springs, said Dean Bucher, the general manager of the Aerospace Corporation’s space enterprise and warfighting division.

The money has, however, not yet been approved by Congress, as the National Defense Authorization Act has not cleared the legislative branch.

Should the Space Force’s budget double, it will have trickle-down effects. There will be more opportunity and need for the nearby communities and local businesses to support the Space Force mission, Bucher said.

It’s not just military spending. Last year, commercial space activity surpassed $544.3 billion, an increase of 13% compared to 2024’s total of $481.5 billion, according to the Space Foundation. It is projected to continue growing, a foundation report said.

“The amount of money that’s going into space and space capabilities is just huge,” Bucher said. “A big part of Space Force’s strategy going forward is to leverage these commercial capabilities because they’re moving rapidly with a lot of innovation.”

Dean Bucher, the general manager of the Aerospace Corporation’s space enterprise and warfighting division, addresses a crowd at the 30th UCCS Economic Forum on Wednesday. (Alex Edwards, The Gazette)

“There’s a lot of opportunity for Space Force to take advantage of that, regardless of what budget they may have going forward,” he said.

Among the projects Colorado Springs stands to gain from is the Golden Dome for America, a missile shield intended to defend the entire nation. The military leader charged with developing it, Gen. Michael Guetlein, estimates it could cost $185 billion over 10 years.

So far, Congress has invested $22.5 billion in the project.

But as exciting as the fiscal impacts of the program may be, its future is murky, and William Craighead, the director of the UCCS Economic Forum, said people should temper their expectations.

“I think people are looking at this as a potential source of a lot of economic activity and high-wage jobs for the Pikes Peak region. I’m not saying that won’t happen, but we need to be a little more cautious about our expectations given the federal budget picture,” Craighead said.

The nation is facing an unprecedented and growing deficit, which reached nearly $2 trillion two weeks ago, according to Reuters. This is an issue today since the government is tapping into the pool of savings it uses to also finance business investment, construction and mortgages. Long term, this could push up interest rates, Craighead said.

Seth Harvey of Bluestaq and Dean Bucher of the Aerospace Corporation lead a panel discussion on Colorado Springs’ future without Space Command. The panel was held as part of the 30th UCCS Economic Forum on Wednesday. (Alex Edwards, The Gazette).

The $1.5 trillion budget proposed by President Trump would only increase the deficit, leading to alarm from some.

But Harvey pointed out that national security space is bipartisan. President Trump created the Space Force in his first term, President Biden increased its budget during his term and, in Trump’s second term, the force’s budget is set to grow further, Harvey said.

“Across both sides of the aisle, there is a recognition that Space Force is a force multiplier for our armed forces,” he said. “There is a greater emphasis being put on bringing Space Force to the forefront of the fight because they recognize the advantages that we can have in every conflict by leveraging our space assets.”

Received more than one ballot? This could be the reason

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A 10-year resident of Colorado Springs who votes in every election, Theresa Martinez was expecting her El Paso County ballot in the mail. She was not expecting the arrival of another ballot from Jefferson County.

“I thought it was kind of peculiar,” she said.

The extra ballot had just one question on it: a ballot measure on whether to increase taxes for the West Metro Fire District, which serves Lakewood and surrounding communities. Martinez is not alone in her confusion about special district elections, says Jefferson County Clerk and Recorder Amanda Gonzalez.

“We’ve been getting a lot of calls about this,” she said.

Special districts are government bodies that provide services like firefighting, sanitation and utilities. They often collect revenue for operations through taxes, which have to be approved by voters. Unlike a regular election ballot, a special district’s ballot can have different criteria for voter eligibility. A Colorado voter who owns taxable property in the district but does not live there can often be eligible to vote.

The list of voters who will receive a special district’s ballot is created by the special district itself, said Gonzalez, though a county clerk and recorder’s office generally handles the work of distributing and counting them. That means it’s the special district’s job to decide who gets a property owner ballot, not the state’s.

Martinez says she’s not aware of any property she owns in Jefferson County that would make her eligible for a West Metro Fire District ballot. Gonzalez said recourse would be to the special district, not election officials.

“Ultimately, the special districts themselves are the ones that determine eligibility for their districts and create that voter list,” she said.

Issues have come up this year when it comes to other special district elections, most notably when 800,000 voters erroneously received a ballot with a question about a tax increase for the creation of a Front Range passenger rail system. The error came from miscommunication about the updated boundaries of the rail district, which includes 13 Colorado counties.

The Secretary of State’s Office has sent guidance to the affected counties on how to tabulate votes to distinguish eligible voters. The protocol will likely include some hand-counting, according to previous Gazette reporting.

In Pueblo County, officials have provided information in response to questions from the public about receiving extra ballots. Due to “lack of accuracy” in special districts vetting registered voter lists against property records, some people who are special district residents also received property owner ballots.

“The entities who submitted content to be included on the November ballot are contractually responsible for accurately vetting and certifying registered voter lists before submitting them to the Election Department,” the statement read.

While potentially “confusing or concerning,” receiving multiple ballots is not necessarily a sign of a mistake, the county’s statement also read. Voters with questions are encouraged to reach out to the Pueblo County Election Department.

“The elections department has a process to verify that people and property owners only get one vote in any of these areas,” said Daniel Lepick, Pueblo County elections supervisor, in a Facebook video explaining the situation.

For people who receive a property owner ballot that they intend to cast, Gonzalez said they can use a secure drop box in any Colorado county just like they would for a regular ballot. The day after Election Day, metro Front Range election officials do a ballot swap to match ballots with their corresponding counties. Ballots received for other parts of the state are also transferred where they need to go.

“It makes sense that people are asking questions and wanting to make sure they have accurate information,” Gonzalez said.
