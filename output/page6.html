Tencent’s WorkBuddy adds remote control, letting users manage AI tasks from their phones

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On Thursday, WorkBuddy, Tencent’s AI agent platform, launched a remote control feature supported by Tencent’s Edge One team. The feature allows users to connect to a computer logged into the same account via the mobile app, view the desktop in real time, and take control of operations when necessary.

Unlike traditional remote desktop tools, which primarily focus on remote access between devices, WorkBuddy’s new feature is designed specifically for AI agent workflows. Users can monitor an agent’s actions in real time and intervene when tasks go off track or require manual authorization.

To use the feature, users need to update the mobile app to version 2.7.0 or later and the macOS desktop client to version 5.9.3 or later. Once connected, the computer screen appears in a floating window on the app’s home page or at the top of a conversation. Users can monitor the agent without leaving the current chat.

If an agent encounters a problem while executing a task, users can intervene directly from their phones. For example, if an account is not logged in or a connector’s authorization has expired, users can try to resolve the issue on their mobile devices rather than returning to their computers.

For manual control, users can tap the full-screen button, which switches the phone interface to landscape mode and enables them to operate the desktop via touch.

The interface provides three options: Controls, Keyboard, and Mouse, which support actions such as switching windows, returning to the desktop, and entering text. A virtual mouse is also available for more precise clicking and scrolling in smaller areas of the screen.

On security, Tencent says the feature limits each computer to one authenticated mobile device at a time. Screen data and control commands are transmitted through an encrypted channel.

The platform does not record user input or store screen footage from remote control sessions. Network traffic generated by remote control is charged against the device’s data usage and does not consume WorkBuddy’s AI agent token quota.

To get started, users must enable the remote connection option for devices logged into the same account in WorkBuddy’s settings, install the required remote control component, and grant screen recording and accessibility permissions. Once configured, users can connect remotely as long as the computer remains powered on and WorkBuddy is online.

In practical terms, the feature helps solve a common challenge in AI agent workflows: the need for human intervention during task execution. AI agents can already handle multi-step tasks such as organizing documents and compiling data, but they may still encounter login requirements, permission requests, or operational errors.

Remote control allows users to monitor progress and make adjustments from their phones without having to stay at their desks.

However, the feature may offer more than just a way for users to operate computers from their phones. It could introduce a more flexible model of human-AI collaboration, with AI handling repetitive work while users step in to make decisions and correct mistakes at critical points.

Rather than pursuing fully autonomous execution at all costs, retaining human oversight may be a more practical approach for AI productivity tools at their current stage of development.

That said, a smartphone cannot fully replicate the experience of working on a computer. Remote access may be convenient for simple tasks such as approving permissions or switching windows, but its efficiency in more complex workflows involving data processing and multiple windows remains to be tested.

The feature is now available for trial to all users. Tencent says future versions will introduce more enterprise-focused capabilities, including tiered permission management and audit logs.

Andy Jassy’s prediction: Amazon could have two $1 trillion businesses within a decade

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Amazon CEO Andy Jassy told analysts Thursday that “some folks are sleeping a bit” on how much progress the company’s stores business has made. (GeekWire File Photo)

No company has ever had a business with $1 trillion in annual revenue. Andy Jassy says Amazon is on a path to have two.

Amazon Web Services and the company’s stores business could both reach that milestone in the next five to 10 years, the Amazon CEO said Thursday in the company’s third-quarter earnings release and on its call with analysts.

It’s “pretty rare” for Jassy to make a projection that far out, Morgan Stanley analyst Brian Nowak observed on the call, asking how he gets there.

Jassy’s answer started with AWS, which is at a $184 billion annual revenue run rate after growing 39% in the third quarter. He expects growth of more than 40% in both 2027 and 2028, and pointed to a $578 billion backlog of contracted AWS revenue.

The largest customers reserve capacity in huge blocks, sometimes as much as a gigawatt at a time, giving Amazon “very good visibility” into 2027, 2028 and part of 2029, he said.

Beyond that, he said, businesses are still early in using AI, the cost of running AI models keeps falling, and new uses keep emerging, such as AI models finding and fixing security flaws.

“You can’t predict with any certainty five to 10 years from now, especially at these types of growth rates,” Jassy said of AWS. “But it’s not a stretch for us to imagine that this could be over a trillion dollar business.”

Jassy’s forecast is a big jump from seven months ago. In March, Jassy told employees that AWS had a chance to reach at least $600 billion in annual revenue within about a decade, double his earlier expectation, according to Reuters.

Amazon’s stores business is bigger than AWS. Sales from Amazon’s online and physical stores totaled $309 billion over the past 12 months, or about $575 billion if Jassy is including third-party seller services and advertising in his trillion-dollar projection. We’ve asked Amazon to clarify.

For comparison, Walmart, the world’s largest retailer, had $713 billion in revenue in its most recent fiscal year.

Whichever numbers he’s using, Jassy said “some folks are sleeping a bit” on how much progress the stores business has made.

Jassy argued that Amazon’s stores business is gaining ground on competitors. Amazon added more than $90 billion in gross sales over the past 12 months, he said, and is adding about twice as much per quarter as its next-largest U.S. competitor.

Units sold are up nearly 15% so far this year, Jassy said. He noted that nearly 80% of retail sales still happen in physical stores, which he expects to shift online over time.

The bold prediction came on a rough day for the company. Amazon shares fell more than 7% in after-hours trading after its profit fell short of Wall Street’s expectations.

It’s not going to be cheap to get there. Amazon expects to spend about $220 billion on capital projects this year, mostly for AWS, and Jassy said the company will spend “meaningfully more” in 2027. He said Amazon has “a clear line of sight to attractive financial returns” and is earning back its capital spending faster than before.

The impact is showing up in Amazon’s finances. Its free cash flow, the cash left over after capital spending, was negative $42.4 billion over the past 12 months, compared with positive $14.8 billion a year earlier.

With its spending running ahead of the cash its businesses bring in, Amazon has raised $115 billion through long-term debt this year. Its interest expense more than tripled in the third quarter to $1.7 billion, cutting into its profit.

“We’re in the very early days of this inflection,” Jassy said. “Our strategy is working, and I expect it will for the foreseeable future.”

Multi-tenant SaaS architecture lets one product serve many customer organizations while some or all of its components are shared. The real design decision is not “shared or not shared”. It is which layers you share (application, database, infrastructure), how you keep each tenant’s data and performance separate, and how you move a tenant to stronger isolation when a contract demands it. This guide walks through the models, the isolation controls, the scaling patterns and the data-protection duties that matter if you sell to customers in Poland and the EU.

TL;DR

  • Multi-tenancy means sharing some components among tenants. SaaS is the delivery model; multi-tenancy is one way to build it.
  • There are three core data and compute models: silo (dedicated stack), bridge (shared stack, dedicated schema) and pool (everything shared). Most mature products end up with a mix mapped to customer tiers.
  • Isolation must be enforced by shared mechanisms outside individual developers’ code. Login and permissions alone are not enough.
  • Noisy neighbors and uneven growth are handled with quotas, observability and cell-based architecture, which limits the blast radius of failures.
  • For Polish and EU customers, plan for GDPR Article 28 processor contracts, risk analysis and 72-hour breach notification from day one.

What multi-tenant means, and what a tenant is

A tenant is a customer or a group of users. In B2B that is typically a company and its employees; in B2C it can be a defined group such as a family. Microsoft’s Azure Architecture Center separates two ideas that are often blurred: SaaS is a business and delivery model in which the provider operates and manages the software, while multitenancy is an architectural approach in which some solution components are shared among tenants.

That distinction matters in practice. You can run a SaaS business on single-tenant deployments, and you can run an internal platform for several business units on a multi-tenant design. The frameworks that implement multi-tenancy describe the same logic: the ABP documentation defines the host as the company that owns and manages the application and the tenant as the customer using it, with each tenant receiving its own logical share of data, configuration, users, functionality and non-functional properties.

The apartment-building analogy is popular for a reason: shared plumbing, separate flats. The engineering work is in deciding how thick the walls are.

Single-tenant vs. multi-tenant: the trade-offs

Most leaders searching this topic want a clear comparison before they commit budget. Here is how the two approaches differ on the criteria that tend to drive the decision.

Criterion Single-tenant Multi-tenant Cost per customer Higher: dedicated infrastructure has low cost efficiency Lower: shared resources spread cost across tenants Isolation Strongest, easiest to explain to a security reviewer Depends on the controls you build and prove Noisy-neighbor risk Minimal Real, must be engineered against Maintenance Time-consuming: many deployments to patch and upgrade One codebase and one release train Customization Easy to fork per customer, which creates long-term drift Needs configuration, feature flags and extension points Onboarding speed Slower, infrastructure per customer Fast, often a database record and configuration

Microsoft’s guidance on tenancy models makes the economic point directly: shared resources can be less expensive, while a single-tenant deployment can be offered at a higher rate to customers who need greater isolation. The same page notes that dedicated infrastructure can reduce noisy-neighbor effects and suit customers with high isolation requirements, but is inefficient on cost and can make maintenance time-consuming.

The practical conclusion is that the question is rarely “which one”. It is “which tenants get which model, and can we change our minds later without a rewrite”.

The three core models: silo, bridge and pool

AWS’s guidance on multi-tenant architectures names three patterns that are useful on any cloud:

  • Silo: each tenant gets a dedicated application stack and database.
  • Bridge: the application stack is shared, but each tenant has a dedicated schema.
  • Pool: the application stack, database instance and objects are all shared.

Framework documentation describes the data side the same way. ABP supports three approaches to storing tenant data: a single database, a database per tenant, and a hybrid of the two.

When pool is the right default

Pool fits products with many small to mid-size tenants, similar usage patterns and standard requirements. Onboarding is cheap, operations are simple and infrastructure utilization is high. The price is that every query, cache key, queue message and log line must carry tenant context, and a single mistake can expose data across tenants.

When bridge earns its place

Bridge gives you per-tenant schema boundaries without per-tenant infrastructure. It suits products where tenants need different data extensions, or where you want per-tenant backup and restore and simpler data deletion. Schema migrations get harder as tenant count grows, so automate them early and test them against a representative sample of tenants.

When silo is worth the cost

Silo is justified when a customer requires its own encryption keys, a specific data-storage location or contractual isolation. Microsoft’s guidance on storage and data approaches lists tenants’ own encryption keys and different data-storage locations among the reasons to isolate more. Enterprise accounts, regulated healthcare data and public-sector deals are the usual triggers.

Map models to tiers, and write it down

A hybrid is where most successful SaaS products land: pool for the standard plan, bridge or silo for premium or regulated customers. One engineering guide suggests mapping tenancy models to business tiers and recording which tenants share what in architecture decision records. We agree with the habit, because the day an enterprise buyer asks “is our data on the same database as everyone else’s?” you want a one-page answer, not an archaeology project.

Isolation: where multi-tenant products actually fail

AWS’s whitepaper on tenant isolation strategies draws two lines that every architect should internalize. First, isolation is achieved through logical constructs, such as row-level security or unique tenant identifiers, or through physical separation, such as dedicated database instances or accounts. Second, isolation should rely on shared mechanisms outside individual developer code to scope access to resources, because authentication and authorization alone are not enough, and tenant context must flow through every interaction to downstream resources.

In plain terms: do not trust every developer to remember a WHERE tenant_id = ? clause.

Push isolation into the data layer

With a shared PostgreSQL database, row-level security moves the tenant filter into the database itself. AWS’s walkthrough of multi-tenant data isolation with PostgreSQL Row Level Security describes how policies scope what each connection can see, so a forgotten filter in application code returns nothing instead of leaking rows.

Make tenant context travel everywhere

A useful checklist for engineering leads:

  1. Resolve the tenant once, at the edge, from the subdomain, token claim or API key, and never from a client-supplied body field.
  2. Propagate it through service calls, message headers, background jobs, scheduled tasks, webhooks and logs.
  3. Scope caches and search indexes by tenant so that cache keys cannot collide.
  4. Make jobs tenant-aware, so one tenant’s 2 million-row export does not starve everyone else’s queue.
  5. Test for leaks, with automated cross-tenant tests in CI that try to read tenant B’s data using tenant A’s credentials.

Identity and access are a layer, not the whole answer

Identity design still deserves its own attention: roles, attributes, enterprise single sign-on and user provisioning. We cover those in detail in our guide to SaaS access control with RBAC, ABAC, SSO and SCIM, and in a practical piece on tenant-scoped B2B SSO that explains how per-tenant identity providers can cause outages if the tenant boundary is fuzzy.

Noisy neighbors and scaling

Sharing means one tenant’s spike can become everyone’s incident. This is the main operational cost of multi-tenancy, and it grows with your largest customers. Memory is a subtle case: a 2026 write-up on hybrid multi-tenant architecture for stateful services argues that competition for in-memory state can create noisy-neighbor problems even when compute is shared.

AWS’s well-architected guidance on cell placement lists the tools for uneven demand: capacity observability, quotas and limits, balanced traffic distribution, and migration of tenants, customers or partition keys when one dominates a cell.

A practical order of operations:

  • Measure per tenant first. Request rate, CPU time, database time, storage and queue depth, all tagged by tenant.
  • Add rate limits and quotas at the API and job layers, tied to plan tiers.
  • Separate workloads such as reporting, imports and AI inference from the transactional path.
  • Move the heaviest tenants to dedicated capacity before they force an architectural crisis.
  • Align pricing with consumption, which is where our usage-based billing playbook helps.

Cell-based architecture

Cell-based design takes the “move heavy tenants” idea and makes it the structure. Systems are split into autonomous, repeatable failure domains called cells, and a routing layer assigns traffic to a cell based on tenant identity. The same source warns about the costs: cell architectures need careful management to avoid router fatigue and cross-cell dependencies.

GitLab’s public design for its Cells program is a concrete example. The goals document explains that placing groups of organizations in independently operating infrastructure cells limits noisy-neighbor effects and failure impact, while new cells can be provisioned for horizontal growth.

Cells are not the first step for a product with fifty tenants. They become attractive when one database or one region is the thing keeping you up at night, or when enterprise customers ask for contained failure domains and regional placement.

Perspective: what we see when teams scale a SaaS platform

In our work on platform and enterprise systems, the costliest multi-tenancy mistakes are rarely about the model chosen. They are about decisions made implicitly. A team starts with a pool because it is fast, never adds tenant context to background jobs, and two years later a single enterprise deal requires dedicated data residency that the codebase cannot express.

Our view is this:

  • Design for a tenant-aware seam from the first sprint, even if every tenant lands on the same database. Tenant ID in every request context, log line and job payload costs little on day one and a great deal on day 500.
  • Keep the isolation model a configuration, not a fork. A tenant should be movable from pool to dedicated database through a runbook, not a rewrite.
  • Treat noisy-neighbor controls as a product feature. Quotas and fair-use limits are part of the plan you sell.
  • Put the architecture decisions in writing, and revisit them when your customer mix shifts from SMB toward enterprise.
  • Invest in quality engineering and DevOps alongside the architecture. Cross-tenant tests, migration automation and per-tenant observability are what make the design real.

Scaling for customers in Poland: GDPR and security duties

If your tenants include Polish or other EU organizations processing personal data, you are usually their processor, and the architecture has to support that role.

  • Processor contract. GDPR Article 28 requires processing by a processor on behalf of a controller to be governed by a binding contract detailing the nature, scope and duration of processing. Your tenancy design should let you state clearly where each tenant’s data lives and who can reach it.
  • Risk analysis and security. Poland’s data protection authority, UODO, fined a medical company PLN 1,440,549 after a breach. It highlighted the importance of risk analysis, appropriate technical and organizational measures and secure cloud management, and found that a poorly secured cloud platform and outdated, unsupported server software may have contributed.
  • Breach notification. Under the European data protection guidelines hosted by UODO, controllers must assess breach risk and notify the supervisory authority without undue delay and within 72 hours where feasible, unless the breach is unlikely to risk individuals’ rights and freedoms. They must also inform affected individuals when the risk is likely high.

The last point has an architectural consequence. In a shared pool, you must be able to answer within hours which tenants and which records were affected. Per-tenant audit logs and tenant-tagged telemetry make that possible. Your security and operations teams also need rehearsed procedures, and structured data breach response training is one way to build them before an incident rather than during one.

Tenant-specific encryption keys, regional placement and per-tenant deletion are easier on bridge and silo models, which is another reason to keep the migration path between models open.

How to choose: a short decision framework

Use these questions in your next architecture review:

  1. Who are your next 50 customers? Many similar SMBs favor pool. A handful of regulated enterprises favor bridge or silo.
  2. What do contracts demand? Customer-managed keys, data location and audit rights push toward more isolation.
  3. How uneven is usage? If your top tenant is 100 times your median, you need quotas, workload separation and a plan to relocate them.
  4. How much customization will you sell? Prefer configuration and extension points over per-customer code branches.
  5. What is your team’s capacity? More deployments mean more operational load. Staffing and delivery model questions are covered in our guides on scaling engineering teams for SaaS and IT outsourcing versus staff leasing.

FAQ

What is the difference between single-tenant and multi-tenant SaaS?

In single-tenant SaaS, each customer has a dedicated instance of the application and often its own database. In multi-tenant SaaS, customers share some or all components, with logical or physical controls separating their data. Multi-tenant is generally cheaper to run and easier to update, while single-tenant gives stronger isolation at a higher price.

Is multi-tenant SaaS less secure?

Not inherently, but it places more weight on your isolation controls. Security depends on mechanisms such as row-level security, tenant-scoped identity and cross-tenant testing, applied outside individual developers’ code. Customers with strict requirements, such as their own encryption keys, may need a more isolated tier.

Can one product use more than one tenancy model?

Yes, and many do. A common setup is a pool for standard customers and a dedicated schema or database for premium or regulated ones. The key is to make the model a per-tenant configuration so a customer can be moved without rewriting the application.

How do you stop one tenant from slowing down the others?

Measure usage per tenant, enforce quotas and rate limits, separate heavy workloads from transactional paths, and migrate dominant tenants to dedicated capacity. At larger scale, cell-based architecture contains the impact of a spike or failure to one cell.

Does GDPR change how I design a multi-tenant platform?

It affects the design when you process personal data for EU customers. You need to support processor contracts under Article 28, document security measures based on risk analysis, and be able to identify affected tenants quickly to meet the 72-hour breach notification expectation.

Coding Dojo co-founder unveils ‘Idea to Launch,’ a 30-day program to help aspiring founders build with AI

Richard Wang says he gets inspiration from mentoring aspiring startup founders. (Idea to Launch Photo)

Richard Wang, co-founder and former CEO of coding bootcamp Coding Dojo, is launching Idea to Launch, a 30-day program that helps would-be founders turn a startup idea into a working product using AI coding tools and hands-on engineering help.

Nearly four years after selling Coding Dojo to Perdoceo for $70 million, Wang was looking for a way to keep doing work that had a measurable impact on people’s lives. He found it in mentoring aspiring founders.

Wang thinks AI is about to do for software what smartphones did for video. Cheap cameras and cloud platforms gave rise to the influencer economy, and he believes AI will push the cost of building a product close to zero.

“We’re going to create this whole new builder economy that’s about to come in the next three to five years,” he said. “Baby boomers are gifted with cheap housing. I think Gen Z is going to be gifted with a cheap price to build.”

He sees plenty of demand. Citing a Shopify-Gallup study and a Zapier/Harris poll, Idea to Launch says 60% of Americans have a business idea but only 9% launch one. Wang said the reasons people hold back include fear of failure, not knowing how to get started, and not wanting to give up a paycheck or benefits.

Unlike accelerators such as Techstars or Y Combinator, Idea to Launch doesn’t take equity, and participants don’t have to quit their jobs. Over 10 evening sessions, founders get coaching, run customer interviews, build a product with AI tools such as Lovable, Replit and Cursor, and plan a launch. Each founder also gets 10 hours of one-on-one help from professional engineers.

Wang said people without technical backgrounds can now vibe-code 80% to 90% of a product on their own. The hard part, he said, is connecting it to other services, setting up the database correctly and making it secure.

“We’ll provide that type of knowledge with real engineering support,” he said. For engineers who have never done customer discovery or marketing, the program fills in the business side.

Security researchers have found serious vulnerabilities in apps built on vibe-coding platforms. A 2025 scan found 170 of 1,645 apps built with Lovable exposing user data, and another case this year exposed nearly 19,000 user records. Wang said the program’s engineers will make sure security holes are fixed “before they launch or before they get onto the first customer.”

The first cohort starts Jan. 11, followed by three more through April. Each is capped at 15 to 20 founders. Tuition is $3,500 for the inaugural cohort, down from the normal $5,000. Sessions will be held in person in Seattle or Bellevue, and Wang said he expects to pick a space within the next month. He is self-funding the venture.

Wang, who immigrated from China at 13 and was the first in his family to attend college, became Coding Dojo’s CEO in 2015. He left in 2017 for Trupanion and returned in 2019. The company grew to $50 million in peak annual revenue, according to Wang, and had 194 employees at the time of the sale, according to GeekWire’s earlier reporting. It operated campuses in several states before shifting online during the pandemic.

Wang sees Idea to Launch expanding to other tech hubs, then online, and eventually licensing the program globally, much as Coding Dojo grew to multiple campuses. If the first cohorts go well, he can envision a follow-up program, Launch to Traction, to help founders find more customers.

He doesn’t plan to invest in participants’ companies. Having spent years building and selling Coding Dojo, Wang said he knows how hard that road is and would rather “cheer them on from the sidelines.” He said taking VC money at Coding Dojo created pressure to grow 100% a year, something he calls “startup steroids.”

For Wang, the point isn’t to find the next unicorn. It’s to give more people a shot at building something now that AI has made it cheaper and easier.

“I think everyone should try at least once just to bring their idea to reality,” he said.

It's how a team makes uncertainty visible early, limits the cost of change, and proves a system is ready to operate.

For enterprise teams, speed only matters when the software remains stable, secure, and maintainable. The practical test is not whether a team can produce code quickly. It's whether every stage leaves behind evidence that lets the next owner make a sound decision.

Why Process Decides Custom Project Outcomes

The 1994 CHAOS results distinguish between successful projects, challenged projects, and projects that were canceled or delivered but never used. The Standish Group's historical summary reports that 52.7% were challenged, meaning they finished late, exceeded budget, or delivered reduced functionality; 31.1% were canceled or never used. The figures describe a historical cohort, not a prediction for any particular project, but they show why “we'll sort it out as we build” is a risky operating plan.

Project size also changes the level of control a team needs. In the 2009 CHAOS Summary, overall success was 32%. Projects requiring less than $750,000 in labor had a reported 71% chance of success, compared with 38% for projects costing $750,000 to $3 million. For projects exceeding $10 million, the reported chance of finishing on time and within budget was 2%. Those historical figures don't mean a large initiative is doomed; they show that growing scope and organizational complexity need stronger controls.

A large custom system behaves less like a single feature and more like a hospital wing. Integrations, business rules, data ownership, and stakeholder decisions create dependencies that a bigger team can't absorb. Discovery reduces unknowns before significant build spend, architecture limits complexity, iterative delivery surfaces risk while it's still manageable, and release governance helps keep a technically complete system usable.

Seven Stages and the Gate for Each

A stage is complete when it produces an artifact someone can inspect and a named owner accepts the decision. The lifecycle below is a connected operating system, not a one-way waterfall: feedback and new evidence can send work back through an earlier gate. For a complementary overview of how product teams define lifecycle stages, see this product lifecycle stages guide from Figr.

Stage Purpose Exit artifact Gate to pass Discovery Reduce uncertainty before build spend Problem statement, metrics, constraints, first-release scope Sponsor accepts outcomes and out-of-scope items Architecture Control complexity as scope grows Context diagram and recorded decisions Interfaces, data, and failure modes are named Development Deliver increments with traceable change Reviewed code in version control and a green pipeline Each increment maps to an accepted story or decision QA Find costly defects before release Test evidence for function, integration, performance, and security Acceptance criteria pass or risks are explicitly waived Deployment Release the same build repeatedly Pipeline, environment configuration, and rollback plan Release owner can roll forward or back Maintenance Keep the system safe and changeable Runbooks, SLOs, patch and incident cadence On-call ownership and debt capacity are funded Governance Keep decisions, AI use, and risk auditable Decision log, provenance, and approval record Named approver signs scope, security, and AI-assisted changes

Think of gates as air-traffic control. Coding is the flight; gates are the clearances. Skipping a clearance doesn't make the flight faster. It leaves the team trying to move forward without confirming the route, the handoff, or the conditions for landing.

Use rule: Don't start the next stage until its exit artifact exists and a named owner has accepted the gate.

Governance runs across the stages, not only at the end. The sponsor approves outcomes and scope; technical and operational owners record decisions and confirm that risks have been addressed. In regulated healthcare, education, and legal work, each gate should carry the relevant security and audit evidence. A final-stage compliance check can't reconstruct decisions that were never recorded.

Discovery and Architecture Before Coding

Discovery and architecture are investment controls. They turn a broad goal into a bounded first release, then decide how that release can evolve without making the next change unnecessarily risky. Teams that need a structured way to define the work can also use this product discovery process guide.

Rather than collecting an undifferentiated requirements list, run four working sessions:

  1. Name the outcome and proof. Choose a business result and a measure that can show progress, such as cycle time, error rate, or release frequency. If the outcome can't be observed, the sponsor can't reliably decide whether the software is helping.

  2. Map the people and rules. Identify users, downstream systems, and the business rules those systems already enforce. A workflow that appears simple on screen may rely on identity, approval, or data-retention rules elsewhere.

  3. Bound the first release. Separate must-have behavior from later options. Make non-goals explicit so a team can say whether a new request changes the agreed scope.

  4. Record immovable constraints. Document data residency, uptime expectations, identity requirements, and the budget band. Treat these as design inputs, not issues to discover after architecture has hardened.

The discovery exit should be a concise scope document with outcomes, constraints, in-scope behavior, and non-goals. It doesn't need to predict every future feature. It needs to give the sponsor and delivery team the same basis for deciding what comes first.

Make architecture decisions reversible where possible

Architecture decides how the scope can change. Record major decisions with their context, the options considered, and the consequences of reversing them. A context diagram should show systems, users, data flows, and the interfaces where ownership or failure could affect the rest of the product.

For legacy platforms, inventory current behavior before choosing whether to rewrite or migrate. List undocumented rules that users and integrations still rely on, then identify seams around those capabilities and keep a rollback path. It's like moving a load-bearing wall: shore the structure, move one opening, and check the result before going further.

Discovery checklist: Leave the stage with an outcome metric, bounded scope, integration map, threat and data notes, and the first recorded architecture decisions.

Build Test and Release on Measured Quality

A defect becomes more expensive when a team finds it late, after dependent components or business processes have been built around it. Independent software-quality research summarized by the National Institute of Standards and Technology found that approximately 45% of software errors were discovered during integration, after components had been created separately. The same findings reported that correcting a defect introduced through poor requirements or coding could cost about twice as much when found during integration, three times as much during testing, and up to six times as much after release.

Treat development, QA, and deployment as one quality system. Developers merge small changes; the pipeline builds the software and runs unit, integration, and dependency checks. QA adds acceptance and performance evidence for the riskiest paths, while release owners promote the same tested artifact and confirm that rollback is ready. A practical QA checklist can help teams make acceptance evidence explicit rather than leaving it to a late bug hunt.

Track throughput and stability together

The 2024 DORA framework describes delivery through change lead time, deployment frequency, and failed-deployment recovery time, alongside stability measures including change-fail rate and deployment rework rate. DORA's metric definitions make the relationship concrete: change lead time runs from code commit to successful production deployment, while change-fail rate counts deployments that require immediate intervention. Deployment rework rate captures deployments spent on unplanned defect fixes.

Calculate change-fail rate as failed deployments divided by total deployments in the same period, multiplied by 100. A team whose deployment needs a hotfix or rollback has a failed deployment for this measure. The following benchmarks are the bands in DORA's metrics history, summarized by IBM:

Band Change failure rate What it means Example Elite 0–15% Few releases need immediate repair 4 failures in 40 releases is 10% High 16–30% Stable enough to improve without freezing releases 8 failures in 40 releases is 20% Medium 31–45% Speed is creating noticeable production repair 16 failures in 40 releases is 40% Low 46–60% Nearly half of releases need a hotfix or rollback 20 failures in 40 releases is 50%

An internal developer platform isn't a delivery metric by itself. The 2024 DORA report says 89% of surveyed organizations use one, but platform adoption alone doesn't guarantee better outcomes. Teams with dedicated platform support showed a 6% productivity gain, while organizations requiring platform use across the application lifecycle experienced an 8% throughput decrease. Instrument the pipeline and incident systems, set baselines, then choose improvement targets. Don't accept a release-frequency goal that improves throughput by increasing failures and corrective work.

Security Gates and AI Code Governance

A security gate is useful only when it produces evidence a team can review and act on. NIST's Secure Software Development Framework groups practices into four areas: Prepare the Organization, Protect the Software, Produce Well-Secured Software, and Respond to Vulnerabilities. The framework fits into an existing lifecycle, so teams can connect its practices to work they already do.

Turn those areas into controls at each stage. During discovery, document threat assumptions and acceptance criteria for authentication, authorization, data minimization, encryption, auditability, retention, and recovery. During implementation, protect repositories, dependencies, and build reproducibility, then run static, dynamic, and dependency analysis in the pipeline. After release, assign owners for vulnerability disclosure and patching, and track whether fixes prevent root causes from recurring. NIST's SSDF publication describes practices for reducing vulnerabilities in released software, limiting the impact of weaknesses that escape detection, and addressing recurring causes.

Treat AI-assisted code as governed change

AI tools don't remove the need for a delivery gate. They increase the need for traceability: teams should record which code, tests, documentation, or infrastructure definitions were AI-assisted, who reviewed them, and what evidence supports approval.

A 2026 GitLab survey of 1,528 developers and technology buyers across six countries found that 80% said their organizations adopted AI coding tools faster than they created governance policies, and 92% reported governance challenges involving AI-generated code. AI coding assistants are in widespread use, while governance and oversight remain uneven.

Require an approval record before AI-assisted changes enter operation. Keep the record tool-agnostic and include:

  • Provenance: Record the assistant or model, the prompt class, and which artifacts were AI-assisted.
  • Human review: Apply closer scrutiny to changes affecting permissions, sensitive data, regulated workflows, or core business rules.
  • Supply-chain checks: Review dependency origin and licensing, and protect source repositories and build inputs.
  • Regression evidence: Run relevant functional, integration, and security checks against generated changes.
  • Approval: Store the reviewer's decision with release evidence, not in a disconnected chat thread.

For sensitive systems, set the evidence required to approve, operate, and maintain AI-assisted software before choosing how quickly an assistant should draft an MVP. A tool-agnostic AI governance framework can help teams turn that requirement into reviewable controls.

Adoption does not establish trust. Google's 2025 DORA research found that 90% of technology professionals reported using AI at work. DORA's analysis of trust in AI found that 30% of developers reported little or no trust in AI-generated code. JetBrains' 2025 developer experience and productivity research found that 85% of developers reported using at least one AI tool for coding or other development activities. Approval criteria should account for both widespread use and the need for accountable review.

Maintenance Debt and a Safe Modernization

A 2025 empirical study analyzed 774,051 methods across 49 open-source projects and found that methods containing self-admitted technical debt were larger, more complex, less readable, more bug-prone, and changed more frequently; the debt also often remained unresolved for extended periods, according to the study published in the proceedings of the International Conference on Mining Software Repositories. That makes debt an operating cost, not a matter of developer taste or a cleanup task that can safely wait until after launch.

Build a debt baseline during discovery. Classify each item by its effect on security, reliability, compliance, or changeability, then assign an owner and a checkpoint. Fund remediation capacity within delivery increments instead of creating a future cleanup project with no protected time. Use architecture decision records, automated tests, and production telemetry to confirm whether a debt item is resolved.

Modernize by bounded capability

Consider a regulated team running a document-heavy platform that still handles billing and access rules. Users and downstream integrations depend on behavior that the original documentation doesn't describe. Replacing the whole platform at once might remove visible complexity while putting critical business rules at risk.

A safer approach treats modernization as a controlled learning system:

  1. Inventory observed behavior. Document what users do, which systems exchange data, and where the current platform makes decisions.
  2. Instrument the existing path. Collect enough operational evidence to identify how the capability is used and what a replacement must preserve.
  3. Introduce a seam. Separate one bounded capability from the rest of the platform without changing its externally visible behavior.
  4. Migrate and compare. Run the new path against the intended workflow, check results, and keep the old path available while the team validates the change.
  5. Pass the checkpoint or roll back. Move forward only when the new capability meets its acceptance criteria; otherwise restore the prior path and address the gap.

Modernization is safer when each release teaches the team something without removing its ability to recover.

Maintenance then follows the same discipline at a smaller scale. Incidents update runbooks; security findings enter the patch and vulnerability-response process; delivery metrics show whether changes are improving without creating more rework. Keep a debt register with an owner, impact class, and checkpoint, not a backlog category called “cleanup.”

Roles, Cadence and Engagement Model

Name the person who accepts each gate before the first iteration. The sponsor owns outcomes, the budget band, and first-release scope; the product owner orders stories and accepts increments. The architect maintains the decision log, engineers own pipeline health and rework, QA or security leads provide acceptance evidence, and operations owns on-call readiness, runbooks, and rollback.

Use short iterations with a demo and acceptance review each cycle. Hold a monthly sponsor gate review and a quarterly review of the debt register and DORA trend. The value of a meeting is whether it resolves an ownership or risk decision, not whether it appears on the calendar.

Practical rule: Put the acceptance artifacts, signer, and response to a failed gate in the contract.

Fixed-price contracts fit a bounded first release with stable scope. Discovery-heavy or legacy work usually fits time-and-materials or staged contracts better, because the team learns about requirements and dependencies as delivery progresses. Internal teams and partners need the same named ownership. If you're evaluating distributed staffing, HireDevelopers.com's resource for hiring developers from Latin America can be one place to explore team options; the engagement still needs clear gate owners on your side.

Decision and Two-Week Start

Proceed only when the outcome owner has accepted the first-release scope and non-goals, the riskiest integration has a recorded architecture decision and rollback path, and the team can measure lead time, deployment frequency, change-fail rate, recovery time, and rework. Security criteria and AI provenance rules must travel through the same approval path as functional stories. If any of those controls is missing, pause. Adding developers won't create the missing decisions or evidence.

In week one, baseline the outcome metric, map integrations, open the debt register, and name an owner for each gate. In week two, set the first gate review and release dashboard, then choose one capability small enough to deploy and roll back. For unfamiliar players, wuxia is more than “Chinese sword fighting.” It is a tradition of roaming martial artists, moral debts, revenge, impossible physical skill, and a society outside ordinary law.

Phantom Blade Zero leans into that cinematic language: rain-soaked duels, airborne strikes, rival masters, haunted landscapes, and a hero caught between loyalty and vengeance.

Screenshot from a gameplay teaser trailer.

But S-Game gives the tradition a harsher, stranger silhouette. The studio calls its blend of classic martial arts, folk horror, fantasy, and mechanical prosthetics “Kungfupunk.”

Screenshot from a gameplay teaser trailer.

The result looks less like historical reconstruction than a stylized martial-arts fever dream—one where a swordsman may face masked assassins, supernatural threats, or machines grafted onto human bodies.

Fast Combat, Not Just Hard Combat

The game’s appeal is likely to rest on its action. Soul can collect more than 30 main weapons and a wide set of secondary “Phantom Edges,” with upgrades, accessories, summons, dodges, counters, and weapon-switching encouraging different builds.

Side quests can open new story routes, while several difficulty settings aim to make the experience accessible beyond the most demanding action-game audience.

Hong Kong kung fu star Donnie Yen, known internationally for the Ip Man films, Rogue One: A Star Wars Story, and John Wick: Chapter 4, served as creative consultant and performed facial and motion capture for Mó Yuan, Soul’s father.

That distinction matters. Though its bosses and checkpoints invite comparisons to Soulslikes, reviews published before launch, including those from GamesRadar and Game Informer, describe a more direct, combo-driven game with adjustable challenge rather than one built around repeated punishment.

Could This Be China’s Next Global Breakout?

The international response before launch has been emphatic. Phantom Blade Zero opened with an 88 Metacritic average and a 91 OpenCritic average. Game Informer awarded it 9.5/10, praising its combat and wuxia scale, while also judging the story less convincing.

TechRadar likewise highlighted the action and atmosphere but noted that its fragmented, terminology-heavy narrative can be difficult to follow.

Although strong reviews alone do not make a Black Myth: Wukong-scale hit, Phantom Blade Zero’s distinctive wuxia world and polished action give it a credible shot at becoming China’s next global breakout.

Google has officially launched Google Wallet, including Google Pay, in Nigeria, giving Android users across the country a new way to make fast, convenient, and secure digital payments. Announced on October 28, 2026, the rollout allows eligible users to add supported bank cards to their digital wallets and pay for purchases using compatible smartphones and Wear OS smartwatches.

The launch marks another milestone for Nigeria’s growing fintech industry as digital banking, mobile payments, and cashless transactions continue to reshape how consumers and businesses exchange money. With Google Wallet, Nigerians can make contactless payments at supported payment terminals, complete online purchases, and store essential digital items in one place.

The service also expands Google’s digital payment ecosystem in Africa, providing Nigerian consumers with access to technology designed to simplify everyday transactions while adding security features to mobile payments.

Google Wallet Arrives in Nigeria With Contactless Payments

The introduction of Google Wallet in Nigeria allows eligible Android users to make payments without carrying physical bank cards. After adding a supported payment card to the Google Wallet app, users can tap their compatible smartphones against contactless payment terminals to complete transactions at participating merchants.

Google Pay also supports payments on participating websites and mobile applications, giving Nigerians another option for online shopping, subscriptions, travel bookings, and other digital purchases. However, users can only access these features when their bank, card, device, and merchant support the service.

At launch, Google’s official list includes eligible Mastercard credit, debit, and prepaid cards from participating Nigerian banks. Supported options include FirstBank Mastercard debit cards, GTBank Mastercard debit and prepaid cards, UBA Mastercard credit, debit and prepaid cards, and Zenith Bank Mastercard credit, debit, and prepaid cards. UBA also supports an eligible Visa prepaid card.

However, not every Nigerian bank card works with Google Wallet yet. Customers should check Google’s official list of supported payment methods before attempting to add their cards to the application.

Google Pay Brings Security and Convenience to Digital Payments

Security remains a central feature of Google Wallet, which uses multiple protective measures to help safeguard users’ financial information during digital transactions.

Google Pay uses tokenisation technology to protect card details. Instead of sharing the actual card number with merchants during supported transactions, the payment system uses a digital token. This approach helps reduce exposure of sensitive financial information when customers make purchases online or at contactless payment terminals.

Google Wallet also offers more than payment functionality. Users can keep loyalty cards, event tickets, and boarding passes in the application, making it easier to access frequently used items from compatible Android devices.

Additionally, Google recommends using its Find My Device service to secure a missing or stolen phone. Users can remotely lock their devices, change their passwords, or erase personal information when necessary.

These features could make mobile payments more attractive to Nigerian consumers seeking convenient alternatives to physical cards and cash. Nevertheless, users should continue protecting their devices, securing their Google accounts, and monitoring their bank transactions for suspicious activity.

Contactless Payment Adoption Remains a Challenge in Nigeria

Despite the launch, the availability of Google Wallet does not automatically mean every Nigerian merchant can accept smartphone payments.

For in store transactions, customers need a compatible Android smartphone with near field communication (NFC), while merchants must have payment terminals that support contactless transactions. Without the necessary hardware and payment infrastructure, customers cannot complete tap to pay purchases at those locations.

Nigeria’s contactless payment infrastructure remains a significant challenge for widespread adoption. According to reporting by Techpoint Africa, contactless transactions account for a relatively small share of payments in the country, while many point of sale terminals lack the necessary capabilities.

Online payments could offer a more immediate opportunity for adoption because customers can use Google Pay at participating websites and applications without relying on physical contactless terminals.

The launch also creates opportunities for Nigerian banks, payment processors, and merchants to expand their digital payment services. As more financial institutions support the platform and more businesses enable contactless payments, Google Wallet could become an increasingly useful option for everyday transactions.

For now, Nigerian users should download Google Wallet from the Google Play Store, check whether their bank cards qualify, and confirm that their devices meet the requirements. The service’s long term success will depend on wider bank support, merchant acceptance, and improvements to Nigeria’s contactless payment infrastructure.
