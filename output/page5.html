Elvira Dyangani Ose Wants Zeitz MOCAA to Belong to Its City

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At the start of this month, Elvira Dyangani Ose assumed the title of artistic director at the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa in Cape Town. The institution is the largest museum on the continent devoted to contemporary art from Africa and its diaspora, but has been in the public eye lately for another reason. Koyo Kouoh, its executive director and chief curator since 2019, died suddenly of cancer in May 2025 at the age of 57. The loss was felt across the art world because at the time Kouoh was also serving as curator of the 2026 Venice Biennale.

Kouoh had put her stamp on Zeitz MOCAA, which opened in Sept. 2017 in a grain silo complex on the V&A Waterfront redesigned by Thomas Heatherwick of Hudson Yards infamy. The founding collection came from Jochen Zeitz, the former Puma chief executive, who co-chairs the board with V&A Waterfront CEO David Green.

Dyangani Ose comes to Cape Town from the Museu d’Art Contemporani de Barcelona, where she served as director from 2021 until this year. Born in Córdoba to a family of Equatorial Guinean descent, she has held curatorial roles at Tate Modern, where she helped develop the museum’s first strategy for collecting art from Africa and its diaspora; Creative Time in New York; the Centro Andaluz de Arte Contemporáneo in Seville; and the Centro Atlántico de Arte Moderno in Las Palmas. She directed the Showroom in London, curated the 2015 Göteborg International Biennial and the 2013 Lubumbashi Biennial. She was one of the curators of “Project a Black Planet: The Art and Culture of Panafrica” at the Art Institute of Chicago.

We caught up with Dyangani Ose to talk about Kouoh’s legacy, what she learned in Barcelona and why she thinks of Cape Town as an argument rather than a city.

Koyo Kouoh is credited with bringing a new vision to Zeitz MOCAA and built programs like a residency for Cape Town artists. Which parts of her program do you view to be essential to preserving, for the museum and her legacy?

Thank you. Koyo did something that sounds simple and is, in fact, extraordinarily difficult: she began to anchor the museum in the reality of Cape Town. She used to say that when she arrived, the institution had not yet landed in the city, and she devoted six years to changing that. The Koyo Kouoh Atelier is perhaps the clearest expression of it: an artist residency inside the galleries, where artists who live and work in Cape Town experiment in front of the public, and where visitors witness art in the making rather than only its results. I consider that essential. So is everything that surrounds it: the BMW Centre for Art Education and its soon-to-be-launched MOCAA On the Move, a purpose-built mobile art classroom; the Pan-African Zeitz MOCAA & University of the Western Cape (UWC) Museum Fellowship Programme; the symposia; the long-term research behind projects such as “When We See Us: A Century of Black Figuration in Painting”; and her insistence on monographic depth, on giving artists like Tracey Rose, whom I had the privilege of working with in Gran Canaria 20 years ago, the retrospectives they deserved.

The programs tying the museum to the local reality of Cape Town are the ones I hope to give wings to. My way of working is both hyper-local and connected to an intercontinental reality: through shared methodologies, and through the formulation of common imaginaries. A legacy is not something one preserves under glass. Koyo understood building institutions on the continent as a matter of urgency, and to honor her is to keep that urgency alive. For me, an institution’s legacy is measured not in individual exhibitions or annual reports, but in the artists it sustains, the publics it forms and the next generation of practitioners and professionals it cultivates.

That is also why my first months are, above all, a time for listening. An institution needs time to be understood, and I want to learn from the people who sustain it every day, from its critics and, just as much, from those who feel it has nothing to say to them. They may not hold the answers, but looking for those answers with them, assuming nothing, is what anchors the work in its place.

Kouoh appointed you to her jury for the Venice Biennale. What was it like to work with her? Did you talk about the museum?

Although I learned about the jury appointment long after her passing, Koyo and I shared almost 20 years of conversations, and her invitation was, in many ways, a continuation of that dialogue. The last time we spoke in person was in Chicago, where she was one of our speakers at the Art Institute for a symposium linked to “Project a Black Planet: The Art and Culture of Panafrica.” We spoke, as we often did, about the people and the infrastructures that have shaped our field: about Bisi Silva and what ÀSÌKÒ and the Centre for Contemporary Art, Lagos, meant for so many of us, about N’Goné Fall, about a new generation of colleagues such as Marie-Hélène Pereira and about the opportunities for capacity building on the continent.

And of course we spoke about Zeitz MOCAA, which I knew and visited from its inception, although I did not attend while she led it, but which was very present in our exchanges. Koyo was building something there that went beyond exhibitions: a way of making the institution belong to its city. I don’t take it as an inheritance to administer, but as a conversation to continue and fortify from the perspectives inherent to my practice.

At MACBA you were the first woman and first person of color to lead the museum since its founding in 1995. What are your proudest accomplishments from those years?

We restored the museum’s connection with the city. MACBA had, at times, been perceived as an ivory tower, a building that looked outward while turning its back on its neighborhood. We worked to make it porous, open to the Raval and to the city, to the communities who perhaps never dare to visit it and who, in the present, make it, produce it. Fostering that community also meant rethinking how it addresses its publics, establishing the museum’s first-ever development department and strategy, and recognizing philanthropic collaboration as a tool to transform a 30-year-old institution beyond its current governance. What I sought after was not only visibility, but rootedness: the slow, difficult work of making an institution genuinely accountable to the communities it claims to serve. Insisting on better communication with our publics, especially younger ones. In Barcelona we never managed to create a Youth Council.

Furthermore, we presented a program of international interest and relevance under economic conditions that were insufficient for what was asked of the museum; not only to return to pre-pandemic audience levels, but importantly, to care for its own history, while offering a critical and generous gaze toward an international audience that experiences a Barcelona—a culture much more diverse and contemporary—in real life, which was not reflected in its programs. Projects such as “Song for Many Movements: Scenes of Collective Creation” and “Project a Black Planet: The Art and Culture of Panafrica” showed the range of what the institution could do. And, perhaps more importantly, we held on despite the difficulties, without needing to underline those limitations at every step.

I believe the work directors do to sustain an institution’s infrastructure and team morale, support their material conditions and accompany them as their practices become more specialized—what Charles Esche once called the “back office”—is fundamental, and we should talk about it more. There was still much to do overall; at the CIMAM, for instance, we hope to contribute to a better understanding of this role. We advocate for public museums like MACBA to have independent, more professional governance, giving their directors more autonomy to operate. However committed a director may be, we work like software on hardware that does not always yield, conceptually or materially; programming alone does not change an institution. All of this generates a better working culture, and a form of capacity building that starts in the museum and, progressively, seeks to influence the sector as a whole. That is part of what I bring with me to Zeitz MOCAA.

You were born in Córdoba to a family of Equatorial Guinean descent. Most of your career has been in Europe and New York. How does that unique vantage point shape how you’ll lead a museum on the continent?

I would gently correct the premise. Much of my work and research has taken place on the continent or in dialogue with it, particularly during my time at Tate Modern, where I helped create Tate’s first Africa Strategy for the acquisition of modern and contemporary art of Africa and its diaspora, and led the trans-local program, “Across the Board,” which connected Tate’s audiences with artists, colleagues and institutions from all over the continent with people like the late beloved Bisi Silva, Ntone Edjabe, Senam Okudzeto and others with whom I devised a series of public programs and research projects in Accra, Douala and Lagos. Before and after that period, I have collaborated with Marilyn Douala-Bell and the Salon Urbain de Douala; I curated the Rencontres Picha in Lubumbashi with artist Sammy Baloji and many others, and conducted my doctoral research on artists’ collectives in Dakar, Lagos and Cape Town, a city I first came to research in 2005.

Still, the vantage point matters. As a Black scholar and curator, a post-colonial subject and an African born and raised in Spain, I became aware early of the power implicit in binary narratives, us and them, here and there, and of the institutions that consolidate that power. In the work of artists, writers and thinkers from Africa and its diaspora, I found poetic and political responses to those concerns, and a notion of togetherness that subverts that rhetoric. That is how I understand Pan-Africanism: not as an origin one returns to, but as a living practice, made of networks, solidarities and translations, including those of Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking Africa, which the art world so often forgets. As “Project a Black Planet” set out to show, Pan-Africanism has never been solely a political, literary or philosophical project; it has also been a proposal of artistic imagination, a century-long practice of solidarity across geographies and temporalities.

I find myself increasingly interested in conversations among contexts and communities of the Global South that no longer route through Europe, at least not in the way they did in the late 20th Century, and in local histories that, studied closely, reveal their ties to many others worldwide. I do not come to lead from outside. I come to listen to a city and a community of artists, South Africans, Panafricans and Panhumanists, who already know what they need, and to place the institution at their service.

At Tate Modern you helped develop the institution’s strategy for collecting art from Africa and its diaspora. How should other museums orient themselves toward this material?

Working with Tate’s Africa Acquisitions Committee taught me that collecting is not mere acquisition; it is a commitment to the context and aesthetic environments from which those artworks come, to their histories, and to their agents. Collecting demands research, long-term relationships with artists and their communities, and the humility to recognize that many of these histories were written by or in artists’ collectives, journals, workshops and ephemeral platforms, not only by the objects themselves.

Too often, museums treat this material as an emergency to manage: a temporary exhibition here, a public program there, a more diverse surface over a structure that stays the same. An intervention that does not touch the structure remains, in the end, just programming. Kerry James Marshall is a key example for me: he realized that placing Black figures within the conventions of history painting was not enough; he had to master that tradition to contest it and change it from within. Museums face the same task. Collecting is structural work, and it begins when those who govern our institutions acknowledge the violence through which some collections were assembled, and the erasures their narratives have sustained.

From there, I would suggest three orientations: sustained commitments to artists across their careers, not single trophies—so to speak (a task that is clearly not the museum’s alone); “collect contexts” as much as works: archives, documents, journals, the infrastructures that made art possible; and to do it in partnership with institutions—no matter their sizes—on the continent, sharing research, loans and authorship, so that knowledge does not travel in one direction only. Zeitz MOCAA, as an institution, its programs and its collection carry that responsibility. It should be a resource for the continent and the world.

You wrote to the New York Times that the museum “is and must continue to be a public institution,” and called Cape Town “a city of contradictions.” Which contradictions do you mean? How do you plan to explore them?

Cape Town is not merely a location; it is an argument, and its contradictions—historical, spatial and political—are themselves a curatorial invitation. Cape Town is a city in which social change has always been written into urban space. The contradictions I mean are those between a city of extraordinary openness and creativity and a city whose geography still tells you who belongs where; between the promise of the public and its fine print. Years ago, while writing with Gabi Ngcobo about Gugulective, we borrowed a phrase that I believe captures the city well: “everyone is welcome, terms and conditions may apply.”

I have been thinking about Cape Town for a long time, and what has always moved me is how its artists and communities have answered those contradictions: the District Six Museum, built from the memories of those who were removed from the city center; the tradition of the cultural workers during the Resistance Art movement; a collective like Gugulective, working from a shebeen in Gugulethu; the implementation of Chimurenga Library and programs in various moments of its trajectory; and the transformation of the artistic context, the proliferation of galleries, auction houses, cultural foundations and scholarly programs to deepen the knowledge of professionals in the field. In my research, the most enduring cultural interventions were never those that announced themselves as monuments; they worked subtly within the fabric of everyday life. Collectivity, in that sense, is not an outcome of cultural work. It is its method.

That is what I mean by a public institution. A museum is public not because of who funds it, but because it assumes responsibility for the program and the institutional space it generates, and for the communities it serves. How an institution is structured is already a curatorial statement, and a public museum must build a vision robust enough to attract the support it deserves. Zeitz MOCAA is a not-for-profit initiative that seeks to be, above all, a public, Pan-Africanist and intergenerational space. That means taking the museum beyond the Waterfront, into the city, but also making sure that communities who do not yet feel addressed by the museum come to feel it as their own, sharing not only the right to change it but also the responsibility for doing so. A museum transforms collectively, from within and from without. The question is not only who visits the museum, but who is able to inhabit it.

Next year marks the 10th anniversary of Zeitz MOCAA. Have you thought about how the institution should mark the event? 

An anniversary is a good moment for an institution to ask itself questions in public. Nine years ago, Zeitz MOCAA opened with an enormous promise. Since then it has lived through a crisis, a profound transformation under Koyo, a pandemic and her loss.

It is still early days—I haven’t had time to talk to the team on the ground as much. However, I keep returning to the idea of belonging, to who the museum belongs to. I think of the museum as an open work, in Umberto Eco’s sense: something evolving and unfinished, which its publics are invited to complete. So I imagine expanding the museum’s own archive to artists, scholars and its publics, and inviting the communities of Cape Town to tell us what the next 10 years should be, in the spirit of a Casa de la Palabra [a house of words], a place where a community gathers in processes of decision-making. I am also interested in convening institutions across the continent, independent spaces, biennials and museums, to think together about how we build and sustain cultural infrastructure, around a question Zeitz MOCAA is singularly placed to ask: what does the continent need from its cultural institutions, and what must those institutions dare to become?

In 2006, at the Centro Atlántico de Arte Moderno, you co-curated a show of 14 emerging South African artists. What has changed most in South Africa’s art scene since that time?

“Olvida quién soy / Erase Me From Who I Am,” which I curated 20 years ago together with Gabi Ngcobo, Tracy Murinik and Khwezi Gule, brought together artists who were then at the beginning of their careers, Nicholas Hlobo, Zanele Muholi, Moshekwa Langa and Tracey Rose among them, and who have since become central to the history of contemporary art. That trajectory is in itself the clearest sign of change: South African artists are no longer guests in the international conversation; they shape it. And so do my co-curators, particularly Gabi Ngcobo, whose work has transformed the field from within.

The infrastructure has changed too: art fairs, galleries, foundations and a museum like this one. But growth brings its own questions. Much of that infrastructure is private and market-driven. At the same time, public institutions have weakened, and many young artists still work without studios, without critical platforms, without the possibility of making a living from their work. What hasn’t changed is the vitality of collectivity: artists organizing themselves and creating their own platforms when institutions fail them. I have spent much of my life learning from those experiences, and from artists who showed me that real transformation begins only when we recognize ourselves as part of something larger. A museum’s responsibility is to learn from them too.

Colin Donnell is returning to Chicago Med.

After leaving the popular NBC drama in 2019, Donnell is back, reprising his role as Dr. Connor Rhodes.

PEOPLE reports that Donnell is returning to the cast for a pair of episodes in season 12, which includes the fall finale, slated to air on Dec. 9. Per PEOPLE, Dr. Rhodes will appear to assist with a complicated heart surgery to be performed on a young patient.

Donnell, 44, was cast in a main role on Chicago Med from the series’ first season until part of the way through the fifth season. He’s been absent from the cast for seven years.

In his time away from the show, Donnell kept busy in front of the screen. His post-Chicago Med TV roles include Love on Iceland, To Catch a Spy, Irreverent, FBI: International, and Zero Day.

Prior to being cast as Dr. Rhodes, Donnell made a name for himself on Broadway, in film, and on the small screen. Jersey Boys and Anything Goes are among the shows he performed on Broadway. He also was part of the cast for the Mamma Mia! tour. His film credits include Every Secret Thing, which starred Elizabeth Banks and Dakota Fanning, and 2019’s Almost Love.

On the small screen, Donnell’s resume includes appearances in Arrow and The Affair before landing the Chicago Med role.

“Dr. Connor Rhodes was the very first character fans met in the opening scene of Chicago Med’s premiere episodes in 2015, “Showrunner Allen MacDonald told PEOPLE. “And he’s one of my absolute favorites in the history of the series, so bringing him back has been an absolute priority for me since I joined the show. I’m so thrilled that Colin Donnel has agreed to return his iconic role.”

New episodes of Chicago Med air on Wednesday nights on NBC.

Noetix’s RMB9,998 Humanoid Is Just the Start

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editor’s note: This article is republished from Jumpstart, a TechNode Group publication.

For RMB9,998, Noetix Robotics offered buyers something that had usually belonged to research laboratories, corporate demonstrations and expensive technology showcases: a humanoid robot they could consider buying for themselves.

Bumi, the small robot the Chinese company introduced in October 2025, was aimed at programming education and companionship. Its appeal rested on an immediate proposition. Children could programme its movements, watch it dance and interact with a machine that looked and moved like a small person.

That proposition also expressed a larger choice about how to build a robotics company. Noetix would first make the body affordable and capable enough to sell. More sophisticated intelligence would follow.

Three years after its founding, the company is attempting to turn that sequence into a much larger business. It is pursuing higher deliveries, investing in its own embodied-intelligence models and seeking customers overseas. In October, media reports said it had confidentially submitted a Hong Kong listing application, potentially targeting a 2027 flotation.

The question connecting those ambitions is whether the hardware business Noetix built first can provide enough customers, experience and cash to support what comes next.

A Price Decided Before Production

Founder Jiang Zheyuan left his doctoral studies at Tsinghua University in 2023 and established Noetix that September. His early technical priority was the robot’s body and motion control: the machinery and software that allow it to balance, move and respond reliably.

The distinction matters. A robot can execute demanding movements while relying on external models for conversation or higher-level instructions. Building an athletic machine and building one that can work out what to do in an unfamiliar room involve different capabilities.

Jiang’s decision was to address the physical machine first. He explained that, when the company began, he saw unreliable bodies and limited manufacturing readiness as the immediate bottlenecks. AI approaches were also changing rapidly. Noetix concentrated its resources on hardware while waiting for a clearer direction in the intelligence stack.

Low cost was part of that engineering brief. In a March interview, Jiang described using composite materials in Bumi’s body to reduce its dependence on metal structures. The price was being worked into the design, rather than left for a sales team to negotiate after production.

That approach gave the company a product with a readily understood purpose. Parents and schools could buy a programmable humanoid for education and interaction. They did not have to wait for general-purpose domestic labour to become commercially viable.

The narrower use case helped Noetix reach buyers sooner. It also established the next commercial task: giving those buyers enough continuing value to make the robot more than an exciting first purchase.

Attention Becomes a Manufacturing Problem

Noetix’s machines found a powerful route to public attention through performance. Its N2 robot finished second in the 2025 Beijing humanoid half-marathon. In 2026, the company’s robots appeared alongside comedian Cai Ming in a sketch on China’s Spring Festival Gala.

The exposure connected technical demonstrations with a mass audience. A robot that families had seen on television was also available through retail channels.

At the AWE exhibition in March, Jiang said orders had surged after the gala and set a target of more than 10,000 Bumi sales during 2026. The company was moving from proving that people would notice its robots to showing that it could deliver them consistently.

Jiang’s account of the obstacles was strikingly practical. He identified component availability and production-line management as central challenges. A missing part can stop an entire machine from being completed. As monthly output rises, the company must train and manage a larger workforce while maintaining assembly quality.

These are consequential problems for a company whose market entry depends on affordability. Every reworked unit, delayed delivery and service visit consumes part of the margin that a low selling price leaves available.

The manufacturing target therefore carries more meaning than a shipment milestone. It tests whether Noetix can preserve the economics designed into Bumi while producing and supporting it in much larger numbers.

The Brain Becomes the Expensive Part

By September, Noetix was making its next technical commitment explicit. It launched the embodied-intelligence sub-brand Scalabot and the HERON world-model architecture, extending its ambitions into how robots understand environments and learn to act within them.

The company had begun assembling a dedicated intelligence team in July 2025, according to Jiang’s September interview with Caijing. The work added a substantial research programme to an organisation already scaling physical production.

His logic connects the two activities. Cheaper, reliable machines can be deployed more widely; those deployments can create opportunities to collect experience for training better models.

Making that connection productive requires another layer of engineering. A robot in a classroom generates experience, but useful training data must still be selected, recorded, organised and connected to tasks the company wants its models to learn. A large installed base creates access to potential data. The collection and training system determines its value.

Jiang told 21st Century Business Herald that intelligence research had become a major area of expenditure. He also described hardware as a potential source of funds for that work.

This is the financial hinge in Noetix’s strategy. The body-first approach opened an earlier route to revenue. The company now wants that operating business to help support a more expensive effort whose commercial returns will take longer to develop.

External capital helps bridge the timing. Its backers include Chendao Capital, an investment platform associated with CATL. A prospective Hong Kong listing would take that financing journey into public markets, where investors would be able to examine more closely how hardware sales and research spending fit together.

Taking a Chinese Starting Point Overseas

Noetix’s domestic route has combined educational demand, accessible hardware and unusually broad public exposure. Its overseas expansion will test how much of that combination travels.

At CES in January, co-founder Zhang Shipu outlined five target regions: North America, Europe, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and Japan and South Korea. He described an ambition to reach thousand-unit scale in overseas expansion during the second quarter, supported by local deployments, customer service and repeatable reference projects.

That programme involves more than shipping robots abroad. Schools need teaching materials and support. Distributors need spare parts and repair arrangements. Families need a reason to keep using the product after its novelty fades.

Noetix has some of those relationships to build from in China. An investor, Beijing Industrial Development Investment Management, reported partnerships with programming-education provider Codemao and retailer Kidswant, alongside deployments and donations involving schools. These connections show how a robot can become part of an existing educational or retail system.

Overseas growth will require comparable connections in markets with different curricula, languages and purchasing habits. The product’s price can win attention, but local usefulness and support will determine whether that attention becomes a durable business.

Noetix’s first move was to make a humanoid affordable enough to enter more classrooms and homes. That choice has given it a product to sell while the broader intelligence problem remains under development.

It is now trying to make that early foothold carry three ambitions at once: larger production volumes, more capable intelligence and international growth. Building the body first won Noetix a place in the market. The next stage is to make that place valuable enough to finance everything it still wants the robot to become.

How Bambu Lab Turned 3D Printing Into a Consumer Hit

Editor’s Note: In 2025, Shenzhen-based Bambu Lab ranked No. 1 globally in shipments of sub-US$2,500 3D printers, with a 37% share, and reportedly surpassed RMB 10 billion in annual revenue. Its MakerWorld platform reached roughly 10 million monthly active users. Backed by IDG Capital, Temasek, True Light, 5Y Capital, and Mingshi Investment, it combines printers, materials, software, and MakerWorld. And its R1 laser cutter extends that ecosystem into personal manufacturing.

A Shenzhen Company With a Global First Act

In 2022, Bambu Lab took an unusual route to market. Instead of debuting at a domestic trade fair, the young Chinese company launched its first major product, the X1 series, on Kickstarter. The campaign drew 5,575 backers and raised RMB 47.28 million, introducing its former DJI team to a global community of early users.

Company ID Card

Afterward, Bambu Lab raised angel backing from Mingshi Investment in 2021, a RMB 30 million Series A from IDG Capital in 2022, a 2023 Series B involving Mingshi, 5Y Capital, Temasek, and True Light Capital, and a RMB 150 million Series C from IDG in April 2024.

Built from the start for global customers, Bambu Lab was founded in Shenzhen in 2020. It operates R&D centers in Shenzhen and Shanghai, with an office in Austin, Texas. Bambu Lab is a Chinese company built from the start for global customers.

For years, desktop 3D printing carried a frustrating contradiction: it promised creative freedom but often required users to become repair technicians. Machines needed leveling, tuning, maintenance, and patience. The X1’s real proposition was not that it made 3D printing new; it made it less exhausting.

With automated calibration, cameras, lidar-assisted monitoring, and its Automatic Material System for multicolor printing, Bambu Lab packaged considerable engineering into something closer to an appliance. Its breakthrough was technical, but also psychological: it reduced the anxiety of pressing print and wondering what would fail.

Overseas First, Ecosystem Next

That approach resonated overseas. Makers, educators, small studios, Etsy sellers, and print-farm operators already knew 3D printing’s potential—and its everyday frustrations: calibration, failed prints, material changes, and maintenance.

Bambu Lab’s answer has been to build an ecosystem around the machine. It sells printers alongside software, branded filaments, accessories, the Bambu Handy app, and MakerWorld, its model-sharing platform. Together, these tools reduce the friction between finding a design and producing a successful print.

Photo by Jakub Żerdzicki on Unsplash

For buyers, that ecosystem matters. Without dependable profiles, usable models, compatible materials, or a community, a printer can quickly become an underused machine.

By the end of 2025, MakerWorld had reached about 10 million monthly active users, with an 83% one-year retention rate. The strategy extends Bambu Lab’s role beyond hardware into the broader 3D-printing workflow.

China, meanwhile, is becoming a stronger second engine. By June 2026, Bambu Lab’s cumulative domestic consumer-printer sales had surpassed one million units, while the company reported 2025 revenue above RMB 10 billion.

From Cult Machine to Category Leader

By late 2025, Bambu Lab had moved to the front of the entry-level market. CONTEXT’s Q4 data put it first globally in shipments of 3D printers priced below US$2,500, with a 37% share. That does not make it the leader in industrial 3D printing, but it is a notable result in a field that includes Creality, Elegoo, and Anycubic.

The company has expanded its X1 halo into a layered portfolio. The newer P2S represents the practical middle: a productive machine for serious hobbyists and small businesses, with AI error detection, upgraded airflow, and a quick-swap nozzle system.

Bambu Lab P2S

At the ambitious end is the H2C. Its Vortek system uses up to six interchangeable hotends and supports as many as 24 filaments, reducing the material waste normally associated with frequent color changes. The H2D Pro reaches toward enterprise users with high-temperature materials, a 350°C nozzle, and an actively heated chamber.

Together, these machines show a company trying to stretch upward without abandoning the users who made it famous: people who want industrial capability without an industrial learning curve.

Scale has also brought legal scrutiny. In September 2026, a Texas jury awarded Stratasys US$27.6 million after finding Bambu Lab liable for infringing four patents; the company plans to seek review and appeal. A European court had earlier declined an injunction in a related case, and the wider dispute remains unresolved.

Beyond the Printer

Having broadened its range within 3D printing, Bambu Lab is now moving into adjacent forms of desktop fabrication. In September 2026, it introduced the R1, a 55W CO₂ laser cutter and engraver—its first dedicated product line beyond 3D printing.

Designed for materials including wood, acrylic, leather, paper, and fabric, the R1 extends the company’s reach from additive manufacturing into cutting and engraving.

The move is logical: a maker who prints a plastic enclosure may also want to cut an acrylic panel or engrave a logo using the same ecosystem. Bambu Lab is thus broadening from 3D printers into a wider range of desktop manufacturing tools.

Xiaomi to invest RMB 1.7 billion in EV battery maker Sunwoda Mobility

Xiaomi plans to invest RMB 1.7 billion in Sunwoda Mobility, acquiring a 5.34% stake in the electric vehicle battery maker through its wholly owned investment subsidiary, according to an October 29 filing by parent company Sunwoda Electronic.

The transaction will involve subscribing for newly issued capital. Following the investment and a planned board reshuffle, Sunwoda Electronic will hold no more than half of Sunwoda Mobility’s board seats. It will consequently cease to control the battery business and stop consolidating its financial results.

The investment deepens Xiaomi’s relationship with a battery supplier as it expands its automotive business. Sunwoda Mobility was already named a strategic partner in Xiaomi’s Dragon Armor battery program, which the automaker introduced in September. [Sunwoda Electronic filing, in Chinese]

ISHO, the sleep technology startup founded by former Xiaomi executive Wang Teng, unveiled its first products on October 29: the ISHO Ring smart ring and the iFlow bed temperature-control system. Prices start at RMB 1,799 and RMB 9,999, respectively.
