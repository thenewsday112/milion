Major ‘Chicago Med’ Star Returning

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →
CHICAGO MED -- Pictured: "Chicago Med" Key Art -- (Photo by: NBCUniversal)

Colin Donnell is returning to Chicago Med.

After leaving the popular NBC drama in 2019, Donnell is back, reprising his role as Dr. Connor Rhodes.

PEOPLE reports that Donnell is returning to the cast for a pair of episodes in season 12, which includes the fall finale, slated to air on Dec. 9. Per PEOPLE, Dr. Rhodes will appear to assist with a complicated heart surgery to be performed on a young patient.

Donnell, 44, was cast in a main role on Chicago Med from the series’ first season until part of the way through the fifth season. He’s been absent from the cast for seven years.

In his time away from the show, Donnell kept busy in front of the screen. His post-Chicago Med TV roles include Love on Iceland, To Catch a Spy, Irreverent, FBI: International, and Zero Day.

Prior to being cast as Dr. Rhodes, Donnell made a name for himself on Broadway, in film, and on the small screen. Jersey Boys and Anything Goes are among the shows he performed on Broadway. He also was part of the cast for the Mamma Mia! tour. His film credits include Every Secret Thing, which starred Elizabeth Banks and Dakota Fanning, and 2019’s Almost Love.

On the small screen, Donnell’s resume includes appearances in Arrow and The Affair before landing the Chicago Med role.

“Dr. Connor Rhodes was the very first character fans met in the opening scene of Chicago Med’s premiere episodes in 2015, “Showrunner Allen MacDonald told PEOPLE. “And he’s one of my absolute favorites in the history of the series, so bringing him back has been an absolute priority for me since I joined the show. I’m so thrilled that Colin Donnel has agreed to return his iconic role.”

New episodes of Chicago Med air on Wednesday nights on NBC.

Noetix’s RMB9,998 Humanoid Is Just the Start

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editor’s note: This article is republished from Jumpstart, a TechNode Group publication.

For RMB9,998, Noetix Robotics offered buyers something that had usually belonged to research laboratories, corporate demonstrations and expensive technology showcases: a humanoid robot they could consider buying for themselves.

Bumi, the small robot the Chinese company introduced in October 2025, was aimed at programming education and companionship. Its appeal rested on an immediate proposition. Children could programme its movements, watch it dance and interact with a machine that looked and moved like a small person.

That proposition also expressed a larger choice about how to build a robotics company. Noetix would first make the body affordable and capable enough to sell. More sophisticated intelligence would follow.

Three years after its founding, the company is attempting to turn that sequence into a much larger business. It is pursuing higher deliveries, investing in its own embodied-intelligence models and seeking customers overseas. In October, media reports said it had confidentially submitted a Hong Kong listing application, potentially targeting a 2027 flotation.

The question connecting those ambitions is whether the hardware business Noetix built first can provide enough customers, experience and cash to support what comes next.

A Price Decided Before Production

Founder Jiang Zheyuan left his doctoral studies at Tsinghua University in 2023 and established Noetix that September. His early technical priority was the robot’s body and motion control: the machinery and software that allow it to balance, move and respond reliably.

The distinction matters. A robot can execute demanding movements while relying on external models for conversation or higher-level instructions. Building an athletic machine and building one that can work out what to do in an unfamiliar room involve different capabilities.

Jiang’s decision was to address the physical machine first. He explained that, when the company began, he saw unreliable bodies and limited manufacturing readiness as the immediate bottlenecks. AI approaches were also changing rapidly. Noetix concentrated its resources on hardware while waiting for a clearer direction in the intelligence stack.

Low cost was part of that engineering brief. In a March interview, Jiang described using composite materials in Bumi’s body to reduce its dependence on metal structures. The price was being worked into the design, rather than left for a sales team to negotiate after production.

That approach gave the company a product with a readily understood purpose. Parents and schools could buy a programmable humanoid for education and interaction. They did not have to wait for general-purpose domestic labour to become commercially viable.

The narrower use case helped Noetix reach buyers sooner. It also established the next commercial task: giving those buyers enough continuing value to make the robot more than an exciting first purchase.

Attention Becomes a Manufacturing Problem

Noetix’s machines found a powerful route to public attention through performance. Its N2 robot finished second in the 2025 Beijing humanoid half-marathon. In 2026, the company’s robots appeared alongside comedian Cai Ming in a sketch on China’s Spring Festival Gala.

The exposure connected technical demonstrations with a mass audience. A robot that families had seen on television was also available through retail channels.

At the AWE exhibition in March, Jiang said orders had surged after the gala and set a target of more than 10,000 Bumi sales during 2026. The company was moving from proving that people would notice its robots to showing that it could deliver them consistently.

Jiang’s account of the obstacles was strikingly practical. He identified component availability and production-line management as central challenges. A missing part can stop an entire machine from being completed. As monthly output rises, the company must train and manage a larger workforce while maintaining assembly quality.

These are consequential problems for a company whose market entry depends on affordability. Every reworked unit, delayed delivery and service visit consumes part of the margin that a low selling price leaves available.

The manufacturing target therefore carries more meaning than a shipment milestone. It tests whether Noetix can preserve the economics designed into Bumi while producing and supporting it in much larger numbers.

The Brain Becomes the Expensive Part

By September, Noetix was making its next technical commitment explicit. It launched the embodied-intelligence sub-brand Scalabot and the HERON world-model architecture, extending its ambitions into how robots understand environments and learn to act within them.

The company had begun assembling a dedicated intelligence team in July 2025, according to Jiang’s September interview with Caijing. The work added a substantial research programme to an organisation already scaling physical production.

His logic connects the two activities. Cheaper, reliable machines can be deployed more widely; those deployments can create opportunities to collect experience for training better models.

Making that connection productive requires another layer of engineering. A robot in a classroom generates experience, but useful training data must still be selected, recorded, organised and connected to tasks the company wants its models to learn. A large installed base creates access to potential data. The collection and training system determines its value.

Jiang told 21st Century Business Herald that intelligence research had become a major area of expenditure. He also described hardware as a potential source of funds for that work.

This is the financial hinge in Noetix’s strategy. The body-first approach opened an earlier route to revenue. The company now wants that operating business to help support a more expensive effort whose commercial returns will take longer to develop.

External capital helps bridge the timing. Its backers include Chendao Capital, an investment platform associated with CATL. A prospective Hong Kong listing would take that financing journey into public markets, where investors would be able to examine more closely how hardware sales and research spending fit together.

Taking a Chinese Starting Point Overseas

Noetix’s domestic route has combined educational demand, accessible hardware and unusually broad public exposure. Its overseas expansion will test how much of that combination travels.

At CES in January, co-founder Zhang Shipu outlined five target regions: North America, Europe, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and Japan and South Korea. He described an ambition to reach thousand-unit scale in overseas expansion during the second quarter, supported by local deployments, customer service and repeatable reference projects.

That programme involves more than shipping robots abroad. Schools need teaching materials and support. Distributors need spare parts and repair arrangements. Families need a reason to keep using the product after its novelty fades.

Noetix has some of those relationships to build from in China. An investor, Beijing Industrial Development Investment Management, reported partnerships with programming-education provider Codemao and retailer Kidswant, alongside deployments and donations involving schools. These connections show how a robot can become part of an existing educational or retail system.

Overseas growth will require comparable connections in markets with different curricula, languages and purchasing habits. The product’s price can win attention, but local usefulness and support will determine whether that attention becomes a durable business.

Noetix’s first move was to make a humanoid affordable enough to enter more classrooms and homes. That choice has given it a product to sell while the broader intelligence problem remains under development.

It is now trying to make that early foothold carry three ambitions at once: larger production volumes, more capable intelligence and international growth. Building the body first won Noetix a place in the market. The next stage is to make that place valuable enough to finance everything it still wants the robot to become.

How Bambu Lab Turned 3D Printing Into a Consumer Hit

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editor’s Note: In 2025, Shenzhen-based Bambu Lab ranked No. 1 globally in shipments of sub-US$2,500 3D printers, with a 37% share, and reportedly surpassed RMB 10 billion in annual revenue. Its MakerWorld platform reached roughly 10 million monthly active users. Backed by IDG Capital, Temasek, True Light, 5Y Capital, and Mingshi Investment, it combines printers, materials, software, and MakerWorld. And its R1 laser cutter extends that ecosystem into personal manufacturing.

A Shenzhen Company With a Global First Act

In 2022, Bambu Lab took an unusual route to market. Instead of debuting at a domestic trade fair, the young Chinese company launched its first major product, the X1 series, on Kickstarter. The campaign drew 5,575 backers and raised RMB 47.28 million, introducing its former DJI team to a global community of early users.

Company ID Card

Afterward, Bambu Lab raised angel backing from Mingshi Investment in 2021, a RMB 30 million Series A from IDG Capital in 2022, a 2023 Series B involving Mingshi, 5Y Capital, Temasek, and True Light Capital, and a RMB 150 million Series C from IDG in April 2024.

Built from the start for global customers, Bambu Lab was founded in Shenzhen in 2020. It operates R&D centers in Shenzhen and Shanghai, with an office in Austin, Texas. Bambu Lab is a Chinese company built from the start for global customers.

For years, desktop 3D printing carried a frustrating contradiction: it promised creative freedom but often required users to become repair technicians. Machines needed leveling, tuning, maintenance, and patience. The X1’s real proposition was not that it made 3D printing new; it made it less exhausting.

With automated calibration, cameras, lidar-assisted monitoring, and its Automatic Material System for multicolor printing, Bambu Lab packaged considerable engineering into something closer to an appliance. Its breakthrough was technical, but also psychological: it reduced the anxiety of pressing print and wondering what would fail.

Overseas First, Ecosystem Next

That approach resonated overseas. Makers, educators, small studios, Etsy sellers, and print-farm operators already knew 3D printing’s potential—and its everyday frustrations: calibration, failed prints, material changes, and maintenance.

Bambu Lab’s answer has been to build an ecosystem around the machine. It sells printers alongside software, branded filaments, accessories, the Bambu Handy app, and MakerWorld, its model-sharing platform. Together, these tools reduce the friction between finding a design and producing a successful print.

Photo by Jakub Żerdzicki on Unsplash

For buyers, that ecosystem matters. Without dependable profiles, usable models, compatible materials, or a community, a printer can quickly become an underused machine.

By the end of 2025, MakerWorld had reached about 10 million monthly active users, with an 83% one-year retention rate. The strategy extends Bambu Lab’s role beyond hardware into the broader 3D-printing workflow.

China, meanwhile, is becoming a stronger second engine. By June 2026, Bambu Lab’s cumulative domestic consumer-printer sales had surpassed one million units, while the company reported 2025 revenue above RMB 10 billion.

From Cult Machine to Category Leader

By late 2025, Bambu Lab had moved to the front of the entry-level market. CONTEXT’s Q4 data put it first globally in shipments of 3D printers priced below US$2,500, with a 37% share. That does not make it the leader in industrial 3D printing, but it is a notable result in a field that includes Creality, Elegoo, and Anycubic.

The company has expanded its X1 halo into a layered portfolio. The newer P2S represents the practical middle: a productive machine for serious hobbyists and small businesses, with AI error detection, upgraded airflow, and a quick-swap nozzle system.

Bambu Lab P2S

At the ambitious end is the H2C. Its Vortek system uses up to six interchangeable hotends and supports as many as 24 filaments, reducing the material waste normally associated with frequent color changes. The H2D Pro reaches toward enterprise users with high-temperature materials, a 350°C nozzle, and an actively heated chamber.

Together, these machines show a company trying to stretch upward without abandoning the users who made it famous: people who want industrial capability without an industrial learning curve.

Scale has also brought legal scrutiny. In September 2026, a Texas jury awarded Stratasys US$27.6 million after finding Bambu Lab liable for infringing four patents; the company plans to seek review and appeal. A European court had earlier declined an injunction in a related case, and the wider dispute remains unresolved.

Beyond the Printer

Having broadened its range within 3D printing, Bambu Lab is now moving into adjacent forms of desktop fabrication. In September 2026, it introduced the R1, a 55W CO₂ laser cutter and engraver—its first dedicated product line beyond 3D printing.

Designed for materials including wood, acrylic, leather, paper, and fabric, the R1 extends the company’s reach from additive manufacturing into cutting and engraving.

The move is logical: a maker who prints a plastic enclosure may also want to cut an acrylic panel or engrave a logo using the same ecosystem. Bambu Lab is thus broadening from 3D printers into a wider range of desktop manufacturing tools.

Xiaomi to invest RMB 1.7 billion in EV battery maker Sunwoda Mobility

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Xiaomi plans to invest RMB 1.7 billion in Sunwoda Mobility, acquiring a 5.34% stake in the electric vehicle battery maker through its wholly owned investment subsidiary, according to an October 29 filing by parent company Sunwoda Electronic.

The transaction will involve subscribing for newly issued capital. Following the investment and a planned board reshuffle, Sunwoda Electronic will hold no more than half of Sunwoda Mobility’s board seats. It will consequently cease to control the battery business and stop consolidating its financial results.

The investment deepens Xiaomi’s relationship with a battery supplier as it expands its automotive business. Sunwoda Mobility was already named a strategic partner in Xiaomi’s Dragon Armor battery program, which the automaker introduced in September. [Sunwoda Electronic filing, in Chinese]
