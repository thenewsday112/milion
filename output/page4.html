Norway Oil Fund CEO Nicolai Tangen Pushes Back on Tech Caps for $2 Trillion Nest Egg

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Nicolai Tangen is defending Norway’s investment approach as technology stocks take a growing share of its national savings. In a September submission to the Ministry of Finance, the Norges Bank Investment Management CEO and central bank governor Ida Wolden Bache advised against caps on individual companies or sectors. Their warning about how quickly the vast fund could sell during a crisis helps explain what is at stake.

This past summer, as much of the world rearranged its waking hours around the World Cup, Norway offered an unexpected reason to keep watching. Powered by its towering blond striker, Manchester City’s Erling Haaland, the team reached its first-ever quarterfinal. Those familiar with Haaland’s appetite for goals might have seen it coming; the spectacle supplied by his country’s supporters, however, was harder to anticipate.

In stadiums and city squares around the world, Norwegian fans crouched in rows, reached forward and pulled back in unison, chanting “Ro! Ro! Ro!”—“Row! Row! Row!”

Thousands of people, one imaginary Viking longship, everyone pulling together.

It was a brazen demonstration of the collective resolve that has helped define the Norwegian spirit. But perhaps the most consequential expression of this shared purpose has unfolded far from the football stands, in the patient accumulation of the country’s vast wealth.

Since its sovereign wealth fund—officially named the Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG)—received its first capital injection in 1996, Norway has methodically invested the proceeds of its North Sea offshore oil and gas resources. Managed by Norges Bank Investment Management, or NBIM, the investment-management arm of the country’s central bank, the Norwegian sovereign wealth fund embodies a commitment to future generations that has outlasted governments and weathered multiple global financial crises.

Deposits and investment returns have built GPFG into the world’s largest sovereign wealth fund: a more-than-$2 trillion behemoth larger than the individual sovereign funds of Saudi Arabia, Abu Dhabi, Kuwait or Qatar.

For a country of just 5.6 million people, that is an extraordinary inheritance, but it also ties Norway’s future to forces far beyond its shores.

Too big to diversify

Somewhere along its voyage, Norway acquired a sizable piece of Silicon Valley—leaving its national savings increasingly exposed to technology’s soaring expectations and sudden reversals.

For most institutional investors, trimming winners and taking profits is prudent housekeeping. For Norway, it raises a more unsettling question: What happens when the investor trying to reduce its risk is large enough to become a risk to everyone else? A retreat from American technology stocks could send tremors through markets increasingly dependent on the same handful of companies. Could Norway’s effort to protect its national nest egg help set off the sell-off it hoped to escape?

Wall Street knows what it means to be too big to fail. Is Norway’s sovereign wealth fund too big to diversify?

“The principal constraint would be the mandate: the benchmark and risk limits set by the Ministry of Finance and the Norges Bank Executive Board define how far we can deviate from the market,” NBIM Deputy CEO Trond Grande tells Observer. “We wouldn’t say the fund is too big to diversify.”

Technology investments represented roughly 32 percent of GPFG’s equity portfolio at midyear, up from about 20 percent five years earlier. That puts technology at approximately 23 percent of the entire fund, compared with roughly 14 percent in 2021—albeit these figures cover the sector globally.

Success has helped spur the concentration. Its technology investments returned over 25 percent in the first half, nearly twice its stock portfolio’s overall return.

“We see [the concentration in tech] as a feature of the market, not a position we’ve taken,” Grande observed.

For Otaviano Canuto, a former World Bank vice president and IMF executive director, counting holdings only tells part of the story.

“It is diversified across the market, not away from it, so a tech-and-AI drawdown would hit it hard,” he tells Observer.

Yet Canuto describes Norway as “more of a price-taker than price-maker.” Its gradual trading and relatively small flows compared with turnover in liquid stocks temper its market impact.

Patient reallocation, however, differs from an emergency exit.

In a September submission to the Ministry of Finance, signed by Norway’s central bank governor, Ida Wolden Bache, and NBIM CEO Nicolai Tangen, Norges Bank warned that the fund’s size severely limits rapid selling during a crisis. A hypothetical A.I.-disappointment scenario modeled in 2025 estimated a 35 percent decline in its value—a stress test, not a forecast. Nevertheless, the bank advised against company or sector caps, citing potential lost returns and their limitations in capturing A.I. exposure.

Norway’s true advantage is its capacity to wait. With no short-term liabilities and a horizon spanning generations, it can withstand turbulence that might force others to sell.

The fund’s third-quarter results, released Oct. 22, brought a quieter disappointment: a 0.5 percent loss. Fixed-income investments fell 3.5 percent, while equities gained 0.5 percent. Even a portfolio built for generations can spend three months going backward.

A stronger Norwegian krone also reduced the value of the fund’s foreign assets when expressed in kroner, while new government deposits partly offset the decline. That currency effect is separate from the investment return. The fund’s value at the end of September was 22,014 billion kroner, or approximately 22 trillion kroner.

But even if NBIM decided to pull back its tech exposure, it would then immediately confront another dilemma: Where do you find alternatives large enough to matter to a $2 trillion portfolio?

“At our size, few investments can make a real difference,” Grande acknowledged. A deal can be enormous by ordinary standards and barely register in Oslo.

Case in point: GPFG’s recent 1.2 billion euro commitment to a renewable-energy fund managed by Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners, a specialist in developing large energy projects. That’s a billion-dollar-plus allocation that amounts to less than one-tenth of one percent of Norway’s portfolio.

Such investments, Grande said, are “unlikely to change the fund’s overall risk profile materially.”

Soft power

Whether Norway chooses to acknowledge it or not, three decades of patient investing have also built something no balance sheet fully captures: an extraordinary reservoir of soft power.

“The fund has given Norwegian soft power a financial dimension,” said Kristian Hvilen, a Norwegian who heads the Norway office of Rud Pedersen, a leading European public affairs firm.

“The challenge is not a lack of confidence,” he said. “It is that we are less comfortable describing the combined effect of these strengths as national power.”

Norway brings judgments about corporate behavior to the shareholder register. Its ethical exclusions have kept money out of tobacco producers, certain weapons manufacturers and companies implicated in human rights violations. Being deemed unfit for Norway’s savings can carry a reputational sting alongside the sale of shares.

But influence runs both ways.

“The portfolio may be globally diversified, while returns are still strongly shaped by US technology, regulation, trade policy and geopolitical tensions,” Hvilen tells Observer.

He argues that the fund’s strength rests on remaining a savings vehicle rather than an instrument of national power. Financial purpose, however, does not erase political significance.

For countries such as Guyana, Suriname and Uganda seeking to turn petroleum wealth into a lasting financial inheritance, Norway offers an alluring model. Yet Canuto sees a potential trap: If more sovereign funds invest according to the same market benchmarks, more money will flow into the same dominant companies. The geopolitical irony is hard to miss. Countries building a foundation for financial independence could deepen their dependence on American technology.

Grande emphasizes the discipline behind Norway’s success: a clear mandate, a long investment horizon and institutions that separate politics from daily investment decisions. “We can’t speak for other countries, and each has different needs.”

“Ro! Ro! Ro!” was the chant that echoed through football stands and city squares last summer—a full-throated expression of a national resolve that has been equally formidable, if less conspicuous, in global finance. Three decades of pulling together have given Norwegians a financial presence that much larger nations might envy.

But should Norway choose to row against the current, the world’s markets may discover just how much water this small country can move—and who gets soaked in its wake.

Armani CEO Giuseppe Marsocci Weighs Who Gets the First 15 Percent of the House

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Giuseppe Marsocci is weighing how to sell the first 15 percent of Giorgio Armani’s fashion house. The founder will name three preferred buyers. The CEO has left open the possibility of dividing the stake among several investors, a choice that could complicate a much larger sale later.

LVMH, L’Oréal and EssilorLuxottica were named in Giorgio Armani’s will. They have different reasons to consider an investment: LVMH has experience running fashion houses, while Armani’s two licensees have longstanding beauty and eyewear businesses.

“It is not written in stone that it has to be one investor,” Marsocci told reporters at Armani’s Milan fashion show on Sept. 27, according to Reuters. He said no decision had been made.

Marsocci said the company intended to follow the founder’s timetable, which puts the first sale between September 2026 and March 2027. But an Oct. 22 report by Business People, citing Il Sole 24 Ore’s reporting the previous day, said the search could extend beyond March and include investors outside the preferred trio.

Putting a price on Armani

Giorgio Armani, who died Sept. 4, 2025, at 91, left detailed instructions for the company he co-founded in 1975. His will calls for an initial 15 percent sale within 12 to 18 months, followed by the sale of an additional 30 percent to 54.9 percent of the company to the same buyer between three and five years after his death. Together, the two sales could leave that investor with 45 percent to 69.9 percent.

A public listing is the alternative to the second sale. The will requires the Giorgio Armani Foundation to retain at least 30.1 percent after a listing. The initial 15 percent stake carries 15 percent of the voting rights and the right to appoint one board member.

Marsocci was 61 when he was appointed CEO on Oct. 16, 2025. A longtime Armani executive, he had spent the preceding six years as global chief commercial officer.

Bankers and advisers consulted by Reuters in September estimated the group’s value at approximately 5 billion euros to 7 billion euros—about $5.6 billion to $7.8 billion at Oct. 30 exchange rates.

At that range, a 15 percent holding would have a proportional value of 750 million euros to 1.05 billion euros, before accounting for negotiated rights or other terms.

The company generated 2.19 billion euros in revenue in 2025, with sales down 2.8 percent at constant exchange rates, Vogue Business reported. Direct-to-consumer sales grew 2 percent, while indirect sales fell 7 percent.

Wholesale orders weakened as Armani narrowed distribution. Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization rose 3 percent despite the sales decline.

Marsocci is also overseeing a business plan ahead of the ownership transition. Bloomberg reported on June 30, citing people familiar with the matter, that Armani was working with Boston Consulting Group and exploring expansion in handbags and hospitality.

On Sept. 16, the group appointed Dario Vitale creative director of Emporio Armani and Giorgio Armani accessories. In the announcement, Marsocci singled out accessories as a growth opportunity.

Three potential buyers, different interests

L’Oréal and EssilorLuxottica already have longstanding commercial relationships with Armani. L’Oréal, led by Nicolas Hieronimus, has developed and distributed Armani fragrances, cosmetics and skin care since 1988. Its beauty license runs through 2050.

In its Oct. 22 sales update, L’Oréal highlighted growth from Emporio Armani Stronger With You and the launch of Giorgio Armani I Will. Its Luxe division recorded 4.18 billion euros in sales in the three months ended Sept. 30, up 6.4 percent on a like-for-like basis. Those are division-wide results, not Armani brand sales.

Armani’s eyewear partnership began with Luxottica in 1988. In 2022, EssilorLuxottica and Armani signed a 15-year licensing renewal effective Jan. 1, 2023. Both licensees have commercial reasons to care about who owns the fashion house, even without taking responsibility for its clothing business.

Reuters put the licensees’ combined 2025 revenue from the Armani agreements at almost 2 billion euros. Armani earns royalties on those sales.

LVMH brings a different kind of experience. Its portfolio includes Louis Vuitton, Dior and Fendi, and it operates fashion houses outright. Taking a small stake in Armani would give it a more limited initial role, with the terms of a later sale or listing still to be determined.

In May, Reuters relayed La Repubblica’s account of a possible three-way split, with each preferred buyer taking 5 percent. The Italian newspaper did not identify its sources. Marsocci’s September comments stopped short of endorsing that split.

At an assumed 5 billion euro valuation, each 5 percent holding would have a proportional value of 250 million euros, compared with 750 million euros for the full initial stake.

Who decides what happens next

Marsocci does not have sole authority over the transaction. Pantaleo “Leo” Dell’Orco, Armani’s longtime partner, chairs the company and its foundation. Under the initial succession arrangements, he held 40 percent of the voting rights and the foundation held another 30 percent.

The will provides for foundation veto rights over specified major decisions, including changes to the company’s statutes, capital increases and mergers. A shared stake would also require agreement on how the outside investors exercise their voting and board rights and participate in the next transaction.

Marsocci has left room for more than one buyer. A split 15 percent sale would still have to resolve who could buy the larger holding later, under a succession plan that envisages a second deal with the same buyer.

Washington governor rips Microsoft over TV ad in leaked audio

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Washington Gov. Bob Ferguson, speaking at an event in Olympia in June, called out Microsoft last week over a TV ad from a PAC the company has helped fund. (Office of the Governor Photo)

Washington Gov. Bob Ferguson called out Microsoft for bankrolling a political action committee responsible for a TV ad describing state government as broken, according to newly leaked audio from a Seattle Chamber leadership conference last week.

Remarks at the Seattle Metropolitan Chamber of Commerce’s Regional Leadership Conference, held at the Suncadia resort near Cle Elum, Wash., are supposed to be off the record. But The Washington Observer, an independent newsletter on state politics, published the audio Friday, saying it was obtained from an unnamed source.

In the audio, Ferguson zeroed in on a line near the start of the ad — that in Washington, “it used to be all you needed was an idea, a dream and the guts to chase it.”

The governor repeated the words “it used to be” for emphasis, in a tone of disbelief.

“What’s up with that?” Ferguson asked the crowd of business and government leaders. “First, does anyone else in this room believe that? I sure as hell don’t.”

“I talk to people every day who have that idea, that dream. They’re chasing it and they’re getting it,” he said. He cited the record $3.5 billion that Seattle-area startups raised in the third quarter, more than triple the same period a year ago, according to PitchBook data.

The Microsoft relationship: Ferguson, who noted that he had worked as a lawyer for Microsoft, said he loves the company and respects what it does. But he seemed especially peeved that the ad ran during the Seattle Seahawks game, where it reached people across the state and those from elsewhere who might want to come here and start a company.

“If we’re partners, partners should treat partners like partners, right?” he said.

He asked the room to imagine him running a multimillion-dollar ad criticizing Microsoft. “You guys would think I lost my mind if I did that,” he said.

Ferguson called Microsoft a key partner and a member of his economic roundtable, and said he works well with the company. “But it works both ways,” he said.

Ferguson acknowledged that the state has problems. “Are there challenges? Sure,” he said. But he said he didn’t think the ad was “helpful for where we all want to go.”

He told the crowd he welcomes constructive feedback, but asked business leaders to think about how they communicate, and to talk up the state’s workforce, economy and startup funding along with its challenges.

“What we have here is so bloody special,” he said.

Ferguson, who took office in January 2025, also reminded the room that he isn’t going anywhere. “I plan on being governor for a long time,” he said, adding that he works with all of them, “Microsoft included.”

The Observer noted that $10 million, roughly what the group has left to spend, would be a good start on a campaign to run someone else for governor in 2028.

The newsletter said it released just the audio from a video of Ferguson’s remarks, to protect its source. Among those in the audience was Michael Mattmiller, who represents Microsoft in Olympia. He and Ferguson “kinda-sorta hugged it out afterward,” the Observer reported.

Mattmiller declined to comment to the Observer, and Ferguson’s office didn’t respond to the publication. Microsoft referred GeekWire’s inquiry to Washington Needs to Compete, which the company described as a growing coalition of more than 60 funders that want to keep the state competitive. GeekWire has contacted the group and the governor’s office for comment.

Who’s behind the ad: The campaign comes from Washington Needs to Compete, a PAC funded in large part by Microsoft and its current and former executives. The group has raised about $13.8 million, according to state campaign finance records, including more than $5 million directly from Microsoft.

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella and former CEO Steve Ballmer each gave $250,000, and Vice Chair and President Brad Smith gave more than $500,000. Other backers include T-Mobile, F5, Alaska Airlines and Weyerhaeuser.

Washington Needs to Compete has spent about $3 million so far, according to state records. Most of it has gone to state legislative races, the Observer reported, including more than $700,000 backing Rep. Amy Walen, D-Kirkland, in the 48th District, home to Microsoft’s Redmond headquarters.

The group spent $97,000 to air the ad during the Seahawks’ Oct. 15 Thursday night loss to the Denver Broncos on Prime Video and $130,000 during their Oct. 25 Sunday night win over the Kansas City Chiefs on NBC, according to the Observer.

Washington Needs to Compete’s TV ad, which aired during two Seattle Seahawks games this month and drew Gov. Bob Ferguson’s criticism. (WA Needs 2 Compete via YouTube)

What the ad says: The spot opens with a nod to “inventors in garages,” “mom-and-pop shops down Main Street” and farmers “feeding the world,” before declaring, “In Washington, it used to be all you needed was an idea, a dream and the guts to chase it.”

Then it turns: “But today, Olympia is broken, spending more while you get less.” Jobs are leaving, the ad says, and “it’s harder than ever to afford a home and raise a family.”

It doesn’t name Ferguson or any legislation. It ends by saying Olympia needs to “quit wasting money, fix what’s broken and put Washington families first.”

Taxes and the business climate: Ferguson this year signed a 9.9% tax on household income above $1 million, known as the millionaires tax, which takes effect in 2028 and is projected to raise about $3 billion a year. Initiative 645, which would repeal it, is on the Nov. 3 ballot.

In September, Smith joined former Gov. Chris Gregoire and dozens of regional officials to launch a partnership aimed at reversing the Seattle area’s economic slide. “The warning signs are flashing on our economy,” Smith said at the time.

The region lost nearly 7,000 jobs in 2025, according to the group, and Washington has fallen from 32nd to 47th in CNBC’s ranking of states by cost of doing business since 2017.

In the same CNBC study, Washington ranks 11th overall, up from 14th last year, and fifth for its economy. WalletHub ranks Washington’s economy second in the nation, behind Massachusetts.

Washington’s gross domestic product grew faster than any other state’s in the first quarter of 2026, driven largely by its tech sector, according to federal data released in June.

Long-term plans: On its website, Washington Needs to Compete describes itself as businesses and residents building “a modern, permanent political organization” to support leaders and ideas that “strengthen our economy, improve affordability and expand opportunity,” through elections and public education.

The site lists its top five contributors as Microsoft, T-Mobile, the Washington Coalition for Responsible Taxes and Spending, F5 and Smith.

The group is saving most of its money for future campaigns, the Observer reported.

Executive Chairman Brunello Cucinelli Raises His Cashmere Empire’s 2026 Forecast

📅 Oct 31, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Now is a good time to be Brunello Cucinelli.

The Italian cashmere king’s namesake company generated €1.124 billion, or roughly $1.25 billion, in revenue during the first nine months of 2026, up 13.3 percent at constant exchange rates. Third-quarter revenue reached €374.2 million, up 13.5 percent.

The performance was strong enough for Cucinelli to raise his full-year revenue growth forecast from 10 to 11 percent to roughly 12 percent at constant exchange rates. He expects another 10 percent or so in 2027.

At a time when much of the luxury sector is still wrestling with uneven demand and exhausted aspirational shoppers, Cucinelli’s company is bucking the trend with typical Italian brio.

Retail revenue rose 19 percent at constant exchange rates through September. Sales increased 20 percent in the Americas and 14 percent in Asia, where the company said China remained one of the main drivers of growth.

Control remains concentrated around the founder. Foro delle Arti, the Cucinelli family holding company placed in an irrevocable trust, owns 50.05 percent of Brunello Cucinelli S.p.A.’s share capital and controls about 64.9 percent of its voting rights.

That structure has helped protect a founder’s vision that would look eccentric almost anywhere else.

Cucinelli talks as readily about Marcus Aurelius, St. Benedict and St. Francis as he does about margins or cashmere. He calls his approach to the product “gentle luxury,” part of a broader philosophy that prizes craftsmanship, restraint and an almost studied lack of logos. The lineage of founder-led luxury stretches through Hermès, Dior, Gucci and Louis Vuitton.

It sounds romantic.

It also sells a lot of sweaters.

A different kind of capitalism

Born in 1953 to a farming family in Castel Rigone, Umbria, Cucinelli trained as a surveyor, enrolled in engineering and abandoned his studies before starting a small company in 1978.

His early idea was simple: take cashmere, then largely associated with neutral colors, and dye it in brighter shades.

Almost half a century later, he remains executive chairman and creative director of a global luxury house whose business model is difficult to separate from the personality of its founder.

Cucinelli calls it “humanistic capitalism.”

At its core is the belief that a profitable company should also protect the dignity, pay and private lives of the people doing the work. The company says its wages are on average slightly higher, employees share defined working hours and nobody is expected to be online for business on weekends.

If it came from another CEO, some of the philosophy might read like image burnishing.

Cucinelli has built enough of it into the company’s actual working practices to make that dismissal harder.

The origins are personal. Cucinelli has often described seeing his father, Umberto, return from a cement factory humiliated by the treatment he received there.

Speaking to world leaders at the G20 in 2021, Cucinelli said that experience helped shape his determination to build a company around the “moral and economic dignity” of human beings.

“I wanted a company that made healthy profits, but did so with ethics, dignity and morals,” he said.

The village behind the vision

Cucinelli’s philosophy is inseparable from Solomeo, the medieval Umbrian hamlet where his wife, Federica, grew up.

He moved there after their marriage in 1982, bought the village’s ruined 14th-century castle in 1985 and inaugurated the company’s new headquarters there in 1987.

The restoration of Solomeo eventually became part corporate campus, part cultural project and part physical manifesto for Cucinelli’s ideas about work.

The working day starts at 8 a.m. and ends at 5:30 p.m., with a long lunch break. Cucinelli has also written that employees should not be online for business after work or on Saturdays and Sundays.

The company established its School of Contemporary High Craftsmanship and Arts in Solomeo in 2013, training younger workers in tailoring, knitwear and other manual trades.

There is a business case tucked inside all the philosophy.

A luxury company selling four-figure knitwear depends on skilled labor, quality and the idea that the object in a customer’s hands is meaningfully different from something mass-produced.

Keeping skilled craftspeople around is therefore good labor policy and good luxury strategy.

A billion-euro business

The latest sales figures make that combination difficult to dismiss.

Nine-month retail revenue reached €741 million, or 66 percent of sales, after rising 19 percent at constant exchange rates. Wholesale revenue reached €382.6 million and rose 4 percent.

Cucinelli has resisted presenting that growth as a race for scale. In his latest sales report, he returned to his preferred description of the business: “absolute luxury, without logo.”

He is equally particular about technology.

For a founder who has spent decades defending human work, Cucinelli has not rejected A.I. His position is that technology should amplify human ingenuity rather than replace it.

Solomei A.I., the technology lab founded by Brunello Cucinelli S.p.A. in 2021, developed Callimacus, the generative platform now powering the fashion house’s online boutique. Instead of a conventional page-by-page website, Callimacus reorganizes information around what an individual visitor is trying to find.

Salesforce invested in the company behind Callimacus this year. Marc Benioff has also been tied to the project through Salesforce Ventures, while industry coverage detailed the Callimacus investment.

It is an unusually modern project for a businessman whose worldview is so deliberately old-fashioned.

Cucinelli once wrote that “sometimes utopias turn into incredible successes.”

The first nine months of 2026 gave him €1.124 billion worth of evidence.

Nearly six months after Greenberg arrived, AlphaSense announced on Oct. 6 that it had added more than 10,000 investment-banking users in the third quarter and that 18 of the world’s top 20 investment banks, and more than 100 banks in all, now use its platform. She answered Observer’s questions about pricing, new capital and a possible IPO.

This Q&A has been edited and condensed for clarity.

Observer: AlphaSense exceeded $600 million in ARR in the first quarter, and The Information reported in July that it had passed $700 million. What’s driving the growth right now? Is it mostly new customers, existing customers spending more or newer products such as SuperAnalyst?

Samantha Greenberg: Our growth is driven by both new customer acquisition and expansion within our existing customer base. We’ve grown to support more than 8,000 enterprise customers while maintaining net revenue retention above 125 percent, reflecting how existing customers are expanding their use of the platform.

With the launch of SuperAnalyst, our always-on A.I. agent, we’re enabling customers to delegate complex, end-to-end workflows to A.I. agents that can continuously monitor information and update their work as conditions change.

How are investment banks expanding their use of the platform? Are they rolling it out more broadly across firms, adding teams gradually or buying additional products?

We’re seeing investment banks expand their use of AlphaSense beyond traditional research and integrate A.I. more deeply into their everyday workflows. That includes deal sourcing, client meeting preparation, pitch deck generation, financial modeling and due diligence.

As agents do more of the work, does that change how you think about pricing the product?

Absolutely. As A.I. agents take on more complex workflows, traditional user-based pricing becomes less reflective of the value customers receive. We’ve introduced consumption-based elements into our pricing model, allowing customers to purchase credits tied to specific research workflows and outcomes.

The scale of this shift is significant. Platform-wide token consumption has also reached an annualized run rate of 54 trillion tokens, up five times compared to early 2026.

We’re also being intentional about monetization, absorbing significant A.I.-related costs to give customers time to experience the platform’s value before expanding adoption. Ultimately, we see an opportunity to align pricing more closely with tailored outcomes rather than simply access to software.

The company raised $350 million in June at a $7.5 billion valuation. Where is most of that money going now?

We’re focused on accelerating innovation across our A.I. platform and proprietary content while continuing to expand internationally. The $350 million funding gives us the flexibility to invest aggressively in these areas while maintaining financial discipline.

We’re investing across our full technology stack, from proprietary content and data to A.I. orchestration and workflow automation, to ensure customers can confidently delegate complex work to A.I.

Is an IPO something the company is actively preparing for? If not, what would need to change before that becomes a serious conversation?

I can’t comment on IPO timing. That’s ultimately a board decision based on market conditions.

We’re continuing to build the business with the financial discipline and operational rigor expected of a public company while investing in innovation and international expansion. We see the public markets as a natural potential next step in our long-term growth journey, but our priorities today are execution and creating lasting value.

As AlphaSense moves deeper into investment banking, what do you see as its strongest advantage over established financial-data platforms such as Bloomberg and newer general-purpose A.I. tools?

Our strongest advantage is that we understand the unique needs of financial institutions, where trust, accuracy and security are essential to making high-stakes decisions. That’s why we’ve built AlphaSense specifically for financial workflows, combining premium, vetted content, including broker research and expert interviews, with A.I. capabilities designed around the use cases bankers rely on every day.

Unlike general-purpose A.I. tools, our platform grounds its outputs in trusted financial and business intelligence, with citations that allow users to verify the underlying sources.

The biggest benefit is giving bankers more time for work that requires human judgment and client relationships. By automating research, financial modeling and presentation preparation, AlphaSense allows analysts to focus on evaluating opportunities, pressure-testing assumptions and developing recommendations. Those improvements support Santander’s policy of returning approximately half its underlying profits through dividends and buybacks. Repurchases reduce the number of shares outstanding. Underlying earnings per share rose 20.7 percent in the first nine months, faster than underlying profit.

Botín has also freed up capital by selling businesses. In January, Santander completed the 7 billion euro sale of a controlling stake in Santander Bank Polska and an interest in its asset management business to Austria’s Erste Group.

The new issuance partly offset the canceled shares; registered capital and the weighted-average share count used for earnings per share are different measures.

The acquisitions also consume capital. Santander’s core capital ratio fell from 14 percent in June to 12.7 percent in September, including a 1.47-percentage-point reduction from Webster. How David Ellison Can Reinvent His Cable Brands Without Cable

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

David Ellison and Tom Cruise" width="970" height="763" data-caption='David Ellison’s Skydance owns cable brands whose talent relationships and cultural identities could outlast the traditional channel bundle. <span class="media-credit">Kate Green/Getty Images for Paramount Pictures</span>'>

David Ellison’s sprawling portfolio of Skydance brands runs longer than the bathroom line at a Rush concert. After combining Paramount and Warner Bros. Discovery on Oct. 6, he has inherited cable networks that once defined popular culture. Most concerning: The old distribution model is shrinking. In May 2025, streaming surpassed broadcast and cable combined in Nielsen’s U.S. television measurement for the first time.

Ellison and co-CEO Ynon Kreiz put the combined company’s annual revenue at nearly $70 billion and are targeting at least $6 billion in annual savings within three years. The proposals below are an argument for how Ellison could use that scale, not initiatives the company has announced.

The cost cutting has already begun. Skydance offered some employees voluntary buyouts and announced a five-day office requirement in an Oct. 26 memo reviewed by Reuters. With the combined company expected to shoulder approximately $80 billion in debt, Ellison has to preserve cable’s cash flow while developing what comes next.

Skydance will report its third-quarter results Nov. 9. The reporting period ended before the Oct. 6 merger, so those results will not represent a full quarter of the combined business. The call could nevertheless give Ellison another opportunity to explain his plans for the networks.

Much like ringtones and the original Four Loko formula, cable television is on its way out. But that doesn’t mean that once proud cable brands Comedy Central, MTV, BET, TNT and TBS are worthless in this modern media hierarchy.

Paramount leadership previously said it won’t be selling off cable networks. To extract value out of these declining assets, the company needs to focus more on what they once provided audiences rather than how they were watched. That way, Skydance can reinvent these linear brands into something valuable. Specifically low-cost, digital cultural destinations.

The strategy

Traditional media long prioritized the company and/or institution above all else. The brand was the star, cultivating the channel, recruiting appropriate talent, and delivering tailored content. You tuned into ESPN, not necessarily for anchor Scott Van Pelt himself.

Digital media flips the equation. A creator’s authenticity is the most powerful currency with audiences, before brands swoop in with fancy offers of infrastructure and resources. Talent creates the content, which is distributed directly to viewers, develops a following, and then teams with bigger brands as their popularity rises.

The latter is an easier entry point for talent, but more crowded competition in breaking through. For Skydance, it can mean a smaller upfront investment, more flexibility and built-in audiences. It’s not dissimilar to what Fox is doing with Tubi’s Stubios, which is a creator talent scouting and development pipeline.

Nothing can stop the Grim Reaper from collecting cable’s corpse. But Skydance has a valuable opportunity to create cost-effective cultural hubs around these brands for digital-native viewers.

Comedy Central

Across television (South Park, The Daily Show, Chappelle’s Show, Key & Peele, etc.) and signature stand-up specials (Mitch Hedberg, Amy Schumer, Kevin Hart, etc.), Comedy Central’s strengths were always identifying talent, building careers, and developing stars. Here’s the rub: Emerging comedians no longer need Comedy Central to reach audiences. TikTok, Instagram, YouTube and podcasts do that for you.

But the brand can still help independent creators in ways they can struggle to build organically: development support, production investment, film and TV crossover opportunities, and a shot at star-level compensation.

Skydance can reposition Comedy Central as the definitive curator of emerging online comedic talent. It should be the go-to destination for young audiences to find the funniest creators across (deep breath) TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Snap, Telegram, WhatsApp Channels, Discord, Reddit, Pinterest, Threads, and even X/Twitter (I’m aging myself with that last one).

Scout and recruit a rotating roster of about 20 promising online creators to develop recurring social formats: sketches, themed showcases, challenges, crossovers, livestreaming events, etc. Allow them to continue posting on their own channels while contributing to Comedy Central’s new-look social accounts. It’s only a good thing to start with more entry points for fans. Open up the historical IP vault for new spins on familiar material (please revive Drunk History). The aim is to build mutually beneficial partnerships.

Most importantly, treat this as a developmental minor league where successful talent can be strategically promoted. Successful social clips and creator showcases develop into episodic digital series and podcasts. Continued success warrants expansion into stand-up specials, live events and comedy tours. Then you get your true breakout with original television series, films and broader IP access.

The digital world is dominated by passionate subcultures that reward authentic and distinctive voices. In this way, Comedy Central becomes a mix of SportsCenter for comedy discovery and Saturday Night Live for creators.

MTV and VH1

Music discovery has been democratized, removing MTV’s first-mover advantage from the 20th century. But MTV’s ethos wasn’t necessarily music videos. It was the ability to shape youth culture and determine what was cool. Let’s rebuild that.

Local: Younger audiences often encounter news through social media. Why not highlight bubbling music scenes with market-specific coverage? Emerging artists, regional music festivals, producer profiles, songwriting processes, tour diaries and more built around music epicenters. Close to home isn’t just a theme. It’s a tactic.

Internet culture: Reimagine successful unscripted MTV formats for the chronically online. MTV should be where young scrollers go for real-time interpretations of virality across trends, memes, vernacular, etc. Think Urban Dictionary meets Tosh.0 blended with Subway Takes.

Lifestyle and identity: Recruit niche creators as correspondents covering fashion, relationships, fitness, college life, early careers and other life stages. Audiences always want to see themselves mirrored on screen.

While MTV plays to Gen Z and Gen Alpha, VH1 should target millennials with pop culture nostalgia. Traditional Hollywood has spent 15 years mining the 1980s and ’90s to great effect.

The key isn’t to try and reclaim the throne as the only cultural trendsetter. It’s about building a dynamic social feed driven by young taste-testing personalities.

BET

BET offers the clearest connective tissue between audience discovery, free streaming and paid customers.

The brand platforms Black culture, develops emerging talent, and serves underrepresented audiences. The streaming boom grew the volume of culturally specific entertainment, yet all that volume and choice made actually finding something to watch a headache. BET should once again serve as a cultural compass. This time, across three connected strategies:

Discovery: Social and digital programming populated by creators, music, interviews, cultural commentary and recommendations. Much like the Comedy Central plan, give creators access to past IP (please revive 106 & Park).

Engagement: Funnel viewers to Paramount’s free, ad-supported streaming television service (FAST) Pluto TV, which already curates culturally specific programming. (Pluto TV, Tubi and the Roku Channel together accounted for 5.7 percent of U.S. television viewing in Nielsen’s May 2025 measurement.) Content blocks can be extensions of specific themes, communities, genres, and personalities: HBCU culture, Black Horror/Comedy, current events, R&B, Eddie Murphy, etc.

Monetization: Try and convert that free engagement into BET+ subscriptions, or whichever consolidated streamer Skydance eventually settles on. If Tyler Perry, whose multi-year production deal with BET Media Group runs through 2028, wants to throw his muscle behind this plan, even better.

All of this lays the groundwork for BET to become a full-funnel brand for Skydance.

TNT and TBS

Thus far, I’ve focused on reinventing cable brands as digital assets. But TNT/TBS should return to developing long-running, broad-appeal sitcoms and procedurals. Just with an eye more towards HBO Max and Paramount+.

Legacy sitcoms and procedurals can deliver massive viewership that helps drive subscriber retention. TBS and TNT cable titles such as Animal Kingdom, The Closer, Rizzoli & Isles, The Last Ship and Major Crimes offer a library of shows built for habitual viewing.

The problem is that streaming services have struggled to consistently develop their own successful versions. There are exceptions, of course: The Pitt, The Lincoln Lawyer, Cobra Kai, Ted Lasso. Yet there are also quite a few one-season misfires such as Disjointed, Reboot, On Call, Pulse and others. Where is streaming’s version of the Law & Order or Chicago universes? Can the medium ever support sitcoms that run for 100-plus episodes? HBO chief Casey Bloys has previously discussed the importance of building on The Pitt’s model. The show delivers 15 episodes per season, compared with the eight to 10 common among streaming dramas.

Rather than sink inordinate amounts of money chasing the next Stranger Things, leverage TBS & TNT as a development pipeline for habitual comfort shows like these.

The limits of reinvention

Is all of this easier said than done? Undoubtedly. There’s risk in relying on fickle social platforms you don’t own outright, and corporate cultures don’t transform at the snap of a finger. Did I provide some blinding glimpses of the obvious? Absolutely. Cable bad, digital popular ain’t exactly rocket science. Will this replace the lucrative financials of cable’s heyday? No shot.

But these strategies are rooted in commercial logic. Comedy Central can still discover new comedians, MTV can still calculate what’s cool, BET can still amplify Black culture, and TNT/TBS can still develop popular programming. None of it requires the archaic cable TV industry at its center. The value unlock comes from applying these capabilities to new forms.

It’s not about rescuing the past. It’s about using what worked to build for the future.

It’s hard to keep track of everything that was embarrassing about the Obama years, even if you never dated a girl who was really into Tumblr. There was so much breathless optimism about everything, even around relatively mundane technology like 3D printing. Some people thought that it could forever change sculpture, or perhaps even disrupt standard means of manufacturing. So far its most innovative user has been Luigi Mangione.

“Solid Pictures: Photosculpture and the Making of Modern Likeness,” a new exhibition at Stanford’s Cantor Arts Center, shows that the technology may never have been that revolutionary in concept. The show marks the first major exhibition devoted to the process of François Willème (1830-1905), who unveiled it to Paris in 1861. For his photosculptures, the subject stood on a platform beneath a glass-domed rotunda 10 meters across while 24 cameras hidden in the walls fired at once, cued by a whistle. Assistants projected the negatives onto frosted glass, traced the silhouettes and ran each profile through a pantograph that cut into a block of clay on a turntable. Ten seconds of posing yielded a bust in 48 hours—in plaster, bisque porcelain, terracotta or bronze. The firm that produced this invention went through two corporate reorganizations, and Willème was pushed out by his own shareholders in May 1867. Curator Patrick R. Crowley has gathered 26 surviving photosculptures (12 belong to the Cantor) alongside patents, prints, glass negatives and a handful of works by Oliver Laric, Aria Dean, Charles Ray and others to argue that this failure is the grandfather of the CT scan, the 3D printer and those contemporary artists. For Silicon Valley, it’s a cautionary tale about an overleveraged startup.

Mechanically produced bust (c. 1860) is a somewhat horrifying proof of concept that reminds me of the work of Medardo Rosso (1858-1928). This representation of a woman’s head is assembled from 100 wedge-shaped sections of wood bound together with twine. Léon Foucault, of the pendulum, said it demonstrated the problems with the technology that would later cause the company to collapse, but removed from context, it’s a fascinating artifact, demonstrating how easy it is for the mind to conjure a face almost out of everything, and then give it emotions—in this case sad ones.

Photosculpture of Matilde de Aguilera y Gamboa (c. 1865) in bisque porcelain shows what the technology could do for someone with a bit more elegance. The sitter was a Spanish aristocrat, sister of the Marqués de Cerralbo. Isabella II summoned Willème to Madrid to make dozens of photosculptures of the royal family, which drew the notice of local aristocrats and earned him a knighthood in the Order of Charles III. Silicon Valley could learn much from this, too, because despite all the technical claims the most memorable parts of the work—the folded taffeta, the crumpled gloves, the complicated hair—were all carved by hand.

NIC Global is closing its 100,000-square-foot manufacturing site in Woodinville, Wash., as demand shifts to Eastern Washington. (NIC Photo)

Metal fabricator NIC Global plans to close its Woodinville, Wash., plant, eliminating 103 jobs. Declining business from local customers is prompting the company to shift production primarily to Eastern Washington to meet data center-related growth.

The layoffs are scheduled to begin Jan. 18, 2027, with final departures Aug. 31, 2028, according to a notice filed with the Washington Employment Security Department. Affected jobs include assembly, engineering, quality assurance, metal fabrication specialists, and forklift positions.

The building will be returned to its landlord when the lease ends in September 2028.

“This is a sad day for our company. Woodinville is where NIC was formed — three Seattle-area sheet-metal shops brought together under one roof in 2001 — and the people in that building built the foundation of everything NIC is today,” Troy Wood, company president and CEO, said via email.

The 100,000-square-foot site mostly served energy and medical device customers. Wood said the volume of work at the plant has declined while demand has increased in Eastern Washington for customers building power distribution technology needed by data centers.

“NIC is a supplier of sheet-metal fabrications and assemblies, and in our business, being close to our customers is what keeps us competitive,” Wood said.

While most of the work from Woodinville will go to Spokane, and a smaller portion will go to NIC’s Gallatin, Tenn., facility. The company also has manufacturing in Pullman; Chillicothe, Ohio; and Denton, Texas. The work outside of Washington is focused on the heavy truck sector. The company’s headcount is nearly 600.

Laid-off workers will be encouraged to apply for openings at other plants.

There are smaller fabricators remaining in the Seattle area, including JEMCO Components & Fabrications, which provides precision machining and sheet-metal fabrication for customers in medical, aerospace and defense sectors, and QUAL-FAB serving medical and dental customers.

NIC’s legal and financial headquarters will stay in the Woodinville area, where the company plans a new office for its finance, IT, sales and West Coast support teams.

Wood praised the quality and reliability of the Woodinville employees’ work.

“I am grateful to every one of them,” he said, “and especially to those who chose to stay with us through two and a half years of uncertainty and kept doing their jobs with integrity.”

Elvira Dyangani Ose Wants Zeitz MOCAA to Belong to Its City

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At the start of this month, Elvira Dyangani Ose assumed the title of artistic director at the Zeitz Museum of Contemporary Art Africa in Cape Town. The institution is the largest museum on the continent devoted to contemporary art from Africa and its diaspora, but has been in the public eye lately for another reason. Koyo Kouoh, its executive director and chief curator since 2019, died suddenly of cancer in May 2025 at the age of 57. The loss was felt across the art world because at the time Kouoh was also serving as curator of the 2026 Venice Biennale.

Kouoh had put her stamp on Zeitz MOCAA, which opened in Sept. 2017 in a grain silo complex on the V&A Waterfront redesigned by Thomas Heatherwick of Hudson Yards infamy. The founding collection came from Jochen Zeitz, the former Puma chief executive, who co-chairs the board with V&A Waterfront CEO David Green.

Dyangani Ose comes to Cape Town from the Museu d’Art Contemporani de Barcelona, where she served as director from 2021 until this year. Born in Córdoba to a family of Equatorial Guinean descent, she has held curatorial roles at Tate Modern, where she helped develop the museum’s first strategy for collecting art from Africa and its diaspora; Creative Time in New York; the Centro Andaluz de Arte Contemporáneo in Seville; and the Centro Atlántico de Arte Moderno in Las Palmas. She directed the Showroom in London, curated the 2015 Göteborg International Biennial and the 2013 Lubumbashi Biennial. She was one of the curators of “Project a Black Planet: The Art and Culture of Panafrica” at the Art Institute of Chicago.

We caught up with Dyangani Ose to talk about Kouoh’s legacy, what she learned in Barcelona and why she thinks of Cape Town as an argument rather than a city.

Koyo Kouoh is credited with bringing a new vision to Zeitz MOCAA and built programs like a residency for Cape Town artists. Which parts of her program do you view to be essential to preserving, for the museum and her legacy?

Thank you. Koyo did something that sounds simple and is, in fact, extraordinarily difficult: she began to anchor the museum in the reality of Cape Town. She used to say that when she arrived, the institution had not yet landed in the city, and she devoted six years to changing that. The Koyo Kouoh Atelier is perhaps the clearest expression of it: an artist residency inside the galleries, where artists who live and work in Cape Town experiment in front of the public, and where visitors witness art in the making rather than only its results. I consider that essential. So is everything that surrounds it: the BMW Centre for Art Education and its soon-to-be-launched MOCAA On the Move, a purpose-built mobile art classroom; the Pan-African Zeitz MOCAA & University of the Western Cape (UWC) Museum Fellowship Programme; the symposia; the long-term research behind projects such as “When We See Us: A Century of Black Figuration in Painting”; and her insistence on monographic depth, on giving artists like Tracey Rose, whom I had the privilege of working with in Gran Canaria 20 years ago, the retrospectives they deserved.

The programs tying the museum to the local reality of Cape Town are the ones I hope to give wings to. My way of working is both hyper-local and connected to an intercontinental reality: through shared methodologies, and through the formulation of common imaginaries. A legacy is not something one preserves under glass. Koyo understood building institutions on the continent as a matter of urgency, and to honor her is to keep that urgency alive. For me, an institution’s legacy is measured not in individual exhibitions or annual reports, but in the artists it sustains, the publics it forms and the next generation of practitioners and professionals it cultivates.

That is also why my first months are, above all, a time for listening. An institution needs time to be understood, and I want to learn from the people who sustain it every day, from its critics and, just as much, from those who feel it has nothing to say to them. They may not hold the answers, but looking for those answers with them, assuming nothing, is what anchors the work in its place.

Kouoh appointed you to her jury for the Venice Biennale. What was it like to work with her? Did you talk about the museum?

Although I learned about the jury appointment long after her passing, Koyo and I shared almost 20 years of conversations, and her invitation was, in many ways, a continuation of that dialogue. The last time we spoke in person was in Chicago, where she was one of our speakers at the Art Institute for a symposium linked to “Project a Black Planet: The Art and Culture of Panafrica.” We spoke, as we often did, about the people and the infrastructures that have shaped our field: about Bisi Silva and what ÀSÌKÒ and the Centre for Contemporary Art, Lagos, meant for so many of us, about N’Goné Fall, about a new generation of colleagues such as Marie-Hélène Pereira and about the opportunities for capacity building on the continent.

And of course we spoke about Zeitz MOCAA, which I knew and visited from its inception, although I did not attend while she led it, but which was very present in our exchanges. Koyo was building something there that went beyond exhibitions: a way of making the institution belong to its city. I don’t take it as an inheritance to administer, but as a conversation to continue and fortify from the perspectives inherent to my practice.

At MACBA you were the first woman and first person of color to lead the museum since its founding in 1995. What are your proudest accomplishments from those years?

We restored the museum’s connection with the city. MACBA had, at times, been perceived as an ivory tower, a building that looked outward while turning its back on its neighborhood. We worked to make it porous, open to the Raval and to the city, to the communities who perhaps never dare to visit it and who, in the present, make it, produce it. Fostering that community also meant rethinking how it addresses its publics, establishing the museum’s first-ever development department and strategy, and recognizing philanthropic collaboration as a tool to transform a 30-year-old institution beyond its current governance. What I sought after was not only visibility, but rootedness: the slow, difficult work of making an institution genuinely accountable to the communities it claims to serve. Insisting on better communication with our publics, especially younger ones. In Barcelona we never managed to create a Youth Council.

Furthermore, we presented a program of international interest and relevance under economic conditions that were insufficient for what was asked of the museum; not only to return to pre-pandemic audience levels, but importantly, to care for its own history, while offering a critical and generous gaze toward an international audience that experiences a Barcelona—a culture much more diverse and contemporary—in real life, which was not reflected in its programs. Projects such as “Song for Many Movements: Scenes of Collective Creation” and “Project a Black Planet: The Art and Culture of Panafrica” showed the range of what the institution could do. And, perhaps more importantly, we held on despite the difficulties, without needing to underline those limitations at every step.

I believe the work directors do to sustain an institution’s infrastructure and team morale, support their material conditions and accompany them as their practices become more specialized—what Charles Esche once called the “back office”—is fundamental, and we should talk about it more. There was still much to do overall; at the CIMAM, for instance, we hope to contribute to a better understanding of this role. We advocate for public museums like MACBA to have independent, more professional governance, giving their directors more autonomy to operate. However committed a director may be, we work like software on hardware that does not always yield, conceptually or materially; programming alone does not change an institution. All of this generates a better working culture, and a form of capacity building that starts in the museum and, progressively, seeks to influence the sector as a whole. That is part of what I bring with me to Zeitz MOCAA.

You were born in Córdoba to a family of Equatorial Guinean descent. Most of your career has been in Europe and New York. How does that unique vantage point shape how you’ll lead a museum on the continent?

I would gently correct the premise. Much of my work and research has taken place on the continent or in dialogue with it, particularly during my time at Tate Modern, where I helped create Tate’s first Africa Strategy for the acquisition of modern and contemporary art of Africa and its diaspora, and led the trans-local program, “Across the Board,” which connected Tate’s audiences with artists, colleagues and institutions from all over the continent with people like the late beloved Bisi Silva, Ntone Edjabe, Senam Okudzeto and others with whom I devised a series of public programs and research projects in Accra, Douala and Lagos. Before and after that period, I have collaborated with Marilyn Douala-Bell and the Salon Urbain de Douala; I curated the Rencontres Picha in Lubumbashi with artist Sammy Baloji and many others, and conducted my doctoral research on artists’ collectives in Dakar, Lagos and Cape Town, a city I first came to research in 2005.

Still, the vantage point matters. As a Black scholar and curator, a post-colonial subject and an African born and raised in Spain, I became aware early of the power implicit in binary narratives, us and them, here and there, and of the institutions that consolidate that power. In the work of artists, writers and thinkers from Africa and its diaspora, I found poetic and political responses to those concerns, and a notion of togetherness that subverts that rhetoric. That is how I understand Pan-Africanism: not as an origin one returns to, but as a living practice, made of networks, solidarities and translations, including those of Spanish- and Portuguese-speaking Africa, which the art world so often forgets. As “Project a Black Planet” set out to show, Pan-Africanism has never been solely a political, literary or philosophical project; it has also been a proposal of artistic imagination, a century-long practice of solidarity across geographies and temporalities.

I find myself increasingly interested in conversations among contexts and communities of the Global South that no longer route through Europe, at least not in the way they did in the late 20th Century, and in local histories that, studied closely, reveal their ties to many others worldwide. I do not come to lead from outside. I come to listen to a city and a community of artists, South Africans, Panafricans and Panhumanists, who already know what they need, and to place the institution at their service.

At Tate Modern you helped develop the institution’s strategy for collecting art from Africa and its diaspora. How should other museums orient themselves toward this material?

Working with Tate’s Africa Acquisitions Committee taught me that collecting is not mere acquisition; it is a commitment to the context and aesthetic environments from which those artworks come, to their histories, and to their agents. Collecting demands research, long-term relationships with artists and their communities, and the humility to recognize that many of these histories were written by or in artists’ collectives, journals, workshops and ephemeral platforms, not only by the objects themselves.

Too often, museums treat this material as an emergency to manage: a temporary exhibition here, a public program there, a more diverse surface over a structure that stays the same. An intervention that does not touch the structure remains, in the end, just programming. Kerry James Marshall is a key example for me: he realized that placing Black figures within the conventions of history painting was not enough; he had to master that tradition to contest it and change it from within. Museums face the same task. Collecting is structural work, and it begins when those who govern our institutions acknowledge the violence through which some collections were assembled, and the erasures their narratives have sustained.

From there, I would suggest three orientations: sustained commitments to artists across their careers, not single trophies—so to speak (a task that is clearly not the museum’s alone); “collect contexts” as much as works: archives, documents, journals, the infrastructures that made art possible; and to do it in partnership with institutions—no matter their sizes—on the continent, sharing research, loans and authorship, so that knowledge does not travel in one direction only. Zeitz MOCAA, as an institution, its programs and its collection carry that responsibility. It should be a resource for the continent and the world.

You wrote to the New York Times that the museum “is and must continue to be a public institution,” and called Cape Town “a city of contradictions.” Which contradictions do you mean? How do you plan to explore them?

Cape Town is not merely a location; it is an argument, and its contradictions—historical, spatial and political—are themselves a curatorial invitation. Cape Town is a city in which social change has always been written into urban space. The contradictions I mean are those between a city of extraordinary openness and creativity and a city whose geography still tells you who belongs where; between the promise of the public and its fine print. Years ago, while writing with Gabi Ngcobo about Gugulective, we borrowed a phrase that I believe captures the city well: “everyone is welcome, terms and conditions may apply.”

I have been thinking about Cape Town for a long time, and what has always moved me is how its artists and communities have answered those contradictions: the District Six Museum, built from the memories of those who were removed from the city center; the tradition of the cultural workers during the Resistance Art movement; a collective like Gugulective, working from a shebeen in Gugulethu; the implementation of Chimurenga Library and programs in various moments of its trajectory; and the transformation of the artistic context, the proliferation of galleries, auction houses, cultural foundations and scholarly programs to deepen the knowledge of professionals in the field. In my research, the most enduring cultural interventions were never those that announced themselves as monuments; they worked subtly within the fabric of everyday life. Collectivity, in that sense, is not an outcome of cultural work. It is its method.

That is what I mean by a public institution. A museum is public not because of who funds it, but because it assumes responsibility for the program and the institutional space it generates, and for the communities it serves. How an institution is structured is already a curatorial statement, and a public museum must build a vision robust enough to attract the support it deserves. Zeitz MOCAA is a not-for-profit initiative that seeks to be, above all, a public, Pan-Africanist and intergenerational space. That means taking the museum beyond the Waterfront, into the city, but also making sure that communities who do not yet feel addressed by the museum come to feel it as their own, sharing not only the right to change it but also the responsibility for doing so. A museum transforms collectively, from within and from without. The question is not only who visits the museum, but who is able to inhabit it.

Next year marks the 10th anniversary of Zeitz MOCAA. Have you thought about how the institution should mark the event? 

An anniversary is a good moment for an institution to ask itself questions in public. Nine years ago, Zeitz MOCAA opened with an enormous promise. Since then it has lived through a crisis, a profound transformation under Koyo, a pandemic and her loss.

It is still early days—I haven’t had time to talk to the team on the ground as much. However, I keep returning to the idea of belonging, to who the museum belongs to. I think of the museum as an open work, in Umberto Eco’s sense: something evolving and unfinished, which its publics are invited to complete. So I imagine expanding the museum’s own archive to artists, scholars and its publics, and inviting the communities of Cape Town to tell us what the next 10 years should be, in the spirit of a Casa de la Palabra [a house of words], a place where a community gathers in processes of decision-making. I am also interested in convening institutions across the continent, independent spaces, biennials and museums, to think together about how we build and sustain cultural infrastructure, around a question Zeitz MOCAA is singularly placed to ask: what does the continent need from its cultural institutions, and what must those institutions dare to become?

In 2006, at the Centro Atlántico de Arte Moderno, you co-curated a show of 14 emerging South African artists. What has changed most in South Africa’s art scene since that time?

“Olvida quién soy / Erase Me From Who I Am,” which I curated 20 years ago together with Gabi Ngcobo, Tracy Murinik and Khwezi Gule, brought together artists who were then at the beginning of their careers, Nicholas Hlobo, Zanele Muholi, Moshekwa Langa and Tracey Rose among them, and who have since become central to the history of contemporary art. That trajectory is in itself the clearest sign of change: South African artists are no longer guests in the international conversation; they shape it. And so do my co-curators, particularly Gabi Ngcobo, whose work has transformed the field from within.

The infrastructure has changed too: art fairs, galleries, foundations and a museum like this one. But growth brings its own questions. Much of that infrastructure is private and market-driven. At the same time, public institutions have weakened, and many young artists still work without studios, without critical platforms, without the possibility of making a living from their work. What hasn’t changed is the vitality of collectivity: artists organizing themselves and creating their own platforms when institutions fail them. I have spent much of my life learning from those experiences, and from artists who showed me that real transformation begins only when we recognize ourselves as part of something larger. A museum’s responsibility is to learn from them too.
