Received more than one ballot? This could be the reason

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A 10-year resident of Colorado Springs who votes in every election, Theresa Martinez was expecting her El Paso County ballot in the mail. She was not expecting the arrival of another ballot from Jefferson County.

“I thought it was kind of peculiar,” she said.

The extra ballot had just one question on it: a ballot measure on whether to increase taxes for the West Metro Fire District, which serves Lakewood and surrounding communities. Martinez is not alone in her confusion about special district elections, says Jefferson County Clerk and Recorder Amanda Gonzalez.

“We’ve been getting a lot of calls about this,” she said.

Special districts are government bodies that provide services like firefighting, sanitation and utilities. They often collect revenue for operations through taxes, which have to be approved by voters. Unlike a regular election ballot, a special district’s ballot can have different criteria for voter eligibility. A Colorado voter who owns taxable property in the district but does not live there can often be eligible to vote.

The list of voters who will receive a special district’s ballot is created by the special district itself, said Gonzalez, though a county clerk and recorder’s office generally handles the work of distributing and counting them. That means it’s the special district’s job to decide who gets a property owner ballot, not the state’s.

Martinez says she’s not aware of any property she owns in Jefferson County that would make her eligible for a West Metro Fire District ballot. Gonzalez said recourse would be to the special district, not election officials.

“Ultimately, the special districts themselves are the ones that determine eligibility for their districts and create that voter list,” she said.

Issues have come up this year when it comes to other special district elections, most notably when 800,000 voters erroneously received a ballot with a question about a tax increase for the creation of a Front Range passenger rail system. The error came from miscommunication about the updated boundaries of the rail district, which includes 13 Colorado counties.

The Secretary of State’s Office has sent guidance to the affected counties on how to tabulate votes to distinguish eligible voters. The protocol will likely include some hand-counting, according to previous Gazette reporting.

In Pueblo County, officials have provided information in response to questions from the public about receiving extra ballots. Due to “lack of accuracy” in special districts vetting registered voter lists against property records, some people who are special district residents also received property owner ballots.

“The entities who submitted content to be included on the November ballot are contractually responsible for accurately vetting and certifying registered voter lists before submitting them to the Election Department,” the statement read.

While potentially “confusing or concerning,” receiving multiple ballots is not necessarily a sign of a mistake, the county’s statement also read. Voters with questions are encouraged to reach out to the Pueblo County Election Department.

“The elections department has a process to verify that people and property owners only get one vote in any of these areas,” said Daniel Lepick, Pueblo County elections supervisor, in a Facebook video explaining the situation.

For people who receive a property owner ballot that they intend to cast, Gonzalez said they can use a secure drop box in any Colorado county just like they would for a regular ballot. The day after Election Day, metro Front Range election officials do a ballot swap to match ballots with their corresponding counties. Ballots received for other parts of the state are also transferred where they need to go.

“It makes sense that people are asking questions and wanting to make sure they have accurate information,” Gonzalez said.

Airman’s Attic on Peterson offers free home goods, uniforms

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

A little retail therapy on a stressful day can go a long way, particularly if you feel like you walked away with a good deal.

The Airman’s Attic on Peterson Space Force Base gives junior military members and their families that hit of dopamine for free.

At the little shop, service members with ranks of E-5 and below and their families can pick up five items each week at no cost. The shop is open to all junior service members in the region, including sergeants and below in the Army and Space Force and staff sergeants and below in the Air Force.

Some of the goods fall into the critical category, like baby wipes, while others brighten up your home or your day, such as a lamp or a new picture book for your child. 

When young troops come through the door, they may also get encouraging words from Rackel Haskell and Cherlyn Griggs, the longtime volunteer co-managers. Both military spouses, the two have spent decades running the shop and know it can be tough for young airmen fresh out of basic training living away from home for the first time. 

“I actually give them hugs,” said Haskell, who drives in from Elbert to run the shop. 

Rackel Haskell is one of two co-managers at the Airman’s Attic on Peterson Space Force Base. (Mary Shinn, The Gazette)

Her co-manager, Griggs, is more likely to offer a bit of cooking advice if young troops are walking out with an item from the grab-and-go food section, in addition to a little nudge toward maturity. 

“I am the one that says, ‘Put your big boy pants on and buck up,’” she said with a laugh. 

The two started Peterson’s first food pantry in the Attic with their own money. It’s an endeavor that the Peterson, Schriever, Cheyenne Mountain Spouses’ Club now runs in a larger space. The Attic maintains a small but popular grab-and-go section.

Other items that line the shelves, such as cups, bowls, Christmas decorations, toys, children’s clothes and other items, come from donations. When service members move, particularly during the summer, donations flow in at such high numbers that the boxes can be lined up outside the building. 

“During the summer, it’s crazy,” Griggs said.  

Service members also pass along their gently used uniform items, such as fatigues, dress blues and boots. The items fill a whole room and can help offset the high cost of buying a whole uniform. A new dress jacket on the Army and Air Force Exchange Services website currently sells for $192. 

A room at Airman’s Attic is dedicated to uniform items that service members can pick up for free. (Mary Shinn, The Gazette)

The shop’s rank limit does not apply to the uniform items and its popular aspect of the Attic, Haskell explained. 

On a single day in October, the uniform section saw the most junior troops up through the equivalent of an Air Force colonel come through, she said. 

Service members can pick up as many items as they need from this room, and it will only count as one item on their limit of five, she explained. 

Running the shop relies on the Attic’s team of 17 volunteers who sort and clean the donations, among many other tasks.

If they receive items they cannot put on their own shelves, they will give them to those who can use them. For example, blankets that come in dirty go on to provide comfort to animals at the Humane Society of the Pikes Peak region, Griggs said. 

After decades of managing the shop, burnout hasn’t caught up to the cheerful managers.

All the volunteers enjoy each other’s company and will spend time together at potlucks and other events, they explained. 

“We love it,” Griggs said.

The Airmen’s Attic on Peterson is open Monday through Wednesday, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m. at 1525 Selfridge St.

GUEST OPINION: State’s income tax debate must include business growth

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Economic development talk in southern Colorado usually centers on landing the next big employer. That work matters. But some of our best opportunities are already here: the local contractor ready to add a crew, the manufacturer pricing out new equipment, the owner thinking about opening a second location. 

None of those are easy calls. Each one takes capital, confidence and a willingness to take a risk. 

Amendment 87, the proposed graduated income tax, would change the math behind those decisions and voters should understand how. 

The measure would replace Colorado’s 4.4% flat income tax with a graduated system. Income under $100,000 would be taxed at lower marginal rates. Taxable income above $500,000 would face marginal rates of 7.4% to 8.4%. 

Supporters point out that about 97% of taxpayers would pay less, and maybe they are right. Plenty of small-business owners would come out ahead. 

Still, a small business isn’t always a small-income taxpayer. 

Many locally owned companies are S corporations, partnerships or other pass-through businesses. They don’t pay corporate income tax. Their profits flow through to the owners’ personal returns, and in some cases owners owe tax on profits that never leave the business. A strong year on paper doesn’t always mean cash on hand to expand. 

Take an owner with $1 million in Colorado taxable income. Under Amendment 87, that owner would pay roughly $15,925 more in state income tax each year than under today’s flat rate. If that owner is deciding whether to hire another employee, buy equipment or move into a bigger building, $15,925 is part of the calculation. 

We’re not claiming every extra tax dollar would have become a new job, or that every affected business would pull back. But higher taxes change the return on expansion, and expansion decisions are how communities create opportunity. 

This hits growing companies hardest. A business might spend years building customers, adding staff and improving its margins. As its taxable income rises, more of it falls into the higher brackets. 

The brackets also aren’t indexed to inflation. Over time, rising prices and wages would push more taxpayers into higher brackets even if their real purchasing power hasn’t changed. For a permanent change to the state tax code, that deserves a hard look. 

We also understand what supporters are trying to do. Good schools, accessible healthcare and affordable childcare make an economy competitive. Employers need educated workers, healthy communities and parents who can afford to stay in the workforce. Amendment 87 would dedicate its new revenue to those priorities, and those benefits should be weighed alongside the costs. 

But the debate also has to account for the employers whose investments create the jobs and tax base that pay for those services. 

Southern Colorado competes with regions across the country for investment, talent and expansion. Taxes aren’t the only factor. Workforce, infrastructure, housing and quality of life count just as much. Still, tax policy is on the list every business reviews before deciding where its next dollar goes. 

For our region, the real question goes beyond what any one taxpayer saves or pays next year. Will Colorado remain a place where locally owned companies can grow, compete and hire? 

Voters deserve a clear picture of both the proposed investments in public services and what they could mean for the employers who keep our communities running. Colorado’s economic future depends on landing the next major employer and also on making sure today’s small businesses have room to become tomorrow’s major employers. 

That needs to be part of the conversation. Vote no on amendment 87! 

Johnna Reeder Kleymeyer is the president & CEO of the Colorado Springs Chamber & EDC.

Perspective: How will Weiser govern?

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

Past performance is a great predictor of future performance. Attorney General Phil Weiser — poised to become Colorado’s next governor — agrees. Weiser has stated his eight years in office give Coloradans “clear guidance and a clear vision” about how he would perform as governor.  

The attorney general has a significant podium from which to advocate for or against laws and policies that impact public safety, as well as to use the office’s massive resources to lead litigation at the state and federal level. Yet, Weiser had scant relevant legal and leadership experience when he was sworn in, resulting in numerous misjudgments and failures.  

Let’s take a look at his record as AG on some high-profile issues to see what it may tell us about how he will govern. 

Budget 

As Colorado’s attorney general, he has excelled at spending taxpayer money and growing government. Since 2018, the year he was elected, Colorado’s population has grown 5.5%. Inflation has increased by more than 28%. Our state budget has ballooned by more than 50%. Weiser put all those metrics to shame. In his time as AG, Weiser doubled his office budget to $165 million and grew his staff by 50% — most of whom still work from home. 

Prediction: Weiser will seek to increase the growth of our Colorado budget beyond its current gluttonous rate. Weiser publicly opposes TABOR and seeks the kind of progressive tax code favored by east coast states, where he grew up. Watch for his support of a Democratic supermajority in the legislature to refer TABOR-repeal — er, reform — measures to our ballot. 

Crime rate 

Colorado is undeniably less safe after eight years of Weiser’s tenure as AG. The year before Weiser took office, the FBI ranked Colorado the 20th-worst state for violent crime. Last year, the year Weiser announced his candidacy for governor, our state had fallen to ninth worst. For property crime victimization, Colorado is third worst. Overall, according to U.S. News & World Report, Colorado is now the second-most dangerous state in America. 

Prediction: Colorado will continue to rank among the worst states in America for public safety as Weiser finds it politically impossible to veto the truckloads of offender-friendly bills sent to him by our DSA/progressive-dominated legislature. 

Fentanyl 

This month, Weiser told the Gazette, “I’ve spent the last nine years making sure I have gotten to be present and engaged and working to address Colorado’s challenges across our whole state.” Not when it comes to the fentanyl crisis still plaguing Colorado.  

After a 2018 campaign pledge to sue anyone who ever marketed opioids of any kind, Weiser went into political witness protection during the 2019 legislative effort to de-felonize enough fentanyl to kill 2,000 Coloradans. Fearing backlash from the drug-legalization-loving left, Weiser said nothing and did nothing as his party passed a bill that would help lead to a nearly 600% increase in fentanyl overdose deaths in Denver, Weiser’s home county. It is no surprise he opposes Proposition 132, the citizen-initiated ballot measure to ensure fentanyl dealers are sent to prison. Instead, Weiser chooses the status quo he helped to create, the one that saw Denver’s overdoses surge 34% from 2024 to 2025 while the rest of America saw a 19% decrease. 

Prediction: Colorado will continue to be one of America’s leaders in overdose deaths, as Weiser gives in to the failed “harm reduction” movement. 

Auto theft 

When Weiser was actually present and engaged in criminal justice matters, the results were equally troubling. The year before Weiser became AG, Colorado ranked sixth in the nation in motor-vehicle theft. By the end of his first term, when Weiser revealed his support for allowing thieves to commit “a third or fourth car theft in, say, three months” before jailing them, Colorado had climbed to first-worst in the nation — for the third year in a row. 

Prediction: Despite Colorado’s rejection of Weiser’s advice, strengthening (a bit) its weak car theft laws and empowering local law enforcement to pursue car thieves, Colorado will fail to break out of the 10-worst states for car theft under Gov. Weiser. 

Guns 

Weiser has an extremely limited view of the Second Amendment — at least for law-abiding citizens. He has never opposed any legislation that would further restrict Coloradans’ right to bear arms. Weiser described the U.S. Supreme Court’s recent decision striking down a Hawaii law that created a default “no carry” restriction on private property as “disappointing.” On his campaign website, Weiser pledges that he will “sign responsible gun safety legislation that will save lives.” 

It is an odd statement given that Weiser has championed access to firearms by — wait for it — convicted drug dealers and car thieves, despite their high statistical correlation with violence and gun crimes.  

The 2025 Colorado Auto Theft Annual Report states there “were 398 weapons charges and 231 cases filed specifically related to weapons offenses associated with motor vehicle theft.” Nonetheless, Weiser championed a change in the law that no longer makes it illegal for them to possess firearms in Colorado. 

Prediction: Weiser will sign every “guns are bad” piece of legislation the Democrat supermajority in the legislature puts on his desk. 

Leadership 

Weiser is most comfortable as a follower of his peers. His lack of meaningful experience prior to assuming the role of AG has led to numerous high-profile misjudgments. 

In fulfillment of his 2018 campaign pledge to sue anyone who ever sold an opioid, Weiser quickly jumped onto a series of global settlements with opioid distributors and manufacturers, shielding them from additional future liability. His “group think” settlement of claims against Johnson & Johnson resulted in just under $73 million to Colorado.  

Not everyone gave up so quickly. Washington’s Democrat AG Bob Ferguson rejected the global offer and filed his own lawsuit, resulting in $149.5 million — nearly double — paid to Washington, a state only 25% more populous than Colorado. 

In 2021, perhaps relying on his scant prior courtroom experience, he signed Colorado up for yet another bandwagon lawsuit, this time against Meta for various juvenile data privacy violations and youth social media addiction. A neighboring state, New Mexico, took a different approach. Democrat Attorney General Raúl Torrez rejected Weiser’s “go along to get along” approach and filed a standalone lawsuit against Meta. The gutsy former district attorney and federal prosecutor’s leadership and pursuit of civil justice for New Mexico resulted in a remarkably disparate outcome from Colorado’s. 

This past August, upon settling Colorado’s claims for a mere $615 million and providing Meta with blanket protection from additional lawsuits, Weiser proudly announced, “The relief we are getting in this settlement is very meaningful and well beyond what any court … is likely to order.” That claim did not age well.  

Last week, after a New Mexico jury found Meta liable for Torrez’s claims, the social media giant was ordered to pay New Mexico — a state roughly one-third the size of Colorado — $25 billion, an amount 4,000% more than Weiser obtained for us by following the crowd. 

Prediction: Having successfully rushed the fraternity of Democratic former AGs-turned-governors, Weiser will bond with Govs. Shapiro of Pennsylvania, Beshear of Kentucky, Ferguson of Washington and Stein of North Carolina and follow their lead on national issues. With no Polis to contend with, Weiser will look for manufactured opportunities to appear as the leader of a party increasingly controlled by socialists. He will continue to find comfort in “listening and collaborating,” while being led by the political current wherever it takes him — and us. 

Partisanship 

Weiser has shown himself to be the most hyperpartisan AG in our state’s history. Despite being attorney general for the entire four-year period of President Joe Biden’s one term in office, Weiser failed to file even a single lawsuit against him. Conversely, in less than two years of President Trump’s second term, Weiser has trumpeted his involvement in 88 lawsuits against him — nearly one per week. 

Weiser’s preference for following his fellow Democratic AGs from across the country is also reflected in his approach to suing the president. Of those 88 lawsuits he has filed, Weiser has led or coled only three. The remainder have been bandwagon “coalition” lawsuits filed by other Democratic AGs. 

Prediction: Weiser will continue to make attacking President Trump a primary focus of the first two years of his administration, especially as a distraction from or blame for, Colorado’s failure to address affordability, opportunity and public safety. Afterward, as he heads into reelection, he will blame Trump for the problems he has failed to fix. 

Civil rights 

When Weiser has chosen to exercise our state’s massive legal authority without the safety net of a crowd to follow, the results were equally troubling. At the Supreme Court of the United States, Colorado has become a punchline for the number of times it has been overturned for violating Coloradans’ constitutional rights.  

Weiser has unsuccessfully defended Colorado’s attacks on individual liberties (especially religious faith) in successive cases. In the cases 303 Creative, Counterman, Trump v. Anderson (a 9-0 drubbing) and Chiles (8-1, ouch), Weiser took positions that individual rights should yield to government. Even the high court’s liberal justices refused to buy his arguments. Heads up: On Nov. 3, the day Weiser is predicted to win the governor’s race, his office will again be in front of the court in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, seeking to force Catholic preschools to bend to the will of the almighty state on matters of faith. No other state has suffered as many Supreme Court smackdowns on First Amendment grounds since Weiser was elected AG. 

Prediction: Weiser will lose the St. Mary Catholic Parish case — but as governor, he will support Colorado government’s ongoing assaults on religious freedom and the First Amendment. If the newly elected AG who replaces Weiser will not do as Weiser did, expect Gov. Weiser to spend taxpayer dollars hiring high-priced, silk-stocking law firms to file suits on his behalf. 

Judiciary 

Weiser will also get to appoint Supreme Court justices and a generation of trial judges who will serve an average of 20 years, as well as the commissions that nominate justices and judges. 

Polis made the process to become a justice or judge deliberately and lopsidedly partisan. He reduced Republican representation on the Supreme Court Nominating Commission to three out of 17 members. For trial judges, the numbers are worse. Of the 161 possible judicial nominating commission members across Colorado, only 19 are Republican. Of the 35 commission members in the entire Denver metro area, only four are Republican. 

Prediction: Weiser will appoint more Republicans than Polis to the nominating commissions and point to it as a “Look, I’m moderate and balanced” moment. But he will not appoint enough to make meaningful balance in the selection of judges. Weiser will use identity politics as his touchstone for judicial appointments. Look for more judges to be appointed based more on their demographics than their potential judicial excellence, past performance or values consistent with the community in which they will serve. 

Clemency 

Weiser would also have unfettered clemency powers to reduce well-earned sentences being served by convicted felons and even to grant pardons and restore gun rights to convicted felons. 

Prediction: Weiser will not grant clemency based on a phone call to Kim Kardashian — like Jared Polis — which is now passé. But he will listen to the extreme-left flank of his party. Look for clemency that can be spun for political points with passing reference to “justice.” Weiser can and should be more transparent with his hand-picked clemency review board appointments and meetings. 

It appears inevitable we will have a Gov. Weiser and, likely, a Democrat-supermajority legislature that is out of control. There will be a lot of “listening and being present and collaborating,” but little leadership and tough decision-making. I fear Colorado’s toughest days are still ahead. 

George Brauchler is the 23rd Judicial District attorney and former district attorney for the 18th Judicial District. Colorado’s economic and business environments shift | Tatiana Bailey

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I’m not prone to exaggeration, so I thoughtfully state that I believe we are living through unusually transformational and precarious times. The pace of technological, geopolitical and economic change is unlike anything I’ve witnessed.

My husband recently sent a photo to the family group chat of a gasoline fill-up at $109. Gasoline and diesel prices have surged, with diesel nearly $6.40 a gallon. Virtually all goods are transported using diesel, so these prices touch almost everything we consume.

Prolonged conflict in the Middle East, tariffs and trade uncertainty further exacerbate inflation, probably keeping interest rates high. As of right now, the 30-year mortgage rate is roughly 7.5%, and experts forecast two more rate hikes from the Fed, which kept rates untouched at last week’s meeting.

Meanwhile, our increasingly K-shaped economy has provided overall resilience, with higher-income households spending while lower- and middle-income households struggle to maintain purchasing power. These lower-income groups are saving less and borrowing more.

Furthermore, artificial intelligence may represent the largest labor market transformation since industrialization.

In my view, the convergence of these forces creates an extraordinary level of economic uncertainty.

But Colorado faces additional headwinds.

Our high housing costs make attracting workers harder, and increasingly complex regulations raise concerns about business competitiveness.

Colorado Chamber of Commerce research found that 67% of surveyed businesses believe our state is headed in the wrong direction.

Colorado’s job growth has also slowed significantly. To be fair, the U.S. has a low-hire environment particularly affecting entry-level workers and AI-exposed occupations. The difference is that Colorado once outperformed the nation in population and employment growth, but that advantage has weakened considerably.

Hence, the central question is whether Colorado can remain competitive in a national milieu of extraordinary uncertainty and technological change.

Colorado Springs cultural office celebrates 20 years

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

You choose the city you want.

That’s what Angela Seals, executive director of the Cultural Office of the Pikes Peak Region, likes to tell people.

Artists in the region are doing well and making us proud, she says, but if we don’t support them, they might just move along or stop doing their craft.

“If you want to have something to do on a Saturday night, you’d better go out and buy a ticket,” she said. “We need to be taking risks with artists. You go to a Switchbacks game even if they might lose. You should have the same spirit of adventure when you go to Theatreworks and see a play you’ve never heard of.”

Jennifer DeDominici and Shabazz Green recently starred in TheatreWorks’ “Sweeney Todd: The Demon Barber of Fleet Street.” (Courtesy of Theatreworks)

This year marks the 20th anniversary of the nonprofit COPPR, the only regional arts agency in Colorado, not to be confused with creative district programs and organizations like the Palmer Lake Arts Council, Manitou Creative District or Pikes Peak Arts Council.

While these volunteer-led support groups and membership organizations help support the arts in their neighborhoods and communities, COPPR, which oversees El Paso and Teller counties, is the only entity with a professional staff and has offered programs for more than two decades.

COPPR’s mission is to support and promote the arts and help create the region’s identity and economy, Seals says. It operates as the voice of the arts in regional leadership and works to connect and grow the sector.

Every 10 years the nonprofit releases a cultural plan, including the most recent, Arts Vision 2030, which debuted in 2021, co-organized with the Bee Vradenburg Foundation, a nonprofit that advocates for and provides grants to local artists and arts and culture organizations.

“It’s pretty special that the Pikes Peak region has a dedicated nonprofit arts agency that is transcending a specific district and looking at the big picture and tying them together,” Seals said.

Two decades ago, the Springs was, at the time, the largest city in the U.S. without a professionally staffed arts agency. After about 60 members of the arts community gathered to create a cultural plan, they realized it would be better served by a more elite group that could dedicate time and energy to the undertaking.

Susan Edmondson and Kimberley Sherwood stepped up and created the COPPR board and nonprofit in 2006. They hired its first executive director, Bettina Swigger, and got to work on the first order of business — helping arts groups with marketing, resulting in PeakRadar.com with its centralized calendar.

The early years were spent building out the first cultural plan and Peak Radar and shouting out the arts scene. In 2014, COPPR founded the first local Arts Month, joining an October tradition started in 1993 by Americans for the Arts, a national nonprofit that works to promote the arts and humanities. Cities across the U.S. celebrate the arts and invite the community to attend and support arts and culture events and groups.

Public support makes up the largest part of COPPR’s revenue at 60%, which includes a Lodgers and Automobile Rental Tax (LART) contract with the city of Colorado Springs and other grants, including from Colorado Creative Industries.

Other revenue sources include in-kind support, foundation support and earned revenue. Individual contributions, program sponsorships, corporate contributions and investments and interest make up the rest.

Last year, COPPR purchased a permanent home at 287 E. Fountain Blvd. downtown. More than 2,000 square feet offers space for community programs.

“It was a really big step for our nonprofit. To say we own a piece of downtown for the creative community, and we did that without a capital campaign and with our own financial management,” Seals said. “We’re doing really well as a nonprofit and making sure these dollars go out into the community.”

FEATURED ENTERTAINER OF THE WEEK

Inde Navarrette

Actress

November 01, 2026

Inde Navarrette’s Quietly Compelling Rise, From Liberty High to Smallville

Inde Navarrette has built her career in emotionally unsettled places: a high school reckoning with violence, a family fractured by secrets and a romance shaped by grief and memory. She does not arrive on screen with the force of a celebrity demanding attention. Her more effective tactic is to make the person she is playing feel as though she had a life before the camera found her.

That quality made Navarrette a valuable late addition to 13 Reasons Why and, soon afterward, a central member of the younger ensemble on Superman & Lois. As Sarah Cushing, later Sarah Cortez, she had to exist beside capes, alternate worlds and city-threatening disasters without becoming incidental to them. Sarah’s depression, ambitions, mistakes and changing relationship with Jordan Kent supplied the series with consequences that could not be solved by superpowers.

Navarrette’s name was presented for this week’s feature alongside claims that she made a Halloween hosting debut on Saturday Night Live, starred in a project called Obsession and is preparing to play Rogue in the Marvel Cinematic Universe. Those claims should not be repeated as fact. They are not supported by established NBC, Saturday Night Live or Marvel casting records, and no reliable official announcement connects Navarrette to Rogue. Her documented career is more compact but also more revealing: a transition from short-form work to a provocative Netflix drama, a Sundance film opposite Sienna Miller and Diego Luna, and four seasons on an unusually family-centered superhero series.

The result is a performer worth watching for what she has actually accomplished. Navarrette entered two heavily scrutinized television worlds before she was old enough to have accumulated a long résumé, then found ways to make supporting characters register as people rather than plot devices. That is the real reason her career merits a closer look.

Early Life

Navarrette was born in Tucson to a family with Mexican and Australian roots: her father is Mexican, while her mother is Australian. That mixed heritage later gave particular resonance to her work on Superman & Lois, where the Cushing family’s Mexican American background and original Cortez surname became part of the story rather than an incidental character detail.

She attended Mar Vista High School in Imperial Beach, a coastal community in San Diego County. Unlike performers whose childhoods are documented through years of studio productions, Navarrette reached wider audiences near the end of her teens. Her early screen credits were modest, allowing her to learn the practical rhythms of production before joining two series with large, vocal audiences.

That timing matters. She was not introduced as the center of a franchise or promoted through a highly managed child-star narrative. Instead, she entered established ensembles and had to define characters through limited screen time. The skill became evident in 13 Reasons Why, where she arrived during the show’s final season carrying the history of a character who was no longer alive. It became even more important on Superman & Lois, where intimate family scenes regularly had to hold their own against the machinery of superhero television.

Career

Navarrette’s first widely seen turning point came in 2020, a year in which she appeared in both a globally discussed Netflix series and a Sundance-selected feature. The two projects asked for different kinds of restraint.

In the fourth and final season of 13 Reasons Why, she played Estela de la Cruz, the younger sister of Montgomery de la Cruz. Monty had committed brutal acts during the series and was subsequently killed off screen; he was also blamed for Bryce Walker’s murder as part of a cover-up devised by the principal characters. Estela therefore entered Liberty High carrying a name that meant something to nearly everyone around her.

The role could easily have functioned as a narrative instrument designed to expose the cover-up. Navarrette instead played Estela as someone trying to understand a brother whose cruelty she could neither erase nor fully reconcile with the person she had known. Estela’s connection with Tyler Down, one of Monty’s victims, made the material especially delicate. Their scenes placed grief, accountability and the limits of inherited guilt in the same room. Navarrette joined a series already surrounded by debate about its handling of trauma, but she brought a comparatively quiet perspective to its final reckoning.

The same year, she appeared as Ellie in Wander Darkly, writer-director Tara Miele’s nonlinear romantic drama starring Sienna Miller and Diego Luna. The film premiered at the Sundance Film Festival before receiving a wider release through Lionsgate. Its structure moves through memory, perception and uncertainty after a traumatic event, requiring the supporting cast to fit into a story that deliberately withholds a stable sense of time. For a young actor, it was valuable company: Miller and Luna are performers known for conveying complicated emotional histories without overexplaining them.

Navarrette’s defining television opportunity arrived with Superman & Lois. Her casting as Sarah Cushing was announced in 2020, before the series premiered on The CW in February 2021. The show began with a familiar comic-book proposition—Superman and Lois Lane returning to Smallville with their teenage sons—but distinguished itself by paying unusual attention to mortgages, marriages, mental health and the accumulated disappointments of adulthood.

Sarah was the daughter of Lana Lang and Kyle Cushing and the older sister of Sophie. She became a friend and romantic interest for Jordan Kent, the anxious son of Clark Kent and Lois Lane who was beginning to develop Kryptonian abilities. Yet Sarah was never simply the non-powered girlfriend waiting beside the superhero story. The pilot established that she had survived a suicide attempt and was receiving treatment for depression. Her frankness with Jordan created an immediate connection between two teenagers accustomed to feeling that something inside them was difficult to explain.

Navarrette’s performance helped keep that relationship from becoming an idealized high-school romance. Sarah could be compassionate and impatient, perceptive about Jordan’s pain and careless with his trust. She pursued music, questioned the future available to her in Smallville and reacted to the collapse of her parents’ marriage. Some of her decisions divided viewers, particularly when the series complicated her relationship with Jordan. Navarrette’s task was not to make every decision agreeable. It was to preserve Sarah’s right to be as inconsistent as the show’s male teenagers.

The Cushing household also gave Superman & Lois a second family through which to examine Smallville. Lana’s history with Clark connected the series to Superman mythology, but Kyle and Sarah represented residents whose lives were shaped by the town rather than by Krypton. As the series explored the family’s Mexican heritage, the Cortez name became significant. That development allowed Navarrette to play a young woman considering not only where she wanted to go, but what parts of her family history she wanted to carry with her.

Superman & Lois ran for four seasons, concluding in 2024 with a shortened final season. Its production demands shifted over time, and its last chapter operated with a reduced regular cast, but Sarah remained part of the emotional geography the series had constructed around Smallville. Navarrette had spent the transition from her late teens into her early twenties playing a character making a similar passage.

The series also placed her within an unusual corner of superhero entertainment. Superman & Lois arrived through The CW, home of the interconnected Arrowverse, but eventually established that its characters inhabited a separate continuity. That separation gave the writers room to build their own Kent and Lang families without remaining tied to every event in the network’s earlier programs. For Navarrette, it meant working in a franchise with extensive mythology while portraying a character whose most important battles were recognizably human.

Her career so far is not a long list of interchangeable credits. It is a small group of projects with demanding subject matter: suicide and recovery, sexual violence and its aftermath, family rupture, memory and adolescent identity. The next meaningful step will be determined less by the size of a franchise than by whether a role gives her the same opportunity to locate a private life inside a public story.

There has been a steep rise in the number of people labelled autistic over the last 20 years. Is that down to greater understanding – or overdiagnosis?

The home of 85-year-old Uta Frith does not exactly look like the latest front in the culture wars. Located in a serenely quiet corner of north-west London, it suggests a life still well lived, a set of deep interests and an idyllic kind of retirement. There are so many books that some are reachable only using a very tall ladder. The four hours I spend talking to her – and her husband Chris, an expert in schizophrenia – are an absolute pleasure. There is no sense of outrage or upset: even when I ask her about the stream of hostile and abusive emails she has recently received, she is relaxed and phlegmatic. “I tend to cope by just deleting them,” she says. “I don’t dwell on it.”

“It” is a reference to the controversy kicked up by her recently expressed views on a subject that sits at the heart of Frith’s academic career: what it is to be autistic, and who should or shouldn’t qualify for a diagnosis. Back in March, Dame Uta Frith – she was honoured with the title seven years ago – gave an interview to TES magazine (formerly the Times Educational Supplement) scattered with stark pronouncements. Among them was a claim that the so-called autistic spectrum has “gone on being more and more accommodating”, to the point that it “has come to its collapse”. Then, in August, she published a piece in the academic journal Psychological Medicine claiming autism was in dire danger of being turned from a “precise, clinical” term into a “vague, culturally amplified identity category”.

Gracie Abrams turned her Halloween visit to Saturday Night Live into a two-part tribute to Baz Luhrmann’s 1996 film Romeo + Juliet.

The two-time Grammy nominee used her pair of musical performances to portray both sides of the movie’s doomed romance, drawing inspiration from the looks worn by Claire Danes and Leonardo DiCaprio in the stylized adaptation of William Shakespeare’s tragedy.

Two Songs, Two Costumes

Abrams began with “Hit the Wall,” a track from her third studio album, Daughter from Hell. For that performance, she appeared in a version of the angel costume worn by Danes in the film.

The staging extended the cinematic reference beyond Abrams’ wardrobe. She started the song from behind an aquarium, adding another visual connection to the movie while performing for the live audience at Studio 8H.

When Abrams returned later in the episode, she switched characters as well as songs. She performed “Here You Come, There You Go” from the deluxe album while dressed in a tropical button-down shirt modeled on DiCaprio’s look, complete with the metal sleeves associated with his suit-of-armor costume.

Together, the appearances gave viewers a pair of coordinated Halloween transformations rather than a single costume carried across both musical numbers. The first performance centered on Danes’ Juliet, while the second moved to DiCaprio’s Romeo.

A Return to Studio 8H

The episode marked Abrams’ second stint as an SNL musical guest. She previously made her debut on the program in 2024.

The night also brought a separate milestone for host Inde Navarrette, who made her hosting debut on the show.

ShowBiz Take

Abrams’ appearance worked as more than a Halloween wardrobe showcase because each costume was assigned its own performance. By dividing the Romeo + Juliet homage between “Hit the Wall” and “Here You Come, There You Go,” she created a visual through line across her two musical slots while still giving each song a distinct presentation.

The film references were also specific and immediately tied to its central characters: Danes’ angel costume for the opening number, followed by DiCaprio’s tropical shirt and armor-inspired sleeves later in the episode. For Abrams’ second visit as musical guest, the coordinated concept made both performances part of the same Halloween-themed statement.

Inde Navarrette used her Saturday Night Live hosting debut to skewer Shane Gillis’ controversial gym comments, tease her upcoming Marvel role and turn her breakout performance in Obsession into a darkly comic opening monologue.

The Halloween episode also delivered a surprise return for Tom Hanks’ David S. Pumpkins during the cold open, while Gracie Abrams joined Navarrette as the night’s musical guest.

Navarrette Turns Her Breakout Role Into an ‘SNL’ Joke

Navarrette opened by addressing the role that brought her acclaim this summer. In Curry Barker’s horror hit Obsession, she plays Nikki Freeman, a woman who is cursed after Bear, played by Michael Johnston, wishes that she would love him more than anyone else in the world.

“You probably know me from the movie Obsession,” Navarrette told the audience. “People ask me if I’m anything like my character Nikki, and I’m like, No. No, no, don’t do that.”

She then insisted she was nothing like Nikki, pointing to her boyfriend, Nick Aiello, in the audience. The camera revealed him duct-taped to his seat as Navarrette declared that they had “such a chill relationship.”

The monologue soon shifted to Navarrette’s next major role: Rogue in Marvel Studios’ forthcoming X-Men reboot. She said she had been working out because of the physical expectations attached to appearing in a Marvel movie.

That setup led directly to the night’s pointed Shane Gillis joke.

“So every morning, I put on my little whore outfit, and I hit the gym,” Navarrette said.

The line referenced Gillis’ Oct. 10 SNL monologue, in which he said women now “dress like whores” at the gym before describing brightly colored shorts and complaining that men were not supposed to look. His remarks generated controversy following the episode.

Halloween Costumes Bring an ‘X-Men’ Audition

Navarrette invited cast members onstage to display their Halloween looks, joking that they could not go out that night. Marcello Hernandez arrived as “a sexy TSA agent,” while Kam Patterson portrayed Elizabeth Holmes.

Jeremy Culhane used the costume segment to pitch himself for Jake Schreier’s upcoming X-Men movie. Dressed as Wolverine, he told Navarrette that he had heard the character had not yet been cast.

“Oh, I’m so sorry. I can’t comment on that,” she replied.

Culhane answered that he could comment — and that he would love to appear in the movie with her.

Grace Reiter then appeared as the bird involved in the viral Kelly Clarkson and Art of Play controversy. Veronika Slowikowska completed the group costume dressed as the American Idol winner, demanding, “Sell me the damn bird!” Reiter’s bird shot back, “I am not for sale!”

Tom Hanks Brings Back David S. Pumpkins

The episode’s cold open began with Reiter and Culhane taking a spooky ride before Hanks made an unexpected appearance as David S. Pumpkins.

James Austin Johnson’s Trump interrupted the Halloween sketch to address the upcoming midterms and compare himself with Pumpkins. Johnson’s character said both had appeared in 2016 and described them as “very loud, very orange” and “very frankly annoying.”

Saidah Belo-Osagie joined him as Nicki Minaj, introduced as Trump’s “friend and future press secretary.” Showing support for the president, she joked, “Trump’s out here killing it — and by it, I mean my career.”

Slowikowska also appeared as Katie Zacharia, Trump’s brief, former White House press secretary. Her character failed to complete the show’s familiar opening declaration, instead beginning, “With confidence. Live from New York. And from so many cities,” before being cut off.

From ‘Obsession’ to Marvel

Navarrette’s hosting debut follows the extraordinary performance of Obsession. The low-budget movie has grossed more than $522 million worldwide and became Focus Features’ biggest film of all time.

The TIFF title also became the top-grossing festival acquisition and recorded the biggest second-weekend box-office increase in modern times outside the Christmas holiday.

Beyond playing Rogue in the Marvel Studios reboot, Navarrette is set to star in Apple Original Films’ science-fiction thriller The Waffle House Index. She will also executive produce that project.

Her episode arrived during SNL Season 52, which has also featured hosts Jalen Brunson, Dakota Johnson and Gillis.

ShowBiz Take

Navarrette’s debut smartly connected every part of her current moment: the unsettling premise of Obsession, the physical preparation for X-Men and the controversy left behind by the show’s previous host. Rather than merely acknowledging Gillis’ remarks, she repurposed them into a compact punchline on the same stage where they were delivered.

The episode also balanced that sharp callback with Halloween nostalgia through Hanks’ surprise appearance, while the Wolverine bit allowed SNL to play directly with the attention surrounding Navarrette’s confirmed role as Rogue.

What Happens Next?

Druski will host the Nov. 7 episode with The Strokes as musical guest. He will be the first social media influencer to host SNL.

John Mulaney will then return to host on Nov. 14, joined by musical guest Phoebe Bridgers.

Inde Navarrette put her upcoming Marvel role front and center during her first turn as host of Saturday Night Live, using her opening monologue to tease playing Rogue in X-Men—and to take a shot at Shane Gillis’ monologue from the previous episode.

Discussing the work required to get into “Marvel shape,” Navarrette delivered a callback to Gillis’ sexist opening remarks.

“So, every morning, I put on my little whore outfit and hit the gym,” she joked, drawing laughs from the studio audience.

The actress also addressed her “scream queen” debut as Nikki in Curry Barker’s Obsession. Navarrette insisted that she was “nothing like Nikki,” only for the monologue to reveal her boyfriend taped to his chair in the audience.

The opening brought several other cast members into the action. Marcello Hernandez modeled a sexy TSA-agent costume, while Kam Patterson appeared as Elizabeth Holmes. Jeremy Culhane dressed as Wolverine and pitched himself for the role in the upcoming Jake Schreier project referenced in the bit.

Veronika Slowikowska and Grace Reiter joined the parade as Kelly Clarkson and the mechanical singing bird involved in Clarkson’s online feud with a Brooklyn gift shop.

Ghosts, disastrous inventions and disruptive flyers

Navarrette’s first sketch paired her with Hernandez as the ghosts of two children from the 1930s aboard the Queen Mary. Their characters repeatedly distracted a tourist who was trying to learn where another ghost had hidden treasure.

Air travel provided the setup for another ensemble piece, a fake Delta commercial promoting “Delta Disruptive,” a special section reserved exclusively for unruly and irrational passengers.

Mikey Day played a traveler attempting to open an exit door while declaring, “This plane is made out of Israel.” Navarrette, meanwhile, impersonated the woman who became known on social media for insisting, “She is not real!”

She switched personas again for The Uninterested Woman Dating Show, playing Dane, one of the show’s so-called “fuckboy” contestants. Jane Wickline and Sarah Sherman appeared alongside her as fellow contestants.

One of the night’s darkest premises came in The Inventor. Navarrette played the widow of an inventor who returned home after his funeral to discover the creations he had left behind, including a robot called PD-44.

Six years later, she brought home a date played by Ben Marshall. The robot attacked him, ripping off his penis and blowing him up. Only then did Navarrette’s character understand that PD-44 stood for “Penis Destroyer”—and interpret the machine’s violent programming as proof of her late husband’s devotion.

“He did love me. My husband did love me after all,” she said while covered in blood.

Navarrette and Hernandez reunited for a Spanish soap-opera spoof built around an ill-fated product-placement assignment. Their characters were required to incorporate Sprite Cherry Lime Habanero into a dramatic scene, but the promotional effort quickly became a disaster for the brand.

The melodrama escalated until Hernandez’s character urged Navarrette’s to drink the soda in order to kill her unborn baby, which had been conceived by his brother.

ShowBiz Take

Navarrette’s hosting debut connected three different parts of her current screen profile in one appearance: the unsettling world of Obsession, the physical preparation for her forthcoming Rogue role and the anything-goes demands of live sketch comedy.

The Rogue reference was the night’s biggest forward-looking movie tease, but Navarrette kept it inside a joke rather than turning her monologue into a straightforward promotional stop. From there, the episode gave her sharply different assignments, including a 1930s ghost, a dating-show contestant, a soap star and a blood-covered widow discovering the brutal meaning behind a robot’s name.

The episode also marked another first: Gracie Abrams made her debut as Saturday Night Live’s musical guest on the same installment that introduced Navarrette as host.

Before Iain Armitage became the young Sheldon Cooper, his first television acting role placed him at the center of a very different kind of story. The actor made his debut as a kidnapping victim in a 2017 episode of NBC’s “Law & Order: SVU.”

Armitage was 8 when the Season 18 episode, titled “Chasing Theo,” premiered on January 11, 2017. He played Theo, a 6-year-old boy whose disappearance prompted an investigation by Lieutenant Olivia Benson, played by Mariska Hargitay, and the rest of the SVU squad.

The case begins after Theo’s mother, played by Rachelle Lefevre, leaves him alone in their apartment for the night while she attends a party. Investigators initially consider whether the child’s nanny, played by Adriana DeGirolami, may have taken him. The inquiry also raises a more complicated question: whether the nanny might have believed she was acting in Theo’s best interests because of his home life.

Because Theo is missing for much of the episode, Armitage does not appear throughout the entire story. His performance is instead concentrated in scenes near the beginning and conclusion of “Chasing Theo,” giving the future sitcom lead a dramatic introduction to television.

A Major Year Followed His “SVU” Debut

The procedural appearance opened an especially active 2017 for Armitage. Later that year, he joined HBO’s “Big Little Lies” as the son of Shailene Woodley’s character.

He also began playing young Sheldon Cooper in “Young Sheldon,” the “Big Bang Theory” spin-off that would make him widely associated with the precocious central character. The contrast between that role and his “SVU” debut is striking: before leading a prequel as its young brainiac protagonist, Armitage entered television through a dark missing-child storyline.

His work during 2017 extended beyond those three television shows. Armitage also appeared in the movies “The Glass Castle,” “Our Souls at Night” and “I’m Not Here.” Taken together, the projects made “Chasing Theo” the starting point of a year that quickly expanded his list of screen roles.

Armitage Had Already Visited the “SVU” Set

Although “Chasing Theo” marked Armitage’s acting debut and his first “Law & Order” role, it was not his first experience on the “SVU” set.

An undated throwback shared by Armitage’s official Facebook page showed him visiting the production before his guest appearance. During that earlier visit, he pretended to be a witness on the stand and spent time with Hargitay and Raúl Esparza, who played Rafael Barba.

Armitage’s connection to Esparza extends beyond the procedural. Armitage’s father, Scottish stage actor Euan Morton, appeared with Esparza in the 2003 Broadway production of “Taboo.” Esparza is also one of Armitage’s godfathers, and Armitage called him “Pumpkin” when he was a child.

“Chasing Theo” remains the only time Armitage and Esparza have acted together on screen. Both later participated in the 2020 virtual concert “Take Me to the World: A Sondheim 90th Birthday Celebration,” which Esparza hosted.

ShowBiz Take

Armitage’s “SVU” appearance matters as more than an early guest credit because it offers a sharp counterpoint to the role that followed. In the space of one year, he went from playing a child at the heart of a grim disappearance case to portraying Shailene Woodley’s on-screen son in “Big Little Lies” and taking the lead in “Young Sheldon.”

The episode also brought his professional debut into a setting he had already visited through his personal connection to Esparza. That combination gives “Chasing Theo” a distinctive place in Armitage’s early work: it was his first acting role, his only on-screen performance alongside one of his godfathers and the opening chapter of a packed 2017 that included television and film appearances.
